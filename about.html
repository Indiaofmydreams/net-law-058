What this site is

One purpose: help a law graduate reach Assistant Professor or JRF eligibility, free.

UGC NET is one three-hour session with two objective papers: Paper 1 (50 questions, 100 marks) and Paper 2, your subject (100 questions, 200 marks). Every question carries 2 marks, so Law alone decides two-thirds of the result.

The site is organised around that structure. Paper 1 holds 5,705 MCQs across 10 syllabus units. Paper 2 (Law 058) holds 3,580 MCQs across 10 units, and the full Law set passes 4,000 questions once the mock-test questions are counted. Around the banks sit timed mock tests, a 20-question quick test, a daily quiz, notes, previous-year questions, a Pomodoro timer, a personalised Exam Strategy Engine and a cut-off trend page. Every one of them is open the moment the page loads.

Each part exists because of a specific difficulty that aspirants report again and again. The next section names those difficulties plainly, and the sections after it show how each feature answers one of them.

The science behind it

Four findings from learning research that shape how the site is built.

Testing yourself beats re-reading

In controlled experiments, students who practised retrieving material remembered more a week later than students who spent the same time re-studying it, even though the re-readers felt more confident.12 A review of ten common study techniques rated practice testing and distributed practice as the two with the strongest evidence, and highlighting and re-reading as low-utility.3 This is why the site is built around questions, not around long text.

Spacing beats cramming

A large quantitative synthesis of published experiments found that spreading study over time improves long-term retention compared with massing it.5 The unit-wise structure, the daily goal and the multi-month plans let you revisit each unit several times rather than once.

Small, specific plans get done

Stating when and how you will act (“after dinner, one unit of 30 questions”) makes follow-through more likely than a vague intention.6 Habits, in turn, formed in a median of about 66 days in one real-world study, with large individual variation and missed days causing little harm.7

Feedback corrects over-confidence

People with weaker skills often misjudge their own performance.8 Mock analysis, the mistakes filter and cut-off data give you an external reference, so your plan rests on measured weakness rather than a feeling.

What this is not: a guarantee. Research describes averages across many learners. These findings explain why we built the site this way; your result still depends on the work you do.

The Pomodoro timer

Why a small timer sits at the bottom left of your screen.

The Pomodoro technique splits work into fixed rounds with short breaks. Our timer offers 25 minutes of focus with a 5-minute break, or 50 minutes with a 10-minute break, and a longer break after every four rounds.

Its role. It turns “study today” into a concrete, finishable unit, such as thirty questions from one unit, and it follows you across practice pages so you never leave to find a clock. It counts rounds completed today, giving you one honest measure of effort.

The evidence, stated carefully. Experiments show that attention to a single long task declines over time, and that brief interruptions can reduce that decline.4 That supports planned pauses in general. The specific 25/5 ratio comes from the technique's inventor9 and is a practical convention, not a proven optimum, which is why we offer a second preset and let you choose.

Where it is deliberately absent. Mock tests use their own exam clock, because a second timer would distract from the paper you are rehearsing. Read why in the FAQ.

Exam Strategy Engine

It answers how you should prepare, not only what to study.

Two aspirants with the same syllabus can need opposite plans: one has two months and weak Paper 1, the other has two weeks and strong Law. The Exam Strategy Engine exists because generic advice fits neither.

You answer 13 plain questions. The engine then combines them with what the site already knows on your device (practice progress, Daily Quiz scores, the 20-question Law test, mock results and the exam date) and runs ten analyses. Each result names the exact parts of the site to use next. It saves the strategy on your device and can recalculate after new practice, showing what changed.

Why it matters. Planning is where most preparation fails, because it is the step with the least feedback. By tying your plan to measured performance, the engine applies the same principle as spaced, targeted practice: spend scarce hours where your own data says marks are being lost.3 Like all guidance here, it does not predict or guarantee a result.

Cut-off trend analysis

Turning past cut-offs into a target you can plan against.

The question “what score do I need?” has no fixed answer, because NTA sets cut-offs for each session and category. What you can do is look at how they have actually moved.

Marks are out of 300 (Paper 1 plus Paper 2), so every 2 marks equals one correct answer. Figure 3 shows unreserved Law cut-offs for eight sessions, December 2022 to June 2026.

How the planning range is calculated

For each outcome, the page takes the latest five sessions that have a full set of figures, finds their average, and measures how much they typically vary (standard deviation). The expected range is the average plus or minus one standard deviation, rounded to an even mark. The safe target is the top of that range plus 6 marks, a cushion of three correct answers.

Applying that to the current data gives, for unreserved candidates, a planning range of 204 to 218 for JRF (average 211) and 178 to 192 for Assistant Professor (average 185), with safe targets of 224 and 198 marks. These are our calculation, not NTA figures.

What it is for, and what it is not

Its role is to set a measurable goal. Once you know that the target is about 99 correct answers out of 150 rather than “as many as possible”, your mock scores become comparable to something, and goal-based effort tends to rise as the goal comes into view.10 Five caveats apply: a few sessions are a small sample, cut-offs depend on the difficulty of that session's paper, some sources disagree (the page marks those cells with a dash), the range ignores category changes in the future, and it is never a promise. Design principles

The behavioural ideas behind the way the site feels to use, and the lines we do not cross.

Remove the first step

No account and no payment. People postpone tasks that start with friction, so the first question is one click away.

Make progress visible

The reading rail, rounds completed, daily goal and streak show effort that would otherwise be invisible. Seeing a goal draw closer increases effort toward it.10

Small commitments first

A 7-minute test, one Pomodoro round, one unit. Small, specific commitments are easier to keep and to repeat.6

Honest feedback

Scores, explanations and cut-off ranges show where you stand, including when that is uncomfortable.

What we do not do: no countdown pressure to buy anything, no hidden paywalls, no invented testimonials or pass rates, and no guarantees. A streak is a tool for you; missing a day is not failure.

How to begin

A sensible first week, in order.

  1. Take the 7-minute quick test. It gives a first, honest reading across all ten Law units. Start
  2. Build your strategy. Answer the 13 questions in the Exam Strategy Engine.
  3. Set your target. Check the cut-off trend and pick a safe score for your category.
  4. Study in rounds. One unit of MCQs per Pomodoro round, then review mistakes. Practise
  5. Test under exam conditions. A full mock test each week, then recalculate your strategy.
