UGC NET Paper 1 Unit 3: Comprehension Notes and MCQs

The comprehension section gives you a short passage and asks questions based only on what it says. It is the most scoring unit for candidates who read regularly and the least dependent on your subject. These notes show how to read a passage, how to separate a stated idea from an inferred one, and how to handle words in context.

What Unit 3 covers

Comprehension improves with regular timed practice, so attempt one passage a day from any reliable newspaper or past paper. The official syllabus lists 4 topics for this unit. Each is explained below with the points examiners most often use.

1Reading comprehension of a passage

  • Read the passage once at a steady pace for the overall idea, then read the questions, then return to the lines you need.
  • Skimming gives the gist quickly. Scanning finds specific names, dates or details. Re-reading helps with difficult sentences.
  • Answer only from the passage. Outside knowledge or personal opinion can lead you to a wrong option.
  • Notice the author's tone and purpose: to inform, to persuade, to criticise or to entertain.

2Identification of central theme, arguments and supporting ideas

  • The central theme is the main idea the whole passage develops. The title and the first and last sentences often point to it.
  • Supporting ideas are examples, reasons or evidence that develop the main idea. They are not the main idea itself.
  • Look for signal words: however, but and yet mark contrast; therefore and thus mark conclusions; for example marks evidence.
  • A good summary covers the main idea and leaves out minor detail.

3Inference, implication and logical conclusions

  • An inference is a conclusion drawn from given information. It must be supported by the passage even if it is not stated word for word.
  • Stated (explicit) meaning is written directly; implied (implicit) meaning must be worked out from clues.
  • An assumption is an unstated idea the argument depends on. Questions on NOT supported or cannot be inferred ask for the option the passage does not justify.
  • Avoid extreme options with words such as always, never and only unless the passage uses them.

4Meaning of words and expressions in context

  • Use the surrounding sentences to decode an unfamiliar word. Context clues include synonyms, antonyms, examples and explanations.
  • A word can have more than one meaning, so choose the meaning that fits the sentence.
  • Know common synonym and antonym pairs, one-word substitutions and idioms. For example, bibliophile means a lover of books.
  • Check prefixes, roots and suffixes: for example, in- often shows negation and anti- means against.

Key terms and facts to remember

Central theme

The main idea that the whole passage develops.

Inference

A conclusion drawn from the passage; it must be supported by the text.

Explicit and implicit

Explicit means clearly stated; implicit means implied but not stated directly.

Tone

The author's attitude, such as critical, neutral, optimistic or sarcastic.

Skimming and scanning

Skimming gets the general idea; scanning searches for a specific detail.

Terse

Brief and to the point; opposite of verbose.

Ephemeral

Lasting a very short time; opposite of eternal.

Meticulous

Showing great attention to detail.

Candid, futile, prudent

Frank, useless and careful, respectively.

To burn the midnight oil

To work or study late into the night.

3Passage: 'Technology has made information easily accessible, yet the ability to judge its reliability remains rare.' The main point is:

  1. Reliability is unimportant
  2. Judging reliability is rare despite easy access
  3. Technology is harmful

Answer: B. Judging reliability is rare despite easy access

How to study this unit

  • Read the questions before the passage when time is short, then read actively for those answers.
  • Eliminate options that go beyond the text. The correct answer is usually the plainest statement the passage supports.
  • Build vocabulary through daily reading of editorials, and note each new word with its sentence, not just its meaning.
