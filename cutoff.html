Ranges and "safe" targets are analysis, not forecasts you can bank on

The expected range is a statistical summary of recent sessions plus our reading of the pattern. NTA does not announce a cut-off in advance, and the actual figure can fall outside any range we show.

Cut-offs depend on how hard the paper was, how many candidates sat it, how they scored in each category and how NTA applies its qualification rules for that session. Past results describe what happened. They do not determine what will happen. A "safe target" is a planning buffer, and no score is safe in a competitive ranking.

Mark targets and study routes are illustrative

The unit-wise marks and the three routes to a target score are an educator's planning frame, not a prediction of how any paper will be set or how you will perform.

Question distribution across units varies by session and is not published in advance. Which topics are "easy" depends on your background and preparation. Use the plans to organise your effort, then adjust them against your own mock-test results.

Educator's analysis and expected range

What five sessions of Law cut-offs say about the next one.

Expected range for the next session (Unreserved)

Calculated from the five most recent sessions that carry all three lines (June 2024 to June 2026): the average, plus and minus one standard deviation, rounded to an even mark. The safe target adds six marks, or three questions, on top of the range.

How I read the numbers

The line is steadier than aspirants fear. Over the last five sessions the Unreserved JRF line moved between 200 and 218, a spread of nine questions. The Assistant Professor line stayed between 178 and 194, and the PhD-only line between 158 and 174. Nobody can say which of those numbers the next session will print, but the whole band is narrow enough to plan against.

December sessions have printed higher than June sessions. December 2024 and December 2025 produced Unreserved lines of 218 and 218 (JRF), 194 and 188 (Assistant Professor), and 174 and 166 (PhD). June 2025 and June 2026 averaged 202, 178 and 159. That is a gap of roughly 16, 13 and 11 marks. Two sessions on each side is a small sample, and June 2024 was a re-examination that sits in between, so I treat this as a tilt rather than a rule. For a December cycle I would plan for the upper half of the range.

Your category changes the number, but not the logic. Averaged over the same five sessions, the OBC (NCL), EWS, SC and ST JRF lines sit about 14, 9, 25 and 30 marks below Unreserved. On the Assistant Professor line the gaps are about 14, 14, 25 and 32. Use the category tabs above to see the exact history, and the calculator below to set your target.

The JRF line is a ranking contest. In the three sessions where candidate counts matched across sources (June 2024, December 2024, June 2025), only 55 to 73 Unreserved candidates cleared the JRF line, against 661 to 1,057 more in the Assistant Professor band and 1,399 to 2,421 more in the PhD-only band. A JRF target is therefore a bet on being near the very top of the paper, and it deserves a larger buffer than the other two lines.

A hard paper does not protect you, and an easy one punishes you. The Unreserved JRF line was 236 in December 2022, the highest in this data set. When a paper is scored generously the line climbs quickly, so the safe habit is to build your score on accuracy in the units you control rather than to hope for a hard paper.

The numbers that would have cleared every one of the last five sessions (Unreserved) are 194 for Assistant Professor, 218 for JRF and 174 for PhD admission. Those are the highest lines in the period. Use them as your upper reference, and treat the range above as your working target.

This is an editorial reading of public data. It is not a prediction by NTA, and no mark is guaranteed to qualify. See the Disclaimer at the top of the page.

Where the easy marks are

The units that pay back fastest in Paper 1 and Paper 2 (Law), with a realistic target for each.

Every question carries 2 marks and there is no negative marking, so the plan is simple: bank the units that repeat and reward revision, protect them with practice, and attempt everything else. I have marked each unit Banker (dependable marks for most candidates), Steady (reliable with focused revision) or Selective (take what you can). The targets below are my judgement of what a well-prepared law graduate can secure, not a statistic from NTA. Paper 1 has five questions per unit and Paper 2 has about ten, though the exact split varies by session.

Paper 1 (out of 100)

  • Comprehension Banker

    8of 10

    Every answer sits inside the passage. Two timed passages a day for two weeks builds the speed you need.

  • Teaching Aptitude Banker

    8of 10

    Definitions, methods and evaluation types repeat from year to year. A week of revision is enough.

  • Research Aptitude Banker

    8of 10

    Types, steps, ethics and referencing. Fixed theory, and familiar ground for a law graduate.

  • Communication Banker

    8of 10

    Models, barriers and mass media. Short factual options with little ambiguity.

  • People, Development and Environment Banker

    8of 10

    Pollution, climate and the SDGs. It overlaps with Environment Law in Paper 2, so one effort earns twice.

  • ICT Steady

    6of 10

    Abbreviations and basic terms. A one-page list revised daily does most of the work.

  • Higher Education System Steady

    6of 10

    Regulators, commissions and policies. Memory-led, so check the latest policy changes.

  • Logical Reasoning Steady

    6of 10

    Law students have an edge on arguments and fallacies. Skip a long Venn diagram if you are slow.

  • Data Interpretation Steady

    6of 10

    Questions are often based on one data set, so mastering one chart type can pay out several marks at once.

  • Mathematical Reasoning Selective

    4of 10

    The most effort per mark in Paper 1. Take series and simple percentage or ratio items, and leave the rest.

Paper 2, Law (out of 200)

  • Constitutional and Administrative Law Banker

    14of 20

    The largest pool of repeating questions: Articles, amendments and landmark cases. Bare text plus about forty key cases.

  • Law of Torts and Consumer Protection Banker

    14of 20

    Concepts, maxims, defences and leading cases, with the Consumer Protection Act, 2019 on top.

  • Environment and Human Rights Law Banker

    14of 20

    Conventions, years and institutions such as the NGT and NHRC. Factual and repetitive.

  • IPR and Information Technology Law Banker

    14of 20

    Statute-driven terms, durations and procedures. One careful read of each Act pays well.

  • Law of Crimes Steady

    12of 20

    Study the BNS, BNSS and BSA. Many questions now ask for new section numbers, so keep an old-to-new comparison sheet.

  • Commercial Law Steady

    12of 20

    Contract, Sale of Goods, Negotiable Instruments and Companies Act. Definitions are exact, so practise eliminating options.

  • Family Law Steady

    12of 20

    Several personal laws with section numbers and recent judgments. Finish one personal law at a time.

  • Public International Law and IHL Steady

    12of 20

    Sources, UN organs, treaties and conventions. Fix names and dates in your head.

  • Comparative Public Law and Systems of Governance Steady

    12of 20

    A narrow syllabus with predictable comparisons. Ideal for a short, high-return sprint.

  • Jurisprudence Selective

    10of 20

    Theory-heavy with close options. Learn the jurist-to-theory pairings and do not chase fine distinctions.

Sources and method

Where the numbers come from, and how the range was calculated.

  1. Collection. Figures come from NTA's published subject and category-wise cut-off notices and from published compilations of them. The June 2025 figures were checked line by line against the NTA notice.
  2. Cross-matching. For every other session we compared several independent published compilations. The label on each row of the table shows how many matched. Where they disagreed on a cell (June 2023, OBC and EWS), we show a dash.
  3. Reading the columns. NTA prints three lines for each category: JRF, Assistant Professor and PhD only. A higher line means a smaller group of candidates qualified at that level.
  4. The expected range. It is the average of the latest five sessions that have all three lines, plus and minus one standard deviation, rounded to an even mark because every question carries 2 marks. On a normal spread, roughly one session in three can land outside such a range.
  5. The safe target. The top of the range plus six marks, which is three questions.
  6. Updating. When NTA publishes a new session, the data file is updated and the range recalculates.
