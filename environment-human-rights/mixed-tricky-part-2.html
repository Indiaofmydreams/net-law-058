23 practice questions with answers

1The 'phrase rarest of rare' in Indian law relates to:

  1. Award of life imprisonment
  2. Cancellation of bail in heinous offences
  3. Grant of presidential pardon
  4. Imposition of death penalty - evolved in Bachan Singh v. State of Punjab (1980)
Show answer and explanation

Answer: D. Imposition of death penalty - evolved in Bachan Singh v. State of Punjab (1980)

Bachan Singh v. State of Punjab (1980) - the SC upheld the constitutional validity of the death penalty but laid down the 'rarest of rare' doctrine: death sentence should be imposed only when the alternative option of life imprisonment is unquestionably foreclosed.

2The Right to Information Act 2005 is relevant to environmental law because:

  1. It only applies to income tax and administrative matters
  2. It allows citizens to obtain environmental data, pollution records, EIA reports, and inspection reports from public authorities
  3. It exempts all environmental information from disclosure
  4. It applies only to Central Government departments
Show answer and explanation

Answer: B. It allows citizens to obtain environmental data, pollution records, EIA reports, and inspection reports from public authorities

RTI Act 2005 is a powerful tool for environmental democracy - citizens can seek: pollution monitoring data from PCBs; EIA reports and environmental clearances from MoEFCC; inspection reports of industries. Section 8(2) mandates disclosure of environmental information even relating to trade secrets if public interest outweighs the harm.

3The Directive Principles of State Policy relating to environmental protection are:

  1. Non-justiciable but fundamental to governance, and courts have read them with Art 21 to create enforceable environmental rights
  2. Justiciable and can be directly enforced by courts
  3. Superior to Fundamental Rights in all cases
  4. Applicable only to the Central Government
Show answer and explanation

Answer: A. Non-justiciable but fundamental to governance, and courts have read them with Art 21 to create enforceable environmental rights

DPSPs (including Art 48A on environment) are non-justiciable under Article 37 - but the SC has consistently used them to expand the scope of Article 21. Article 48A has been read with Article 21 to create a judicially enforceable right to clean environment through harmonious construction.

4The concept of 'environmental justice' includes:

  1. Only access to environmental courts
  2. Justice for environmental damage to wildlife only
  3. Compensation payable by states for transboundary pollution
  4. Equitable distribution of environmental benefits and burdens, particularly for marginalized communities, along with procedural access to information and courts
Show answer and explanation

Answer: D. Equitable distribution of environmental benefits and burdens, particularly for marginalized communities, along with procedural access to information and courts

Environmental justice combines: distributive justice (fair distribution of environmental benefits and burdens); procedural justice (access to information, participation and courts); and corrective justice (remediation and compensation). It connects environmental law to human rights and social justice.

5Which of the following cases established that the right to shelter/housing is part of Article 21?

  1. Olga Tellis v. Bombay Municipal Corporation (1985)
  2. Chameli Singh v. State of UP (1996)
  3. Francis Coralie Mullin v. UT of Delhi (1981)
  4. All of the above
Show answer and explanation

Answer: D. All of the above

All three cases recognized housing/shelter as part of Article 21: Francis Coralie Mullin (1981) - right to live with human dignity includes shelter; Olga Tellis (1985) - right to livelihood and shelter of pavement dwellers; Chameli Singh (1996) - SC explicitly held right to shelter is a fundamental right under Article 21.

6'Human rights due diligence' as applied to corporations means:

  1. Ongoing process to identify, prevent, mitigate and account for human rights impacts in business operations and value chains
  2. Compliance with domestic labour laws only
  3. Annual reporting on corporate social responsibility
  4. Compensation for environmental damage caused by industrial operations
Show answer and explanation

Answer: A. Ongoing process to identify, prevent, mitigate and account for human rights impacts in business operations and value chains

Human rights due diligence (under the UN Guiding Principles on Business and Human Rights 2011 - the Ruggie Framework) requires corporations to: assess actual and potential human rights impacts; integrate findings into business processes; track responses; and communicate how impacts are addressed.

7The principle that 'human rights are inalienable' means:

  1. Rights cannot be transferred to another person or surrendered voluntarily
  2. Rights can never be restricted under any circumstances
  3. Rights belong only to nationals, not aliens
  4. Rights cannot be enforced in courts
Show answer and explanation

Answer: A. Rights cannot be transferred to another person or surrendered voluntarily

Inalienability means human rights cannot be taken away, waived, or transferred - they belong to every person by virtue of being human. A person cannot validly 'consent' to be enslaved or tortured. Inalienability does not mean rights are absolute - it means they cannot be permanently extinguished or given up.

8The 'Ruggie Framework' (2011) on business and human rights establishes three pillars. Which correctly states them?

  1. Promote, Protect, Punish
  2. Regulate, Monitor, Enforce
  3. Legislate, Adjudicate, Compensate
  4. State duty to Protect human rights; Corporate responsibility to Respect human rights; Access to Remedy for victims
Show answer and explanation

Answer: D. State duty to Protect human rights; Corporate responsibility to Respect human rights; Access to Remedy for victims

The UN Guiding Principles on Business and Human Rights (2011) - the 'Ruggie Framework' - rests on three pillars: Protect (state duty to protect against third-party human rights abuses); Respect (corporate responsibility to respect human rights - due diligence); Remedy (access to effective remedy for those affected).

9The relationship between the NHRC and State Human Rights Commissions under the PHR Act 1993 is:

  1. Hierarchical - NHRC can overrule SHRC decisions
  2. Competitive - either can take up any matter
  3. Sequential - complaints must first go to SHRC then to NHRC
  4. Complementary but non-hierarchical - NHRC handles central/inter-state matters, SHRC handles List II/III matters; NHRC cannot overrule SHRC
Show answer and explanation

Answer: D. Complementary but non-hierarchical - NHRC handles central/inter-state matters, SHRC handles List II/III matters; NHRC cannot overrule SHRC

The PHR Act 1993 establishes a complementary (not hierarchical) relationship: NHRC has jurisdiction over central government and Union List matters; SHRC has jurisdiction over State and Concurrent List matters. Section 36(1) prevents NHRC from inquiring into matters pending before SHRC. NHRC cannot overrule or review SHRC decisions.

10The 'Durban Declaration and Programme of Action' (2001) deals with:

  1. Climate finance for developing countries
  2. Racism, racial discrimination, xenophobia and related intolerance
  3. Rights of indigenous peoples
  4. Disarmament and non-proliferation
Show answer and explanation

Answer: B. Racism, racial discrimination, xenophobia and related intolerance

The Durban Declaration and Programme of Action (DDPA) was adopted at the World Conference Against Racism (Durban, South Africa, 2001). It is the most comprehensive international document on combating racism, racial discrimination, xenophobia and related intolerance.

11The 'right to food' as a human right in India has been recognized through:

  1. Right to Food Act 2013 (National Food Security Act)
  2. Supreme Court orders in PUCL v. UOI (2001 - ongoing) and the National Food Security Act 2013
  3. Article 21 alone
  4. ICESCR alone
Show answer and explanation

Answer: B. Supreme Court orders in PUCL v. UOI (2001 - ongoing) and the National Food Security Act 2013

PUCL v. UOI (2001 - 'Right to Food case') - the SC converted various food security schemes into judicially enforceable entitlements by interpreting Article 21 to include the right to food. The National Food Security Act 2013 subsequently gave statutory basis to this right.

12The term 'stateless person' in international human rights law refers to:

  1. A person without a passport
  2. A person who is not considered as a national by any state under the operation of its law - defined in the 1954 Convention Relating to the Status of Stateless Persons
  3. A refugee who has lost their nationality
  4. A person living in a failed state
Show answer and explanation

Answer: B. A person who is not considered as a national by any state under the operation of its law - defined in the 1954 Convention Relating to the Status of Stateless Persons

The 1954 Convention Relating to the Status of Stateless Persons defines 'stateless person' as a person who is not considered as a national by any state under the operation of its law. Statelessness is a serious human rights problem. India is not party to either the 1954 or 1961 Convention.

13The concept of 'positive peace' (as distinguished from 'negative peace') means:

  1. A peace treaty signed with positive mutual intentions
  2. Peace maintained by positive military force
  3. International peace agreements that create positive legal obligations
  4. Not merely the absence of war but the presence of justice, equality, and fulfilment of human rights
Show answer and explanation

Answer: D. Not merely the absence of war but the presence of justice, equality, and fulfilment of human rights

Johan Galtung (1969) distinguished: Negative peace (absence of war, direct violence) and Positive peace (absence of structural violence - presence of justice, equality, respect for human rights). This distinction is relevant to the third-generation right to peace and the development-rights nexus.

14PIL (Public Interest Litigation) and environmental rights in India are related because:

  1. PIL cannot be used for environmental matters - only regular suits can
  2. PIL has been the primary vehicle through which environmental rights have been enforced in India, relaxing locus standi requirements
  3. PIL is limited to fundamental rights and cannot cover environmental statutes
  4. PIL can only be filed by registered NGOs
Show answer and explanation

Answer: B. PIL has been the primary vehicle through which environmental rights have been enforced in India, relaxing locus standi requirements

PIL relaxed the traditional locus standi requirement - any public-spirited citizen can approach the SC (Article 32) or HC (Article 226). This opened the floodgates for environmental litigation in India. M.C. Mehta, RLEK, Vellore Citizens, Almitra Patel - all landmark environmental cases were PILs.

15The Principle of 'Non-Regression' in environmental law means:

  1. Environmental law must always become stricter over time
  2. State environmental standards cannot be lower than international standards
  3. Once a certain level of environmental protection is achieved through law or policy, it should not be rolled back
  4. Environmental regulations once enacted cannot be amended
Show answer and explanation

Answer: C. Once a certain level of environmental protection is achieved through law or policy, it should not be rolled back

Non-regression (or standstill principle) holds that environmental standards already achieved should not be deliberately diminished. It prevents 'regulatory rollback' - governments weakening environmental protections once achieved. Related to the prohibition on retrogression in socioeconomic rights under ICESCR.

16UNGA Resolution 76/300 (July 2022) recognized:

  1. Right to a safe climate as a human right
  2. Right to biodiversity protection
  3. Right to a clean, healthy and sustainable environment as a universal human right
  4. Right to compensation for climate change victims
Show answer and explanation

Answer: C. Right to a clean, healthy and sustainable environment as a universal human right

UNGA Resolution 76/300 (28 July 2022) - adopted by consensus (161 in favour, 0 against, 8 abstentions) - recognized for the first time that access to a clean, healthy and sustainable environment is a universal human right. The UNHRC had first recognized it through Resolution 48/13 (October 2021).

17'Climate litigation' as a global trend refers to:

  1. Disputes between nations on climate finance
  2. Lawsuits filed before domestic and international courts to compel governments and corporations to take climate action or pay for climate damage
  3. Criminal prosecution of polluters under domestic law
  4. Arbitration of climate-related trade disputes
Show answer and explanation

Answer: B. Lawsuits filed before domestic and international courts to compel governments and corporations to take climate action or pay for climate damage

Climate litigation - cases filed in domestic courts challenging government inaction on climate change. Landmark cases: Urgenda Foundation v. Netherlands (2019 - Dutch SC ordered government to reduce emissions by 25%); Neubauer v. Germany (2021 - German Constitutional Court ordered stronger climate law).

18The 'Bharat Stage' emission norms in India are:

  1. Environmental clearance stages for industries
  2. Vehicle emission standards prescribed for automobiles, modelled on European standards
  3. Standards for thermal power plant emissions
  4. Standards for industrial zones near urban areas
Show answer and explanation

Answer: B. Vehicle emission standards prescribed for automobiles, modelled on European standards

Bharat Stage (BS) emission norms are vehicular emission standards prescribed by the Central Pollution Control Board under EPA 1986. India directly leapt from BS-IV to BS-VI norms in April 2020, skipping BS-V, following directions of the Supreme Court in vehicular pollution cases.

19The Scheduled Castes and Scheduled Tribes (Prevention of Atrocities) Act 1989 was challenged and partially diluted by the Supreme Court in:

  1. Indra Sawhney v. Union of India (1992)
  2. Subhash Kashinath Mahajan v. State of Maharashtra (2018)
  3. M. Nagaraj v. Union of India (2006)
  4. Jarnail Singh v. Lachhmi Narain Gupta (2018)
Show answer and explanation

Answer: B. Subhash Kashinath Mahajan v. State of Maharashtra (2018)

In Subhash Kashinath Mahajan v. State of Maharashtra (2018), the SC directed that: no automatic arrest on SC/ST Act complaints; prior inquiry by DSP before FIR; anticipatory bail can be granted. Parliament responded by enacting the SC/ST (POA) Amendment Act 2018 to negate this judgment.

20The 'intersectionality' concept in human rights law (coined by Kimberle Crenshaw) means:

  1. The intersection of different branches of law
  2. The intersection of international and domestic human rights law
  3. The way multiple forms of discrimination (race, gender, class, disability) overlap and compound each other creating unique forms of disadvantage
  4. How human rights intersect with criminal law
Show answer and explanation

Answer: C. The way multiple forms of discrimination (race, gender, class, disability) overlap and compound each other creating unique forms of disadvantage

Kimberle Crenshaw (1989) coined 'intersectionality' - Black women face discrimination that is not simply the sum of race discrimination + sex discrimination but is a unique, compounded form of disadvantage. This concept has been adopted in human rights law by the CEDAW Committee, CRC Committee and others.

21The 'non-discrimination' principle in human rights law contains both 'formal equality' and 'substantive equality'. The key difference is:

  1. Formal equality means identical treatment; substantive equality means treating similarly situated persons similarly
  2. Substantive equality is more limited than formal equality
  3. They are two names for the same concept
  4. Formal equality means treating everyone identically regardless of circumstances; substantive equality means taking into account structural disadvantages to achieve real equality of outcomes
Show answer and explanation

Answer: D. Formal equality means treating everyone identically regardless of circumstances; substantive equality means taking into account structural disadvantages to achieve real equality of outcomes

Formal equality (treating like cases alike) can perpetuate inequality if structural disadvantages are not addressed. Substantive equality requires positive measures to overcome historical and structural discrimination - the basis for affirmative action/reservations. Indian constitutional law through Articles 15(4), 16(4) embodies substantive equality.

22The 'Aarhus Convention 1998' is sometimes called a bridge between:

  1. Environmental law and criminal law
  2. Domestic law and international trade law
  3. North and South - developed and developing nations
  4. Environmental law and human rights law
Show answer and explanation

Answer: D. Environmental law and human rights law

The Aarhus Convention explicitly links environmental protection with human rights - its Preamble states that every person has the right to live in an environment adequate to their health and well-being. The three rights it protects (information, participation, access to justice) are procedural human rights applied in the environmental context.

23The Scheduled Castes and Scheduled Tribes (Prevention of Atrocities) Amendment Act 2015 added new offences. Which of the following was NOT an existing offence under the original 1989 Act?

  1. Social or economic boycott of SC/ST communities
  2. Forcing a member of SC/ST to leave their house or village
  3. Committing a sexual offence against an SC/ST woman
  4. Wrongful dispossession of land belonging to SC/ST
Show answer and explanation

Answer: A. Social or economic boycott of SC/ST communities

Social or economic boycott was not in the original SC/ST (POA) Act 1989 - it was added as a new offence by the Amendment Act 2015. The 2015 Amendment significantly expanded the list of offences to cover social boycott, garlanding with footwear, forcing to eat inedible/obnoxious substances, and other forms of social humiliation.
