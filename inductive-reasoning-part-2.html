About this question set

This set has 25 multiple-choice questions on Inductive Reasoning from the UGC NET Paper 1 syllabus, unit 5, Mathematical Reasoning and Aptitude. Try each question first, then tap Show answer to check the key and read the explanation. 25 of the 25 questions carry a written explanation.

For the concepts and case law behind these questions, read the Mathematical Reasoning notes. Once you are comfortable, test yourself under exam conditions with a full-length mock test.

25 practice questions with answers

1Perfect induction is based on:

  1. A hypothesis
  2. Analogy
  3. A sample of the class
  4. Examination of all members of the class
Show answer and explanation

Answer: D. Examination of all members of the class

With complete enumeration the conclusion is certain, which makes it a special case.

Report this question

2In induction by simple enumeration, a generalisation is made because:

  1. An authority approves it
  2. No contrary instance has been found
  3. The cause has been established
  4. It follows by deduction
Show answer and explanation

Answer: B. No contrary instance has been found

"all observed X are Y, and no exception is known".

Report this question

3Scientific induction rests on the principles of:

  1. Law of identity
  2. Law of contradiction
  3. Uniformity of nature and law of causation
  4. Law of excluded middle
Show answer and explanation

Answer: C. Uniformity of nature and law of causation

The future resembles the past, and every event has a cause.

Report this question

4Imperfect (ampliative) induction proceeds from:

  1. A definition to its example
  2. All cases to some cases
  3. Observed cases to unobserved cases
  4. One premise to its converse
Show answer and explanation

Answer: C. Observed cases to unobserved cases

The conclusion extends beyond the evidence.

Report this question

5An online poll counts only those who choose to reply. The resulting error is:

  1. Self-selection bias
  2. Random sampling
  3. Stratified sampling
  4. Perfect induction
Show answer and explanation

Answer: A. Self-selection bias

Volunteers differ from the general population.

Report this question

6Studying only successful start-ups to find "the recipe for success", ignoring those that failed, shows:

  1. Perfect induction
  2. Abduction
  3. Random sampling
  4. Survivorship bias
Show answer and explanation

Answer: D. Survivorship bias

The evidence set leaves out the failures.

Report this question

7Citing only favourable evidence and ignoring contrary evidence is the fallacy of:

  1. Abduction
  2. Suppressed evidence
  3. Deduction
  4. Analogy
Show answer and explanation

Answer: B. Suppressed evidence

Cherry-picking weakens inductive strength.

Report this question

8In a poll using a random sample, a larger sample generally gives:

  1. No effect on error
  2. A larger margin of error
  3. A biased result
  4. A smaller margin of error
Show answer and explanation

Answer: D. A smaller margin of error

More random data means more precision.

Report this question

9Dividing a population into sub-groups and then drawing a random sample from each is called:

  1. Stratified sampling
  2. Convenience sampling
  3. Quota sampling
  4. Snowball sampling
Show answer and explanation

Answer: A. Stratified sampling

Every stratum is represented in proportion.

Report this question

10Mill's "Joint Method" combines:

  1. Residues and Concomitant Variation
  2. Agreement and Difference
  3. Agreement and Residues
  4. Difference and Concomitant Variation
Show answer and explanation

Answer: B. Agreement and Difference

It uses two sets of instances, one for agreement and one for difference.

Report this question

11The doctrine that the same effect may be produced by different causes is known as:

  1. Unity of cause
  2. Law of identity
  3. Plurality of causes
  4. Single causation
Show answer and explanation

Answer: C. Plurality of causes

For example, fever can be caused by many illnesses.

Report this question

12In inductive science, a hypothesis is generally accepted when:

  1. It is liked by most scientists
  2. It is supported by evidence and survives testing
  3. An authority endorses it
  4. It is the most complex one
Show answer and explanation

Answer: B. It is supported by evidence and survives testing

Confirmation comes through testing, not preference.

Report this question

13Karl Popper held that scientific theories are distinguished by:

  1. Induction
  2. Verifiability
  3. Falsifiability
  4. Authority
Show answer and explanation

Answer: C. Falsifiability

A scientific theory must be capable of being refuted by evidence.

Report this question

14The "inductive leap" refers to:

  1. Moving from observed cases to a claim about unobserved cases
  2. Matching two propositions
  3. Rejecting all evidence
  4. Deriving a theorem from axioms
Show answer and explanation

Answer: A. Moving from observed cases to a claim about unobserved cases

The conclusion goes beyond the data in hand.

Report this question

15Which of the following is an example of inductive reasoning?

  1. Car A resembles car B, and B has a sunroof, so A has one
  2. The ground is wet, so it rained
  3. All squares have four sides; this is a square; so it has four sides
  4. Every ice cube I have touched is cold, so all ice cubes are cold
Show answer and explanation

Answer: D. Every ice cube I have touched is cold, so all ice cubes are cold

Generalisation from observed instances. (C) is deduction, (B) abduction and (A) analogy.

Report this question

16An inductive generalisation is made stronger by:

  1. Assuming the premises
  2. Increasing the number and variety of instances
  3. Using ambiguous terms
  4. Deleting contrary evidence
Show answer and explanation

Answer: B. Increasing the number and variety of instances

More and more varied instances reduce the chance of bias.

Report this question

17A poll of 50,000 voters taken only from one city to predict a national result is weak because the sample is:

  1. Unrepresentative
  2. Random
  3. Stratified
  4. Too large
Show answer and explanation

Answer: A. Unrepresentative

Size cannot make up for lack of representativeness.

Report this question

18An argument from authority is:

  1. Deductive
  2. Always valid
  3. Inductive, and strong only when the authority is a genuine expert in the relevant field
  4. Abductive
Show answer and explanation

Answer: C. Inductive, and strong only when the authority is a genuine expert in the relevant field

Strength depends on the relevance and reliability of the expert.

Report this question

19Assuming that what is true of the parts must be true of the whole is the fallacy of:

  1. Division
  2. Accent
  3. Equivocation
  4. Composition
Show answer and explanation

Answer: D. Composition

"each player is excellent, so the team is excellent" does not follow.

Report this question

20Assuming that what is true of the whole must be true of each part is the fallacy of:

  1. Amphiboly
  2. Composition
  3. Division
  4. Equivocation
Show answer and explanation

Answer: C. Division

It is the reverse of composition.

Report this question

21Statement I: In inductive reasoning, the premises can be true while the conclusion is false.
Statement II: Adding more instances, regardless of bias, always makes an inductive argument stronger.

  1. I false, II true
  2. Both false
  3. Both true
  4. I true, II false
Show answer and explanation

Answer: D. I true, II false

Induction gives no guarantee, and biased instances do not add strength.

Report this question

22Assertion (A): Inductive conclusions are probable, not certain.
Reason (R): They go beyond the information contained in the premises.

  1. A true, R false
  2. Both true, and R is the correct explanation of A
  3. Both true, but R is not the correct explanation
  4. A false, R true
Show answer and explanation

Answer: B. Both true, and R is the correct explanation of A

Because the conclusion says more than the premises, it cannot be guaranteed.

Report this question

23Match List I with List II:
List I: (a) Method of Agreement (b) Method of Difference (c) Method of Residues (d) Concomitant Variation
List II: (i) Remainder after subtracting known effects (ii) Common circumstance (iii) Single differing circumstance (iv) Proportional change

  1. a-ii, b-iii, c-i, d-iv
  2. a-ii, b-i, c-iii, d-iv
  3. a-iv, b-iii, c-i, d-ii
  4. a-iii, b-ii, c-i, d-iv
Show answer and explanation

Answer: A. a-ii, b-iii, c-i, d-iv

Each of Mill's methods is paired with its core test.

Report this question

24Arrange the steps of inductive scientific method in order:
1. Observation
2. Formulating a hypothesis
3. Testing by experiment
4. Generalisation or theory

  1. 4, 3, 2, 1
  2. 2, 1, 3, 4
  3. 1, 2, 3, 4
  4. 1, 3, 2, 4
Show answer and explanation

Answer: C. 1, 2, 3, 4

Observe, hypothesise, test, then generalise.

Report this question

25Which of the following is NOT a feature of inductive arguments?

  1. Strength is a matter of degree
  2. The conclusion follows with certainty
  3. The conclusion goes beyond the premises
  4. New evidence may change the strength
Show answer and explanation

Answer: B. The conclusion follows with certainty

Certainty belongs to deduction.
