4-week pathway

You have 4 weeks. Use them with focus.

Overview: stop widening, start sharpening

Four weeks is a short window, and that makes focus your advantage. This is the time to stop reading more and start retrieving what you know. Use the weeks to revise, test, find weak areas and close them. If you have already studied the syllabus, this is the plan for you. If you have not, it still works, because you will concentrate on the subjects and skills that earn the most marks.

The science behind this plan

Every choice below rests on research about how people actually learn. Five ideas matter most.

  • Test yourself, don’t just reread. In a classic experiment, students who studied a passage once and then took repeated recall tests remembered 61% of it a week later, against 40% for students who simply restudied it. Restudying felt better and even scored higher at five minutes (81% against 75%), which is why so many people trust it.2 A major review of ten study techniques rated practice testing and spaced practice as the two most useful, and rated rereading and highlighting as low in usefulness.1 Retrieval has also beaten elaborate note-making methods in direct comparison.4
  • Space it out. Reviews of the research find that spreading study over time generally beats cramming the same hours into one block.3 Revisit each topic after a day, a week and a month.
  • Mix it up. Alternating between topics, rather than finishing one before starting the next, improves later performance, even though it feels harder while you do it.5,6 Researchers call this a “desirable difficulty”: effort that feels slower now and pays off later.9
  • Practise where you are weak, with feedback. Expert performance comes from focused practice on the weakest parts, with clear feedback.7 Feedback works best when it tells you where you stand, where you are heading and how to close the gap.8 Your error log does exactly this.
  • Protect sleep and belief. Sleep helps consolidate what you have learned.10 And belief in your own ability drives effort and persistence.11 Students who see ability as something that can grow tend to do better, especially when they were struggling.12

What the exam asks of you

CLAT PG is one paper of 120 multiple-choice questions in 120 minutes, with 1 mark for each correct answer and a deduction of 0.25 for each wrong one.15 That means about one minute per question, and the questions are passage-based, so reading speed and legal reasoning matter as much as memory. Two facts about the marking shape your strategy:

+0.06average marks from a blind guess among four options
+0.17after eliminating one option
+0.38after eliminating two options

The arithmetic is simple: with four options, a blind guess is worth 0.25 × 1 minus 0.75 × 0.25, which is about +0.06. Eliminating options is what lifts your expected gain, so train yourself to eliminate before you guess. These figures assume four options per question, as in recent papers.

Your loop for every topic: read it, close the notes, test yourself, check the answer, fix the gap, and test again after a day, a week and a month.

A typical day

  • Morning: one retrieval session on a weak subject.
  • Afternoon: two timed passage sets or a mock.
  • Evening: review errors, update the log, and write tomorrow’s plan. Stop at a fixed time and sleep.

Plan about 6 to 8 full mocks across the month. Review each one for at least as long as it took to write.

What to avoid

  • Rereading and highlighting as your main method. They feel productive and rank low in the evidence.1
  • Finishing one subject completely before touching the rest, then never returning.
  • Taking mocks and not reviewing them. A mock you do not analyse teaches almost nothing.8
  • Starting new books. This is not the time to widen your reading.

Say this to yourself

  • I have four weeks, and I will use every day on purpose.
  • Focus beats volume, and I am choosing focus.
  • Every mistake I find now is a mark I will win later.
  • I have already learned more than I think, and testing will show it.
  • Calm and consistent is how I will walk into the exam.

Your mind on exam day

  • Write your worries down. In a classroom study, students prone to test anxiety who wrote about their worries for ten minutes before an exam performed better than those who did not.13 Try it in the last mocks too.
  • Sleep the night before. A fully rested brain recalls more than a crammed one.10
  • Know your pace. Plan a first pass of about 90 minutes and keep the last 30 for the questions you marked.
  • Arrive ready. Download your admit card, check the centre and reporting time, and carry the documents it lists.
