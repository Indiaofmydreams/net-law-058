14 practice questions with answers

1'Certiorari' is a writ that:

  1. Restrains a person from acting in a particular way
  2. Asks why a person should not be produced before a court
  3. Commands a person to do a public duty
  4. Brings up the record of a lower tribunal to a superior court to quash the decision
Show answer and explanation

Answer: D. Brings up the record of a lower tribunal to a superior court to quash the decision

Certiorari is issued by a superior court to an inferior tribunal to remove proceedings and quash them if the inferior tribunal has acted without jurisdiction, in excess of jurisdiction, in violation of natural justice, or there is an error apparent on the face of the record.

2'Mandamus' as a writ is issued when:

  1. A court certifies a question to a higher court
  2. A decision needs to be quashed
  3. There is an illegal detention
  4. A public authority fails to perform its public duty
Show answer and explanation

Answer: D. A public authority fails to perform its public duty

Mandamus (Latin: 'we command') is issued by a superior court to a public authority, official, corporation, or lower court directing them to perform a public duty which they have failed or refused to perform.

3Under what circumstances is the writ of Habeas Corpus issued?

  1. To quash an illegal order
  2. To compel performance of a public duty
  3. To direct production of a detained person before court to examine legality of detention
  4. To restrain a court from exceeding its jurisdiction
Show answer and explanation

Answer: C. To direct production of a detained person before court to examine legality of detention

Habeas Corpus (Latin: 'you have the body') is the most important writ for protection of individual liberty. It requires the detaining authority to produce the detained person before the court so the court can determine the legality of the detention.

4The grounds of judicial review in English law were authoritatively classified into three categories in which case?

  1. Council of Civil Service Unions v. Minister for Civil Service — GCHQ Case (1985)
  2. Anisminic v. Foreign Compensation Commission (1969)
  3. Associated Provincial Picture Houses v. Wednesbury Corporation (1948)
  4. Ridge v. Baldwin (1964)
Show answer and explanation

Answer: A. Council of Civil Service Unions v. Minister for Civil Service — GCHQ Case (1985)

Lord Diplock in the GCHQ Case (1985) classified the grounds of judicial review into three categories: (1) Illegality; (2) Irrationality (Wednesbury unreasonableness); (3) Procedural Impropriety. Concept note: Lord Diplock's Three Grounds (GCHQ Case, 1985): (1) Illegality — decision-maker must understand and act within the law; (2) Irrationality — decision so unreasonable no reasonable authority could have made it (Wednesbury); (3) Procedural Impropriety — failure to observe statutory procedures or natural justice. A 4th ground — Proportionality — is now recognized in EU/Human Rights contexts.

5'Wednesbury unreasonableness' refers to:

  1. A decision made without jurisdiction
  2. A decision against natural justice
  3. A decision so outrageous in its defiance of logic or accepted moral standards that no sensible person could have arrived at it
  4. A decision that is slightly wrong
Show answer and explanation

Answer: C. A decision so outrageous in its defiance of logic or accepted moral standards that no sensible person could have arrived at it

Wednesbury unreasonableness comes from Associated Provincial Picture Houses Ltd. v. Wednesbury Corporation [1948] 1 KB 223, where Lord Greene MR described it as a decision so unreasonable that no reasonable authority could have come to it.

6The doctrine of 'Ultra Vires' in administrative law means:

  1. Acting against natural justice
  2. Acting with malice
  3. Acting beyond the powers granted by the enabling statute
  4. Acting within the granted powers
Show answer and explanation

Answer: C. Acting beyond the powers granted by the enabling statute

Ultra vires (Latin: 'beyond powers') means acting outside the scope of authority granted. Two types: (1) Substantive ultra vires — acting beyond the scope of powers; (2) Procedural ultra vires — failing to follow prescribed procedures.

7'Proportionality' as a ground of judicial review requires that:

  1. The decision must be proportionate to the resources available
  2. The means adopted must be proportionate to the end sought — fair balance between individual rights and public interest
  3. Punishment must be proportionate to the offence in criminal cases only
  4. Administrative decisions must be made within a proportionate time
Show answer and explanation

Answer: B. The means adopted must be proportionate to the end sought — fair balance between individual rights and public interest

Proportionality requires a fair balance between the means used and the end sought. It is stricter than Wednesbury unreasonableness. Recognised in India in Om Kumar v. Union of India (2001). Courts examine whether a less restrictive means could achieve the same result.

8Judicial review of administrative action in India is primarily based on:

  1. Articles 32 and 226 of the Constitution
  2. The Administrative Tribunals Act, 1985
  3. Article 136 SLP only
  4. Article 131
Show answer and explanation

Answer: A. Articles 32 and 226 of the Constitution

Judicial review of administrative action in India primarily flows from Articles 32 (Supreme Court) and 226 (High Courts) through the writ jurisdiction. It is a constitutional power, not merely a statutory one.

9The doctrine of 'Legitimate Expectation' as a ground of judicial review was first clearly expounded in Indian law in:

  1. Maneka Gandhi v. Union of India
  2. Navjyoti Coop. Group Housing Society v. Union of India (1992)
  3. A.K. Kraipak v. Union of India
  4. S.R. Bommai v. Union of India
Show answer and explanation

Answer: B. Navjyoti Coop. Group Housing Society v. Union of India (1992)

The doctrine of Legitimate Expectation was clearly expounded in Indian administrative law in Navjyoti Coop. Group Housing Society v. Union of India (1992), where the SC held that a change in policy without giving an opportunity to be heard violated legitimate expectation.

10'Mala fide' (bad faith) as a ground of judicial review implies:

  1. The authority acted with personal animosity or improper purpose
  2. The authority had no jurisdiction
  3. The decision was unreasonable
  4. The authority made a technical error
Show answer and explanation

Answer: A. The authority acted with personal animosity or improper purpose

Mala fide or bad faith as a ground of judicial review means the authority exercised its power for an improper purpose, with personal animosity, or in abuse of power. Courts are very slow to impute mala fides — it must be clearly established.

Report this question

11'Error apparent on the face of the record' as a ground of certiorari means:

  1. Any error in the decision
  2. Only an error so obvious and manifest that it does not require elaborate reasoning to detect
  3. Factual error
  4. Jurisdictional error only
Show answer and explanation

Answer: B. Only an error so obvious and manifest that it does not require elaborate reasoning to detect

Error apparent on the face of the record must be clear and manifest without requiring detailed argument or examination of evidence. Established in Syed Yakoob v. K.S. Radhakrishnan (1964 SC).

12In Anisminic Ltd. v. Foreign Compensation Commission (1969) [HL], the House of Lords held:

  1. Natural justice cannot apply to tribunals
  2. The Commission had acted within its powers
  3. Certiorari does not lie against statutory tribunals
  4. Any error of law (not just jurisdictional error in the old technical sense) makes a decision a nullity
Show answer and explanation

Answer: D. Any error of law (not just jurisdictional error in the old technical sense) makes a decision a nullity

Anisminic (1969) effectively abolished the old distinction between 'jurisdictional error' (always reviewable) and 'error within jurisdiction' (not reviewable by certiorari). The HL held that any error of law goes to jurisdiction and makes the decision a nullity.

13The 'Doctrine of Proportionality' in Indian administrative law was specifically adopted in:

  1. Om Kumar v. Union of India (2001)
  2. Indian Express Newspapers v. Union of India (1985)
  3. Maneka Gandhi v. Union of India (1978)
  4. Saraswathi v. Union of India (1999)
Show answer and explanation

Answer: A. Om Kumar v. Union of India (2001)

In Om Kumar v. Union of India (2001), the Supreme Court specifically adopted the doctrine of proportionality in Indian administrative law for reviewing punishments imposed on government servants.

14Match the writs with their purpose: (i) Mandamus (ii) Habeas Corpus (iii) Prohibition (iv) Quo Warranto — (a) Release from illegal detention (b) Perform a public duty (c) Prevent inferior tribunal from exceeding jurisdiction (d) Question authority to hold public office

  1. i-b, ii-a, iii-c, iv-d
  2. i-a, ii-b, iii-c, iv-d
  3. i-b, ii-c, iii-a, iv-d
  4. i-d, ii-a, iii-c, iv-b
Show answer and explanation

Answer: A. i-b, ii-a, iii-c, iv-d

Mandamus (b) — compels performance of a public duty. Habeas Corpus (a) — secures release from illegal detention. Prohibition (c) — prevents inferior tribunal from exceeding jurisdiction (preventive). Quo Warranto (d) — questions by what authority a person holds a public office.
