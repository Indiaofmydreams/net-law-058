18 practice questions with answers

1'Promissory Estoppel' against the Government was confirmed in Indian law in which case?

  1. A.K. Kraipak v. Union of India
  2. M/s Motilal Padampat Sugar Mills v. State of UP (1979)
  3. Century Spg. & Mfg. Co. v. Ulhas Nagar Municipality
  4. State of Punjab v. Okara Grain Buyers
Show answer and explanation

Answer: B. M/s Motilal Padampat Sugar Mills v. State of UP (1979)

In Motilal Padampat Sugar Mills v. State of UP (1979), the Supreme Court confirmed that the doctrine of promissory estoppel applies against the Government in India. The State had given an exemption from sales tax and the SC held the Government was estopped from withdrawing it after the company acted on the representation. Concept note: Promissory Estoppel vs Legitimate Expectation: Promissory Estoppel — equitable doctrine; government cannot retract a representation if the party acted on it to its detriment. Legitimate Expectation — public law doctrine; gives right to be heard before expectation is defeated, but not necessarily the promised benefit.

2The 'Doctrine of Pleasure' under Article 310 does NOT apply to:

  1. Officers of All India Services
  2. Members of Defence Services
  3. Constitutional authorities appointed under special provisions
  4. Judges of Supreme Court and High Courts — they hold office 'during good behaviour'
Show answer and explanation

Answer: D. Judges of Supreme Court and High Courts — they hold office 'during good behaviour'

Article 310 establishes the doctrine of pleasure for government servants. However, judges of the SC (Article 124) and HC (Article 217) hold office during 'good behaviour' — they can only be removed by the impeachment process under Articles 124(4) and 217(1)(b)/(3), not at the pleasure of the President.

3In Ram Jawaya Kapur v. State of Punjab (1955), the Supreme Court held that:

  1. India follows strict separation of powers
  2. The executive cannot act without a law
  3. The Indian Constitution does not recognise separation of powers in its absolute rigidity, and executive power is co-extensive with legislative power unless restricted
  4. Textbook nationalisation was unconstitutional
Show answer and explanation

Answer: C. The Indian Constitution does not recognise separation of powers in its absolute rigidity, and executive power is co-extensive with legislative power unless restricted

This is the leading case on the Indian approach to separation of powers.

4The maxim delegatus non potest delegare in administrative law means that a delegate:

  1. Can freely sub-delegate
  2. May delegate to courts
  3. Cannot sub-delegate unless authorised by the parent statute (Ganpati Singhji v. State of Ajmer)
  4. Must be elected
Show answer and explanation

Answer: C. Cannot sub-delegate unless authorised by the parent statute (Ganpati Singhji v. State of Ajmer)

Sub-delegation requires express or necessarily implied authority.

5In Padfield v. Minister of Agriculture, Fisheries and Food (1968), the House of Lords held that:

  1. Ministerial discretion is unfettered
  2. Discretion must be exercised to promote the policy and objects of the Act, and refusal without reason may be quashed
  3. Courts cannot review discretion
  4. Ministers need no reasons
Show answer and explanation

Answer: B. Discretion must be exercised to promote the policy and objects of the Act, and refusal without reason may be quashed

No discretion is truly unfettered.

6In Subramanian Swamy v. Director, CBI (2014), a Constitution Bench of the Supreme Court:

  1. Struck down s.6A of the DSPE Act (prior sanction to investigate officers of Joint Secretary level and above) as violative of Art. 14
  2. Upheld s.6A of the Delhi Special Police Establishment Act as a valid classification
  3. Held the CBI is unconstitutional
  4. Held prior sanction is required for all public servants
Show answer and explanation

Answer: A. Struck down s.6A of the DSPE Act (prior sanction to investigate officers of Joint Secretary level and above) as violative of Art. 14

Section 6A (the 'Single Directive' statutorily revived in 2003) created an unreasonable classification among corruption-accused public servants and was struck down under Art. 14.

7In Om Kumar v. Union of India (2001), the Supreme Court stated that the doctrine of proportionality:

  1. Never applies in India
  2. Applies only to penalties
  3. Replaces Wednesbury in all cases
  4. Applies where fundamental rights are affected (primary review), while Wednesbury unreasonableness applies to ordinary administrative action (secondary review)
Show answer and explanation

Answer: D. Applies where fundamental rights are affected (primary review), while Wednesbury unreasonableness applies to ordinary administrative action (secondary review)

Proportionality is a stricter standard of review.

8In Union of India v. Hindustan Development Corporation (1993), the Supreme Court held that legitimate expectation:

  1. Is an enforceable right in all cases
  2. Is a Fundamental Right
  3. Applies only against private parties
  4. Is not a legal right by itself, but denial of a reasonable expectation arbitrarily may be reviewed; it cannot override public interest
Show answer and explanation

Answer: D. Is not a legal right by itself, but denial of a reasonable expectation arbitrarily may be reviewed; it cannot override public interest

It operates as a ground of review under Art. 14.

9In S.N. Mukherjee v. Union of India (1990), the Supreme Court held that:

  1. Administrative authorities need not give reasons
  2. Reasons are needed only for judicial decisions
  3. Reasons must be recorded for administrative decisions with civil consequences, unless a statute excludes it, including by a confirming authority under the Army Act
  4. Reasons are needed only in service matters
Show answer and explanation

Answer: C. Reasons must be recorded for administrative decisions with civil consequences, unless a statute excludes it, including by a confirming authority under the Army Act

The duty to give reasons is part of natural justice.

10Syed Yakoob v. K.S. Radhakrishnan (1964) held that a writ of certiorari:

  1. Is an appeal on facts
  2. Is available against private bodies in all cases
  3. Can reverse findings of fact freely
  4. Is supervisory and can correct errors of jurisdiction and errors of law apparent on the face of the record, but not errors of fact
Show answer and explanation

Answer: D. Is supervisory and can correct errors of jurisdiction and errors of law apparent on the face of the record, but not errors of fact

Certiorari is not an appellate remedy.

11Which statement distinguishes the writs of prohibition and certiorari?

  1. Prohibition is issued to prevent an inferior body from exceeding jurisdiction while proceedings are pending; certiorari quashes an order already made
  2. Prohibition is issued after the order, certiorari before it
  3. Both are issued against private persons
  4. Both are issued against administrative bodies only
Show answer and explanation

Answer: A. Prohibition is issued to prevent an inferior body from exceeding jurisdiction while proceedings are pending; certiorari quashes an order already made

Both lie only against judicial or quasi-judicial authorities.

12The first reported Public Interest Litigation case in India is generally considered to be:

  1. S.P. Gupta v. Union of India
  2. Hussainara Khatoon v. State of Bihar
  3. Bandhua Mukti Morcha v. Union of India
  4. M.C. Mehta v. Union of India
Show answer and explanation

Answer: B. Hussainara Khatoon v. State of Bihar

It concerned under-trial prisoners and established the right to speedy trial under Art. 21.

13Bhikraj Jaipuria v. Union of India (1962) is associated with Art. 299(1), which requires that contracts made in the exercise of the executive power of the Union or a State be:

  1. Expressed to be made by the President/Governor and executed on their behalf by persons authorised, and these formalities are mandatory
  2. Oral if urgent
  3. Approved by Parliament
  4. Registered under the Contract Act
Show answer and explanation

Answer: A. Expressed to be made by the President/Governor and executed on their behalf by persons authorised, and these formalities are mandatory

Non-compliance makes the contract unenforceable against the Government.

14S.P. Sampath Kumar v. Union of India (1987) upheld the exclusion of High Court jurisdiction by the Administrative Tribunals Act, 1985; this view was later overruled in:

  1. Kesavananda Bharati
  2. L. Chandra Kumar v. Union of India
  3. Indira Sawhney
  4. S.R. Bommai
Show answer and explanation

Answer: B. L. Chandra Kumar v. Union of India

L. Chandra Kumar (1997) held judicial review by High Courts is part of the basic structure.

15In Ram Krishna Dalmia v. Justice S.R. Tendolkar (1958), the Supreme Court held that a Commission of Inquiry under the Commissions of Inquiry Act, 1952:

  1. Can pass binding judgments
  2. Can punish for contempt of Parliament
  3. Is a court of record
  4. Is a fact-finding body whose findings are not binding and have no legal consequence by themselves
Show answer and explanation

Answer: D. Is a fact-finding body whose findings are not binding and have no legal consequence by themselves

The Act was upheld; the Commission only advises.

16In Vineet Narain v. Union of India (1997), the Supreme Court:

  1. Dismissed the petition
  2. Held the CVC cannot supervise the CBI
  3. Abolished the CBI
  4. Gave the CVC statutory status and prescribed a minimum two-year tenure for the CBI Director, and invalidated the 'Single Directive' requiring prior sanction to investigate senior officers
Show answer and explanation

Answer: D. Gave the CVC statutory status and prescribed a minimum two-year tenure for the CBI Director, and invalidated the 'Single Directive' requiring prior sanction to investigate senior officers

The 'Single Directive' was later invoked again as s.6A DSPE Act, struck down in Subramanian Swamy (2014).

17In CPIO, Supreme Court of India v. Subhash Chandra Agarwal (2019), the Constitution Bench held that:

  1. The Office of the Chief Justice of India is outside the RTI Act
  2. The Office of the CJI is a public authority under the RTI Act, and transparency is compatible with judicial independence
  3. RTI does not apply to the judiciary at all
  4. Only the Registry is a public authority
Show answer and explanation

Answer: B. The Office of the CJI is a public authority under the RTI Act, and transparency is compatible with judicial independence

Personal information was subject to the fiduciary and privacy exemptions.

18Under the Lokpal and Lokayuktas Act, 2013, the Lokpal's jurisdiction over the Prime Minister excludes allegations relating to:

  1. Corruption in office
  2. Cabinet decisions
  3. Election funding
  4. International relations, external and internal security, public order, atomic energy and space
Show answer and explanation

Answer: D. International relations, external and internal security, public order, atomic energy and space

Inquiry against the PM needs approval of two-thirds of the full Lokpal bench and is held in camera.
