16 practice questions with answers

1The Trade Marks Act, 1999 came into force in:

  1. 1999
  2. 2000
  3. 2001
  4. 2003
Show answer and explanation

Answer: D. 2003

Though the Trade Marks Act was passed by Parliament in 1999, it came into force on 15 September 2003.

Report this question

2A trademark registration in India is valid for:

  1. 7 years, renewable for 7 years
  2. 10 years, renewable indefinitely
  3. 14 years, renewable once
  4. 20 years, not renewable
Show answer and explanation

Answer: B. 10 years, renewable indefinitely

Under the Trade Marks Act, 1999, a trademark registration is valid for 10 years from the date of application and can be renewed indefinitely for successive periods of 10 years.

Report this question

3An action for 'passing off' can be maintained:

  1. Only in respect of registered trademarks
  2. In respect of both registered and unregistered trademarks
  3. Only by government bodies
  4. Only in respect of unregistered trademarks
Show answer and explanation

Answer: B. In respect of both registered and unregistered trademarks

Passing off is a common law remedy available to protect goodwill/reputation associated with a mark, whether or not the mark is registered. Section 27 of the Trade Marks Act preserves this right.

Report this question

4The three essential elements of an action for passing off are:

  1. Registration, similarity, and damage
  2. Distinctiveness, confusion, and loss of sales
  3. Prior use, registration, and reputation
  4. Goodwill, misrepresentation, and damage
Show answer and explanation

Answer: D. Goodwill, misrepresentation, and damage

The classic trinity for passing off, established in Reckitt & Colman Products Ltd v Borden Inc (1990) (the Jif Lemon case), requires: (1) goodwill/reputation, (2) misrepresentation, and (3) damage.

Report this question

5A 'well-known trademark' under the Trade Marks Act 1999 is defined in Section:

  1. Section 2(zb)
  2. Section 2(zg)
  3. Section 11
  4. Section 29
Show answer and explanation

Answer: B. Section 2(zg)

Section 2(zg) of the Trade Marks Act defines a 'well-known trade mark' as a mark which has become well-known to the substantial segment of the public which uses such goods or receives such services.

Report this question

6Which of the following marks CANNOT be registered as a trademark in India?

  1. An invented word
  2. A name used descriptively to describe the nature of the goods
  3. An arbitrary word having no connection with the goods
  4. A distinctive logo
Show answer and explanation

Answer: B. A name used descriptively to describe the nature of the goods

Section 9(1)(b) of the Trade Marks Act prohibits registration of marks that consist exclusively of marks or indications which may serve in trade to designate the kind, quality, quantity, intended purpose, or other characteristics of the goods.

7In N.R. Dongre v. Whirlpool Corporation (1996), the Supreme Court held:

  1. Transborder reputation of a trademark can be protected in India even without prior use here
  2. A foreign mark cannot be protected in India without registration
  3. Passing off requires registration
  4. Foreign companies cannot file trademark suits in India
Show answer and explanation

Answer: A. Transborder reputation of a trademark can be protected in India even without prior use here

The Supreme Court held that Whirlpool Corporation had acquired trans-border reputation in India for its 'WHIRLPOOL' mark through use in other countries and advertising in international publications available in India, and granted protection against passing off.

Report this question

8'Distinctiveness' in trademark law can be acquired through:

  1. Registration alone
  2. Legislative declaration
  3. Court order
  4. Long and extensive use (secondary meaning)
Show answer and explanation

Answer: D. Long and extensive use (secondary meaning)

A mark that is not inherently distinctive can acquire distinctiveness through long and extensive use in trade, such that the mark has come to identify the goods/services of a particular trader in the minds of the public — this is called 'secondary meaning' or 'acquired distinctiveness'.

9The Madrid System for international registration of trademarks is administered by:

  1. WIPO
  2. WTO
  3. UNCTAD
  4. UN
Show answer and explanation

Answer: A. WIPO

The Madrid System, comprising the Madrid Agreement (1891) and the Madrid Protocol (1989), is administered by WIPO and allows for the international registration of trademarks in multiple countries through a single application.

10A 'certification mark' under the Trade Marks Act differs from a 'collective mark' in that:

  1. They are identical concepts
  2. A collective mark is used by the government
  3. A certification mark guarantees the standard/quality of goods, while collective mark identifies goods of members of an association
  4. A certification mark is unregistrable
Show answer and explanation

Answer: C. A certification mark guarantees the standard/quality of goods, while collective mark identifies goods of members of an association

A certification mark certifies that goods/services meet a particular standard (e.g., AGMARK, ISI), while a collective mark is used by members of a collective organisation (e.g., a trade association) to identify their goods/services.

11'Trade dress' protection in trademark law covers:

  1. Patterns on clothing
  2. Only colour marks
  3. The shape of packaging only
  4. The overall commercial image/appearance of a product
Show answer and explanation

Answer: D. The overall commercial image/appearance of a product

Trade dress refers to the total image and overall appearance of a product, including its size, shape, colour combinations, texture, graphics, and the techniques used to market the product.

Report this question

12Dilution of a trademark refers to:

  1. Physical degradation of the mark
  2. Unauthorised assignment of the mark
  3. Non-renewal of the mark
  4. Reduction in the distinctiveness or reputation of a famous mark without causing confusion
Show answer and explanation

Answer: D. Reduction in the distinctiveness or reputation of a famous mark without causing confusion

Trademark dilution is the weakening of a famous mark's distinctiveness (blurring) or harm to its reputation (tarnishment) through unauthorised use, even without likelihood of consumer confusion.

Report this question

13Yahoo Inc. v. Akash Arora (1999) — Delhi High Court — was India's first major case relating to:

  1. Software patents
  2. Cyber defamation
  3. Domain name dispute / cybersquatting
  4. Copyright in computer programs
Show answer and explanation

Answer: C. Domain name dispute / cybersquatting

Yahoo Inc. v. Akash Arora is considered India's first major domain name dispute. The Delhi High Court granted an injunction restraining the defendant from using 'yahooindia.com', holding that it was deceptively similar to Yahoo's trademark.

Report this question

14'Cybersquatting' refers to:

  1. Hacking of government websites
  2. Sending offensive online messages
  3. Registering domain names of famous trademarks/brands in bad faith to profit from them
  4. Unauthorised copying of software
Show answer and explanation

Answer: C. Registering domain names of famous trademarks/brands in bad faith to profit from them

Cybersquatting involves registering, trafficking in, or using a domain name that is identical or confusingly similar to a trademark belonging to another person/company, with bad faith intent to profit.

Report this question

15The WIPO dispute resolution mechanism for domain names is known as:

  1. WIPO Arbitration Centre
  2. International Court of Cyber Disputes
  3. WIPO Mediation Centre
  4. UDRP — Uniform Domain-Name Dispute-Resolution Policy
Show answer and explanation

Answer: D. UDRP — Uniform Domain-Name Dispute-Resolution Policy

The Uniform Domain-Name Dispute-Resolution Policy (UDRP), administered by WIPO's Arbitration and Mediation Center, provides an international procedure for resolving domain name disputes.

Report this question

16Which of the following correctly distinguishes 'passing off' from 'trademark infringement'?

  1. Passing off requires registration; infringement does not
  2. Infringement requires registration of the mark; passing off is available even for unregistered marks
  3. Both require registration
  4. Neither requires registration
Show answer and explanation

Answer: B. Infringement requires registration of the mark; passing off is available even for unregistered marks

Trademark infringement is an action under Section 29 of the Trade Marks Act available only to owners of registered marks. Passing off is a common law action available irrespective of registration, protecting goodwill and reputation.
