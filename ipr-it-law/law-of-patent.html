23 practice questions with answers

1The Patents Act in India was enacted in:

  1. 1947
  2. 1970
  3. 1999
  4. 2005
Show answer and explanation

Answer: B. 1970

The Patents Act, 1970 replaced the Indian Patents and Designs Act, 1911. It was based on the recommendations of the Ayyangar Committee Report (1959).

Report this question

2The Ayyangar Committee Report (1959) recommended:

  1. Full product patent protection for all inventions
  2. Adoption of TRIPS standards in advance
  3. Abolition of product patents in certain sectors
  4. Introduction of utility models
Show answer and explanation

Answer: C. Abolition of product patents in certain sectors

Justice N. Rajagopala Ayyangar's report recommended, among other things, the abolition of product patents in the food and pharmaceutical sectors, allowing only process patents — a key public health safeguard.

Report this question

3Under the Patents Act 1970, the term of patent protection is:

  1. 14 years
  2. 15 years
  3. 20 years
  4. 25 years
Show answer and explanation

Answer: C. 20 years

After the 2002 amendment (to comply with TRIPS Article 33), the term of every patent in India is 20 years from the date of filing of the patent application.

Report this question

4Section 3(d) of the Patents Act 1970 (as amended) is a provision against:

  1. Evergreening of pharmaceutical patents
  2. Software patents
  3. Biopiracy
  4. Compulsory licensing
Show answer and explanation

Answer: A. Evergreening of pharmaceutical patents

Section 3(d) prohibits grant of patents for new forms of known substances unless the applicant demonstrates significantly enhanced efficacy. This prevents 'evergreening' — the practice of seeking new patents on minor modifications to extend monopoly protection.

Report this question

5In Novartis AG v. Union of India (2013), the Supreme Court:

  1. Upheld Section 3(d) and refused patent to Gleevec (Imatinib)
  2. Struck down Section 3(d) as unconstitutional
  3. Granted product patent to Novartis
  4. Ordered compulsory licensing for Gleevec
Show answer and explanation

Answer: A. Upheld Section 3(d) and refused patent to Gleevec (Imatinib)

The Supreme Court upheld the constitutional validity of Section 3(d) and held that Novartis had not demonstrated enhanced therapeutic efficacy for the beta-crystalline form of Imatinib Mesylate (Gleevec), refusing the patent.

Report this question

6Which of the following is NOT patentable under Section 3 of the Patents Act 1970?

  1. A mathematical method
  2. A new drug molecule
  3. A new manufacturing process
  4. A biotechnology invention
Show answer and explanation

Answer: A. A mathematical method

Section 3(k) excludes from patentability mathematical or business methods, computer programmes per se, and algorithms.

Report this question

7Compulsory licensing under Section 84 of the Patents Act can be applied for after:

  1. 1 year from grant of patent
  2. 2 years from grant of patent
  3. 3 years from grant of patent
  4. 5 years from grant of patent
Show answer and explanation

Answer: C. 3 years from grant of patent

Section 84(1) allows any person interested to apply to the Controller for grant of compulsory licence after 3 years from the date of the grant of a patent.

Report this question

8India's first compulsory licence was granted in 2012 to Natco Pharma for the drug:

  1. Gleevec (Imatinib)
  2. Herceptin (Trastuzumab)
  3. Nexavar (Sorafenib)
  4. Avastin (Bevacizumab)
Show answer and explanation

Answer: C. Nexavar (Sorafenib)

In March 2012, the Patent Controller granted India's first compulsory licence to Natco Pharma for the manufacture and sale of Sorafenib Tosylate (marketed by Bayer as Nexavar), a kidney cancer drug.

Report this question

9The three criteria for patentability are:

  1. Novelty, utility, disclosure
  2. Novelty, inventive step, industrial application
  3. Originality, novelty, non-obviousness
  4. Novelty, creativity, commercial value
Show answer and explanation

Answer: B. Novelty, inventive step, industrial application

Section 2(1)(j) defines an 'invention' as a new product or process involving an inventive step (non-obviousness) and capable of industrial application — the three pillars of patentability.

Report this question

10Under which section of the Patents Act can a patent be revoked?

  1. Section 64
  2. Section 25
  3. Section 84
  4. Section 48
Show answer and explanation

Answer: A. Section 64

Section 64 lists the grounds on which a patent may be revoked by the High Court (or the Controller on a counter-claim in an infringement suit).

Report this question

11The amendment which brought India's patent law in full compliance with TRIPS by introducing product patents for pharmaceuticals was made in:

  1. 1999
  2. 2002
  3. 2005
  4. 2008
Show answer and explanation

Answer: C. 2005

The Patents (Amendment) Act, 2005 introduced product patents for food, drugs, chemicals, and micro-organisms, completing India's TRIPS compliance obligations.

Report this question

12Under the Patents Act 1970, a patent application is published after:

  1. 18 months from filing
  2. 12 months from filing
  3. 24 months from filing
  4. Immediately on filing
Show answer and explanation

Answer: A. 18 months from filing

Section 11A of the Patents Act provides that an application for a patent shall be published after 18 months from the date of filing or the date of priority, whichever is earlier.

Report this question

13Diamond v. Chakrabarty (1980) US Supreme Court case held that:

  1. Living organisms can be patentable subject matter
  2. Mathematical algorithms are patentable
  3. Software is patentable
  4. Methods of treatment are patentable
Show answer and explanation

Answer: A. Living organisms can be patentable subject matter

The US Supreme Court in Diamond v. Chakrabarty held that a live, human-made microorganism is patentable subject matter under US patent law, establishing that living organisms produced through human ingenuity can be patented.

Report this question

14Under the Patents Act 1970, which section deals with the rights of a patentee?

  1. Section 3
  2. Section 48
  3. Section 64
  4. Section 84
Show answer and explanation

Answer: B. Section 48

Section 48 of the Patents Act grants the patentee the exclusive right to prevent third parties from making, using, offering for sale, selling, or importing the patented product or process.

Report this question

15In Bayer Corporation v. Union of India (2014), the Supreme Court upheld India's first compulsory licence for Nexavar on the ground that:

  1. The drug lacked inventive step
  2. Bayer had not filed for patent in India
  3. The drug violated Section 3(d)
  4. The reasonable requirements of the public were not satisfied and the drug was not available at a reasonably affordable price
Show answer and explanation

Answer: D. The reasonable requirements of the public were not satisfied and the drug was not available at a reasonably affordable price

The Supreme Court upheld the compulsory licence granted to Natco Pharma finding that Bayer failed to work the patent in India, the drug was not available to the public at a reasonably affordable price, and public requirements were not being met.

Report this question

16Which of the following is the ground for pre-grant opposition under Section 25(1) of the Patents Act?

  1. Non-working of the patent
  2. The patentee has not paid renewal fees
  3. The patentee has assigned the patent
  4. The invention was publicly known or used in India before the priority date
Show answer and explanation

Answer: D. The invention was publicly known or used in India before the priority date

Section 25(1) allows any person to oppose grant of a patent on specified grounds including that the invention was publicly known, publicly used, or published in India before the priority date.

Report this question

17A 'patent of addition' under the Patents Act 1970 relates to:

  1. An improvement or modification of an already patented invention
  2. A second patent on the same invention
  3. An additional inventor added to the patent
  4. Extension of patent term
Show answer and explanation

Answer: A. An improvement or modification of an already patented invention

Section 54 of the Patents Act provides for a patent of addition for an improvement in or modification of a patented invention, which subsists for the remaining term of the main patent.

Report this question

18Traditional Knowledge Digital Library (TKDL) in India was created primarily to:

  1. Prevent biopiracy by serving as prior art to defeat patent claims
  2. Register traditional knowledge as a patent
  3. Protect traditional knowledge as a trademark
  4. Commercialise traditional knowledge
Show answer and explanation

Answer: A. Prevent biopiracy by serving as prior art to defeat patent claims

TKDL was established as a defensive tool. By documenting traditional knowledge (e.g., medicinal uses of plants) in a structured database accessible to international patent offices, it prevents grant of patents on traditional knowledge by establishing prior art.

Report this question

19Atomic energy inventions are excluded from patentability in India under:

  1. Section 3(b)
  2. Section 3(d)
  3. Section 3(p)
  4. Section 4
Show answer and explanation

Answer: D. Section 4

Section 4 of the Patents Act provides that no patent shall be granted in respect of an invention relating to atomic energy falling within sub-section (1) of Section 20 of the Atomic Energy Act, 1962.

Report this question

20Section 3(k) of the Patents Act, 1970 excludes ________ from patentability:

  1. Biotechnology inventions
  2. Process claims for pharmaceutical inventions
  3. Methods of treatment of human beings
  4. A mathematical or business method or a computer programme per se or algorithms
Show answer and explanation

Answer: D. A mathematical or business method or a computer programme per se or algorithms

Section 3(k) of the Patents Act specifically excludes mathematical or business methods, computer programmes per se, and algorithms from patentable subject matter.

Report this question

21India successfully challenged the US patent on 'turmeric' for wound healing on the ground that it was:

  1. Lacking inventive step
  2. Not capable of industrial application
  3. Not novel — constituted prior art published in ancient Indian texts
  4. Covered by Biological Diversity Act
Show answer and explanation

Answer: C. Not novel — constituted prior art published in ancient Indian texts

India's CSIR challenged the US patent on turmeric's wound-healing properties before the USPTO, successfully arguing that the use was well-documented in ancient Indian texts like Ayurvedic treatises, constituting prior art that negated novelty.

Report this question

22The 'Doctrine of Equivalents' in patent law was applied in the US case of:

  1. Diamond v. Chakrabarty
  2. Monsanto v. Schmeiser
  3. Bilski v. Kappos
  4. Graver Tank v. Linde Air Products
Show answer and explanation

Answer: D. Graver Tank v. Linde Air Products

The US Supreme Court in Graver Tank & Mfg. Co. v. Linde Air Products Co. (1950) is the seminal case establishing the doctrine of equivalents — a patent claim is infringed if a competing product or process performs substantially the same function in substantially the same way to achieve the same result.

23The Traditional Knowledge Digital Library (TKDL) in India was established jointly by:

  1. WIPO and Government of India
  2. CGPDTM and Ministry of Law
  3. NBA and Ministry of Environment
  4. CSIR and Ministry of AYUSH
Show answer and explanation

Answer: D. CSIR and Ministry of AYUSH

TKDL was established jointly by the Council of Scientific and Industrial Research (CSIR) and the Department of AYUSH (Ayurveda, Yoga, Unani, Siddha, and Homeopathy), Ministry of Health.
