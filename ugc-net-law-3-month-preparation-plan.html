You have 3 months. Here is how I would approach it.

Three months is enough, if you start with a plan rather than a pile of books.

You cannot be leisurely, but you do not need to panic. The syllabus can be covered in about two months if you move steadily, which leaves a full month for what decides most results: revision, past questions and mock tests.

So I would not separate studying from testing at all. Read a topic in the notes, then attempt its MCQs straight away. The first attempt will often feel uncomfortable, and that is useful: effortful retrieval of something you have only half learned tends to help it stay.9,2 Then read the explanations, fix the gap, and attempt again.

Study → Attempt MCQs → Understand mistakes → Revisit the concept → Attempt again

Do Paper 1 alongside Law, in small daily slots. It rewards steady exposure far more than a last-minute rush.

You don’t need another hundred sources. You need to use the resources you already have properly.

1What your priority should be

  • Cover the syllabus in about two monthsMove at a steady pace. Depth comes from the cycle, not from a slow first read.
  • Notes and MCQs together, from day oneEvery topic is followed by its questions. MCQs are how you study, not an extra.
  • Build a weak-unit list earlyBy the end of month 1 you should know where you are losing marks.
  • Keep the last month for revision and mocksThis is where retrieval becomes reliable.

  • Mon–FriOne unit’s notes, then its MCQs the same day. Paper 1 in a small daily slot.
  • SaturdayPYQs, plus a re-attempt of the week’s mistakes.
  • SundayRead your error list and revisit the weakest unit. Keep it light.

Don’t do this

  • Don’t collect endless study material.
  • Don’t postpone MCQs until you finish the syllabus.
  • Don’t repeatedly read notes without testing recall.
  • Don’t take mocks without analysing your mistakes.
  • Don’t completely change your resources in the final weeks.
  • Don’t try to make every unit perfect on the first pass.
  • Don’t spend week 1 hunting for the ‘best’ sources.

THE EVIDENCEWhy MCQs sit at the centre of this plan

Bjork’s idea of “desirable difficulties” is that conditions which make learning feel harder, such as testing yourself instead of re-reading, can improve long-term retention.9 Roediger and Karpicke (2006) found that students who took tests on a passage remembered more after a week than students who studied it again.2

For multiple-choice questions specifically, Butler and Roediger (2008) found that testing helped later performance and that feedback reduced the chance of remembering wrong options.5 That is why I keep asking you to read the explanations, including for questions you got right. Cepeda and colleagues’ work on spacing is why the plan returns to weak units a few days later rather than the same evening.7

What this does and doesn’t show. These studies are mostly laboratory and classroom experiments on memory. None of them tested a UGC NET strategy, and none can promise a result in the exam. This pathway is informed by evidence on retrieval practice, practice testing and distributed learning. It is guidance, not a guarantee.

IF YOU NEED TO SETTLE A DOUBTStandard reference works

These are well-known texts I would consult when a note or an explanation leaves a doubt. They are not a new reading list. Use them to resolve a question, then go back to the cycle.

  • CONSTITUTIONAL LAWM. P. Jain, Indian Constitutional Law; D. D. Basu, Introduction to the Constitution of India; Granville Austin, The Indian Constitution: Cornerstone of a Nation (1966).
  • JURISPRUDENCESalmond on Jurisprudence; H. L. A. Hart, The Concept of Law (1961); R. W. M. Dias, Jurisprudence.
  • PUBLIC INTERNATIONAL LAWIan Brownlie, Principles of Public International Law; Malcolm N. Shaw, International Law.
  • CRIMINAL LAWK. D. Gaur’s textbook on criminal law. Check that your edition reflects the Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita, 2023.
  • CONTRACT AND TORTSAvtar Singh, Law of Contract and Specific Relief; Ratanlal & Dhirajlal, The Law of Torts.
  • FAMILY LAWParas Diwan, Modern Hindu Law; Mulla’s Principles of Mahomedan Law.
  • ENVIRONMENT AND IPRP. Leelakrishnan, Environmental Law in India; Cornish, Llewelyn & Aplin, Intellectual Property.
  • PAPER 1C. R. Kothari, Research Methodology; R. S. Aggarwal, A Modern Approach to Verbal & Non-Verbal Reasoning.
