Industrial Relations Code, 2020 (II): Standing Orders, Dispute Resolution Machinery, Strikes and Lock-outs

iOverview

How disputes are raised and resolved (conciliation, tribunals, arbitration), the 2020 changes (direct access to Tribunal, two-member Tribunal) and the strike/lock-out notice rules which now cover every industrial establishment.

★Must know

  1. Standing Orders (IR Code Chapter IV; replaces Industrial Employment (Standing Orders) Act 1946): apply to every industrial establishment with 300 or more workers (earlier 100); the employer must submit draft standing orders; model standing orders are prescribed by the Central Government (Model Standing Orders for manufacturing sector, and Model Standing Orders notified in 2026 Verify); certification by the Certifying Officer; deemed certification if no objection is raised within a prescribed time (about 60 days) Verify; appeal to the appellate authority; modifications only by agreement or by certification; matters provided in standing orders include classification of workers, manner of intimating working hours, holidays, pay-days, shift working, attendance, leave, conditions of discharge, termination, disciplinary action for misconduct, grievance redressal. Standing orders have statutory force (Western India Match Co. v. Workmen, 1973; U.P. State Road Transport Corp. v. Mohan Lal Gupta, 2000). Where standing orders do not exist, model standing orders apply.
  2. Notice of change (Chapter V; ID Act s.9A): an employer who proposes to effect any change in conditions of service specified in the Schedule must give a 21-day notice to the workers affected; exceptions where change is under a settlement or award.
  3. Dispute resolution machinery: Conciliation Officers appointed by the appropriate Government; the Industrial Tribunal (replacing Labour Courts and Tribunals of the ID Act) with two Members - one Judicial Member and one Administrative Member - and the National Industrial Tribunal for questions of national importance or interest to establishments in more than one State; Voluntary arbitration (Chapter VI; ID Act s.10A) by agreement of the parties to refer the dispute to arbitrator(s) before it is referred to Tribunal.
  4. Raising a dispute (IR Code 2020): notice to the other party and to the conciliation officer; conciliation to be completed within a prescribed period (60 days); settlement in the course of conciliation is binding on the parties; if conciliation fails, the conciliation officer submits a failure report and parties may approach the Tribunal; under the Code, the party may approach the Tribunal directly after conciliation failure (no discretion of Government to make a 'reference' as under ID Act s.10) Verify. Individual disputes (discharge, dismissal, retrenchment, termination): a worker may apply to the conciliation officer and, if not settled within 45 days, to the Tribunal within two years from the date of discharge, dismissal, retrenchment or termination Verify. Tribunal awards: binding on the parties; enforcement as a decree; Tribunal's power to grant interim relief; powers of a civil court.
  5. Section 11A ID Act (Tribunal's power to give appropriate relief in case of discharge or dismissal): where the Tribunal finds the punishment disproportionate, it may substitute a lesser punishment; Workmen of Firestone Tyre v. Management (1973); Delhi Transport Corporation v. D.T.C. Mazdoor Congress (1991) - 'hire and fire' rule; the employer must prove misconduct by fair domestic enquiry; if enquiry defective, employer may lead evidence before the Tribunal (Shankar Chakravarti v. Britannia Biscuit, 1979). Reinstatement with back wages is the general rule for illegal termination (Deepali Gundu Surwase v. Kranti Junior Adhyapak Mahavidyalaya, 2013); compensation in lieu is permitted in appropriate cases; the 'long delay/no back wages' approach in Allahabad Jal Sansthan v. Daya Shankar Rai (2005).
  6. Strike and lock-out (IR Code Chapter VIII; ID Act ss.22-24): 'strike' - cessation of work by a body of persons employed in any industry acting in combination, or a concerted refusal or refusal under a common understanding to continue to work or accept employment; the Code expressly includes 'concerted casual leave on a given day by fifty per cent or more of the workers' as a strike. 'Lock-out' - temporary closing of a place of employment or suspension of work or refusal by an employer to continue to employ persons. Under the Code no person employed in an industrial establishment (not only a public utility service as under the ID Act) shall go on strike in breach of contract: (a) without giving notice of strike to the employer within sixty days before striking; (b) within fourteen days of giving such notice; (c) before the expiry of the date of strike specified in the notice; (d) during the pendency of conciliation proceedings before a conciliation officer and seven days after the conclusion; (e) during the pendency of proceedings before a Tribunal or National Tribunal and sixty days after the conclusion; (f) during the pendency of arbitration proceedings and sixty days after the conclusion; (g) during any period in which a settlement or award is in operation in respect of any of the matters covered. The same restrictions apply to lock-outs by employers (s.62) Verify · section number.
  7. Legal and illegal strikes: illegal if in contravention of s.62; strike justified where employer's conduct is provocative; wages for strike period are denied unless strike is legal and justified (Crompton Greaves v. Workmen, 1978; Bank of India v. T.S. Kelawala, 1990). Lock-out must be bona fide. Participation in an illegal strike and incitement to strike are penalised. T.K. Rangarajan: government employees and others have no fundamental/statutory right to strike. Right to strike is a statutory right at best (Syndicate Bank v. K. Ramanujam, 1973; Ex-Capt. Harish Uppal v. Union of India, 2002 - lawyers strikes illegal).
  8. Public utility services: ID Act s.2(n) list (e.g., railways, postal, telecom, power, water, sanitation, hospitals) and the Code's extension of notice to all; Essential Services Maintenance Acts (ESMA) of Central and State governments; Essential Services provisions; wage during strike. Compensation for lock-out/ strike in public utility.
  9. Settlements and awards: settlement arrived at in conciliation binds all parties and workers; bipartite settlement (outside conciliation) binds signatories only; arbitration award; Tribunal award - enforceable by the Government; persons bound by award; commencement and period of operation (usually one year) ID Act s.19.
  10. Powers of the appropriate Government: refer disputes (pre-Code); prohibit continuance of strike; give directions; prescribe thresholds by notification; exemption from provisions in public interest; appoint conciliation officers; set up Tribunals; Tribunals under the Code have the power of civil courts, can compound offences, and may grant relief to the worker for wrongful termination.

§Key provisions

ProvisionWhat it says

⚖Landmark cases

Workmen of Firestone Tyre & Rubber Co. v. Management

1973

Tribunal's power to examine misconduct and punishment; domestic enquiry principles.

Shankar Chakravarti v. Britannia Biscuit Co.

1979

If enquiry is vitiated, employer may adduce evidence before Tribunal; must plead and apply.

Delhi Transport Corp. v. D.T.C. Mazdoor Congress

1991

Struck down regulation 9(b) of DTC Regulations (termination without reasons) as arbitrary.

Deepali Gundu Surwase v. Kranti Junior Adhyapak Mahavidyalaya

2013

Full back wages and reinstatement is the normal rule after illegal termination.

Bank of India v. T.S. Kelawala

1990

Wages for strike period; legality and justification of strike.

T.K. Rangarajan v. Government of Tamil Nadu

2003

No fundamental right to strike.

Ex-Capt. Harish Uppal v. Union of India

2002

Strike by lawyers is illegal; reaffirmed that right to strike is not absolute.

Western India Match Co. v. Workmen

1973

Standing orders have statutory force.

✎Memory aids

  • Strike notice: '60 days before; not within 14 days; none during conciliation (+7), tribunal (+60), arbitration (+60), or while award/settlement operates'.

!Exam traps

  • TrapNotice for strikes now applies to all industrial establishments, not only public utility services.
  • TrapConcerted casual leave by 50% or more workers is treated as a strike.
  • TrapA settlement in conciliation binds all workers; a bipartite settlement binds only signatories.
  • TrapStanding orders threshold 300 (not 100).

?Likely questions

  1. How many days' notice must be given before a strike? (60)
  2. Standing orders apply to establishments with how many workers? (300 or more)
  3. Which section of the ID Act empowers the Tribunal to interfere with punishment? (s.11A)
  4. What is 'concerted casual leave'? (Treated as strike if by 50%+ workers)
