Joint and Constructive Liability: Common Intention (s.3(5)) and Unlawful Assembly / Common Object (s.190)

High yield1–2 Qs expected4 min readBNS Ss. 3(5), 189-191 (IPC Ss. 34, 141, 149, 146-148)

iOverview

Section 3(5) BNS (IPC 34) lays down a rule of evidence and joint liability

★Must know

  1. Section 3(5) BNS (IPC 34) lays down a rule of evidence and joint liability: when a criminal act is done by several persons in furtherance of the common intention of all, each is liable as if he alone did it.
  2. Ingredients: (1) criminal act by several persons; (2) common intention of all; (3) participation by each in the act. Common intention requires a PRE-ARRANGED PLAN and prior meeting of minds, but it may be formed on the spot (Pandurang v State of Hyderabad, 1955).
  3. Section 3(5) creates no substantive offence; it only attributes liability. Section 149 (BNS 190) also creates constructive liability but is more extensive in scope.
  4. Mahbub Shah v Emperor (1945, Privy Council): common intention implies a pre-arranged plan; mere presence is insufficient.
  5. Barendra Kumar Ghosh v Emperor (1925): the Privy Council held that a person who merely stood guard ('they also serve who only stand and wait') shares the common intention and is liable for the act of the principal offender.
  6. Common object (s.190, IPC 149): if an offence is committed by any member of an unlawful assembly in prosecution of the common object, every member is liable if the offence was likely to be committed in prosecution of the common object (BNS wording tracks IPC).
  7. Unlawful assembly (s.189): five or more persons with a common object of any listed kind: overawing the government/public servant by criminal force, resisting execution of law, committing mischief or criminal trespass, obtaining possession of property by criminal force, or compelling a person to do what he is not legally bound to do.
  8. Rioting (s.191): force or violence used by an unlawful assembly or a member of it in prosecution of the common object. Rioting armed with a deadly weapon is a graver offence.
  9. Distinctions: s.3(5) needs common INTENTION; s.190 needs common OBJECT. s.3(5) requires active participation; s.190 requires only membership. s.3(5) applies to any number of persons (two or more); s.190 needs five or more. s.3(5) is about a criminal act; s.190 about an offence committed by any member.
  10. Mere membership is not enough for s.190 if the member joined without sharing the object; innocent bystanders and passers-by are not liable (Masalti v State of UP, 1965). Where a large crowd is named but only a few are proved to be actively involved, courts require that at least five be proved members.
  11. Related new offences: s.196 (promoting enmity between groups; IPC 153A), s.197 (imputations prejudicial to national integration; IPC 153B), s.192 (wantonly giving provocation with intent to cause riot; IPC 153), s.194 (affray, IPC 159-160).

⚖Landmark cases

Barendra Kumar Ghosh v King Emperor

1925

Participation need not be in the commission of the act; standing and waiting (sentry) is participation.

Mahbub Shah v King-Emperor

1945

Common intention needs a prior meeting of minds; distinct from similar intention.

Pandurang v State of Hyderabad

1955

Common intention may develop at the spot but must precede the act; prior concert shown by circumstances.

Krishna Govind Patil v State of Maharashtra

1964

Section 34 applies only if common intention shown; where doubts exist, benefit goes to the accused.

Masalti v State of UP

1965

Where a large number of persons are accused, conviction under s.149 requires at least five proved to be members; the court should be cautious.

Lalji v State of UP

1989

Common object: liability under s.149 attaches to all members for offences likely in prosecution of the object; membership is a matter of fact.

!Exam traps

  • TrapSection 3(5)/34 = common intention (any number from two); Section 190/149 = common object (five or more).
  • TrapMere presence at the scene does not make one liable under s.34, but in s.149 a member continues to be liable even if he did not actually commit the offence.
  • TrapSection 34 does not create a separate offence; s.149 does not either, but s.189/191 (unlawful assembly/rioting) do.
