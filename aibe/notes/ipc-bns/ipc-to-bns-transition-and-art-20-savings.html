Transition from IPC to BNS: Temporal Application, Savings and Constitutional Limits

iOverview

Three new codes replaced the colonial criminal law with effect from 1 July 2024

★Must know

  1. Three new codes replaced the colonial criminal law with effect from 1 July 2024: Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita, 2023 (BNS) replaced the Indian Penal Code, 1860; Bharatiya Nagarik Suraksha Sanhita, 2023 (BNSS) replaced the Code of Criminal Procedure, 1973; Bharatiya Sakshya Adhiniyam, 2023 (BSA) replaced the Indian Evidence Act, 1872. Each received Presidential assent on 25 December 2023.
  2. Rule of temporal application: BNS applies to offences committed on or after 1 July 2024. Offences committed before that date continue to be governed by the IPC, even if the FIR is registered, or the trial takes place, after that date. This follows Article 20(1) of the Constitution: no person shall be convicted of any offence except for violation of a law in force at the time of the commission of the act, nor subjected to a penalty greater than that which might have been inflicted under the law in force at the time.
  3. Article 20(1) bars ex post facto CRIMINAL LAWS and enhanced penalties; it does not bar retrospective procedural changes. The beneficial-provision principle: where the new law reduces the punishment, the accused may claim the benefit of the lighter punishment (T. Barai v Henry Ah Hoe, 1983; Rattan Lal v State of Punjab, 1964: benefit of ameliorative provisions).
  4. Procedure for pending matters: BNSS s.531 (repeal and savings) provides that pending appeals, applications, trials, inquiries and investigations under the CrPC continue under the CrPC as if it had not been repealed. A trial that began before 1 July 2024 continues under the CrPC; a fresh FIR for an offence committed before that date is registered under the BNSS procedure but the substantive offence is the IPC offence.
  5. Continuing offences and offences spanning the cut-off date: where the commission of the offence began before 1 July 2024 and continued after, courts look at the date on which the offence is complete; for a continuing offence, the law in force when the conduct continues applies to the post-cut-off conduct. The Supreme Court and High Courts have stressed that the FIR must correctly invoke the Code in force on the date of the alleged offence; invoking the wrong Code is generally a curable defect unless it causes prejudice Verify · the latest judgments.
  6. BNS has 358 sections in 20 chapters (IPC: 511 sections in 23 chapters). Official summaries speak of about 20 new offences and about 19 repealed provisions; remember the broad picture rather than exact counts Verify.
  7. Savings clause (BNS s.358): repeals the IPC but preserves the effect of the repealed Code on offences committed before commencement, and any notification, rules or orders made under the IPC are saved to the extent consistent with the BNS.
  8. Special and local laws: the general provisions of the Code (definitions, general exceptions, abetment and so on) apply to offences under special and local laws unless excluded, and the Code does not affect special or local laws (IPC s.5 saving, carried into the BNS preliminary provisions) Verify · exact BNS section.
  9. Territorial scope: the Code operates over every person for acts or omissions within India; for citizens and for ships/aircraft registered in India; for offences by any person targeting a computer resource in India. Section 1(5): the BNS applies to any offence committed by a citizen of India outside India, to any person on a ship or aircraft registered in India wherever it may be, to any person outside India who commits an offence targeting a computer resource located in India. Verify · the exact wording of s.1(5) in the bare act.
  10. Explanation of s.1: the word 'offence' includes every act committed outside India which, if committed in India, would be punishable under the Code. Extradition and trial in India: BNSS s.208 (CrPC 188) requires sanction of the Central Government for inquiry or trial of an offence committed abroad by a citizen.
  11. Persons exempt from the operation of the Code: the President and Governors (Art. 361: no criminal proceedings during term of office), foreign sovereigns and ambassadors (diplomatic immunity under the Diplomatic Relations (Vienna Convention) Act, 1972), foreign armies with consent, and Members of Parliament/Legislatures for privileged statements (Arts. 105, 194).
  12. Hot exam point: the new codes dropped the word 'Indian' in favour of 'Bharatiya', and the substance of many IPC provisions was carried verbatim. Where the exam asks 'under which Code would X be tried', first check the date of the offence.
  13. Offences under special laws (NDPS, PMLA, UAPA, POCSO, IT Act, PC Act) are not affected by the repeal of the IPC, except where the special law cross-refers to IPC sections (those references are read as references to the BNS equivalents: General Clauses Act s.8 and BNS s.358 construction clause). Example: POCSO and the Juvenile Justice Act contain IPC cross-references now read as BNS references.

⚖Landmark cases

Rattan Lal v State of Punjab

1964

Ameliorative legislation (reducing punishment or giving probation) can be applied retrospectively to the accused's benefit; Art. 20(1) protects only against harsher penalties.

T. Barai v Henry Ah Hoe

1983

A reduction of punishment by a later law operates for the benefit of the accused where the prosecution is still pending.

Soni Devrajbhai Babubhai v State of Gujarat

1991

Art. 20(1) forbids enhanced sentence by a later law; the maximum is that applicable on the date of the offence.

Ravinder Singh v State of Haryana

1975

Penalty in force at the time of the offence governs, even if the law subsequently changed.

Maru Ram v Union of India

1981

Constitutional validity of retrospective remission restrictions: Art. 20(1) read with Art. 21; the benefit of the then-existing remission regime.

!Exam traps

  • TrapA murder committed on 30 June 2024 is tried under IPC 302 even if the trial happens in 2026; one on 1 July 2024 is tried under BNS 103.
  • TrapArt. 20(1) bars retrospective criminal laws and enhanced punishment; it does not bar retrospective ameliorative provisions.
  • TrapPending proceedings under CrPC continue under CrPC by virtue of BNSS s.531.
