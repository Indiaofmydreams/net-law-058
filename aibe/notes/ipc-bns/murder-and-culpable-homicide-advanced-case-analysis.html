Murder, Culpable Homicide and Allied Offences: Advanced Doctrines and Case Analysis

High yield2 Qs expected7 min readBNS Ss. 100-106 (IPC Ss. 299-304, 304A)

iOverview

Chart for answering problems: (1) Was there an act causing death? (2) Was there intention or knowledge as in s.100? If no, consider s.106 (negligence) or accident.

★Must know

  1. Chart for answering problems: (1) Was there an act causing death? (2) Was there intention or knowledge as in s.100? If no, consider s.106 (negligence) or accident. (3) If yes, does any of the four clauses of s.101 apply? If no, culpable homicide not amounting to murder. (4) If yes, does any Exception 1-5 apply? If yes, culpable homicide; Part I or Part II punishment depends on intention/knowledge.
  2. Section 100 Explanations: (1) causing the death of a person suffering from a disorder, disease or bodily infirmity, and thereby accelerating death, is culpable homicide; (2) where death is caused by bodily injury, the person causing the injury is deemed to have caused the death although proper remedies or skilful treatment might have prevented death; (3) causing the death of a child in the mother's womb is not homicide, but causing the death of a living child, any part of which has been brought forth, may amount to homicide even though the child has not breathed or been completely born.
  3. Clause (b) of s.101 (IPC 300 'secondly'): intention to cause bodily injury which the offender knows is likely to cause the death of the particular person: requires subjective knowledge about the victim's condition (e.g., blow on the head of a known weak person). Clause (c) (thirdly): objective test of 'sufficient in the ordinary course of nature to cause death': Virsa Singh; Rajwant Singh v State of Kerala (1966): the injury need not be intended to cause death; intended injury sufficient is enough. Clause (d) (fourthly): act imminently dangerous (firing in a crowd, shooting at random); no intention to harm particular person; knowledge that it must in all probability cause death
  4. Exception 1 (grave and sudden provocation): conditions - (i) the provocation must be grave and sudden; (ii) it must deprive the offender of self-control; (iii) death must be of the person who gave the provocation or by accident or mistake of another; the provocation must not be sought or voluntarily provoked; not given by anything done in obedience to law or by a public servant in the lawful exercise of powers or in the lawful exercise of private defence. Cases: Nanavati; Ghapoo Yadav v State of MP (2003); Budhi Singh v State of HP (2012): words and gestures may amount to provocation depending on the person's standing; the Court considers the test of a reasonable man of the same class; Mancini v DPP is the English parallel. Confession of marital infidelity: courts have been reluctant when there is a time lapse; a plea of provocation is lost where there is cooling time.
  5. Exception 2 (exceeding the right of private defence): act done in good faith in exercise of private defence, exceeds the power given by law, causes death without premeditation and without any intention of doing more harm than necessary. The Exception sits between acquittal (full private defence) and murder: Partap v State of UP (1976). Courts have cautioned that the accused who initiates the attack cannot claim this exception.
  6. Exception 3 (public servant or aiding public servant): the act is done in the advancement of public justice, in good faith, exceeding the powers given by law, without ill-will; e.g., a policeman exceeding the law in making an arrest. Exception 4 (sudden fight): conditions - sudden fight, without premeditation, in the heat of passion upon a sudden quarrel, and the offender has not taken undue advantage or acted in a cruel or unusual manner: Surinder Kumar v Union Territory, Chandigarh (1989); Gurmukh Singh v State of Haryana (2009); the court looks at who started the fight, the weapon, the number of blows. Exception 5 (consent): the person killed is above 18 and suffers death or takes the risk with his own consent (e.g., a duel; euthanasia attempts historically): consent must be free and informed; passive euthanasia under Aruna Shanbaug (2011) and Common Cause v UoI (2018) is permitted for terminally ill patients, with advance directives guided by the Court's procedure, modified in January 2023 for simplification.
  7. Culpable homicide not amounting to murder: Part I of s.105 (IPC 304 first part): act done with the intention of causing death or such bodily injury as is likely to cause death (but covered by an exception) - life or up to 10 years and fine; Part II: act done with knowledge that it is likely to cause death but without intention - up to 10 years or fine or both. Sentence is determined by the nature of the weapon, circumstances, and whether the accused took advantage.
  8. Thin dividing lines in cases: Reg v Govinda (1876) - husband kicked wife, causing a bleeding; no intention to cause death; held culpable homicide. Pulicherla Nagaraju v State of AP (2006): factors - nature of the weapon, parts of the body, force used, the circumstances, premeditation, the number of blows. Rampal Singh v State of UP (2012): 'intention to cause death' vs 'knowledge'; the accused who aimed a gun at vital parts is guilty of murder.
  9. Dowry death vs murder: dowry death is a separate offence requiring proof of cruelty or harassment for dowry soon before death within 7 years of marriage, with the burden of proof shifting to the accused through the statutory presumption (BSA s.118); the same facts can attract s.80 (dowry death), s.85 (cruelty), s.108 (abetment of suicide) and s.103 (murder) in the alternative. The Supreme Court has held that, if the death is homicidal, it is not 'dowry death' as 'otherwise than under normal circumstances' covers both; the court may convict under s.304B even if murder is not proved (Kans Raj; Hira Lal).
  10. Negligence offences: death by rash or negligent act (s.106(1)); medical negligence: Jacob Mathew prescribes the Bolam test with a higher threshold for criminal negligence, that the practitioner is liable only if no reasonable practitioner would have done the same; a private complaint against a doctor requires a credible opinion of another competent doctor (BNSS procedure for doctors; BNS s.106(1) proviso punishes up to 2 years for registered medical practitioners). Rash and negligent driving: s.281; s.106(1); hit and run: s.106(2) (see 4.5). Drunk driving causing death: culpable homicide where knowledge is shown (Alister Anthony Pareira).
  11. Attempt to murder vs hurt: 'intention or knowledge' and 'under such circumstances that if death had been caused he would be guilty of murder': hurt need not be caused (Sarju Prasad v State of Bihar, 1965: firing at a person with intent to kill is attempt even if no injury results). The second part of s.109 (hurt caused: life or up to 10 years) applies where hurt is caused to the person attacked.

⚖Landmark cases

Pulicherla Nagaraju v State of Andhra Pradesh

2006

Factors to decide whether the case is murder or culpable homicide not amounting to murder: nature of weapon, part of body, force, premeditation.

Rajwant Singh v State of Kerala

1966

Third clause of s.300: intended injury objectively sufficient in the ordinary course of nature.

Ghapoo Yadav v State of MP

2003

Grave and sudden provocation test applies to the reasonable man of the same class; provocation must be sudden.

Surinder Kumar v UT Chandigarh

1989

Sudden fight: no undue advantage or cruelty; the offender must not have taken unfair advantage.

Gurmukh Singh v State of Haryana

2009

Exception 4 to s.300 explained: heat of passion, sudden quarrel, no premeditation.

Aruna Ramchandra Shanbaug v Union of India

2011

Passive euthanasia permitted with court supervision in the absence of a law; active euthanasia is illegal.

Common Cause v Union of India

2018

Right to die with dignity is part of Art. 21; advance directives and 'living wills' recognised.

Kans Raj v State of Punjab

2000

'Soon before' requires proximity; cruelty for dowry must be established.

Hira Lal v State (Govt of NCT Delhi)

2003

304B and 498A: the court must examine the existence of cruelty and the presumption in section 113B of the Evidence Act.

!Exam traps

  • TrapException 5 (consent) requires the deceased to be above 18 years.
  • TrapA person who provokes the fight or takes undue advantage cannot claim Exception 4.
  • TrapExplanation 3 to s.100: a child in the womb does not count as a human being unless a part is born.
