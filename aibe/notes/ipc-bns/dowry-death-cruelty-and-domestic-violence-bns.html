iOverview

Offences relating to marriage in BNS: cohabitation caused by a man deceitfully inducing a belief of lawful marriage (s.81

★Must know

  1. Offences relating to marriage in BNS: cohabitation caused by a man deceitfully inducing a belief of lawful marriage (s.81; IPC 493): up to 10 years and fine; bigamy (s.82(1); IPC 494): up to 7 years and fine; bigamy with concealment of the former marriage (s.82(2); IPC 495): up to 10 years and fine; marriage ceremony fraudulently gone through without lawful marriage (s.83; IPC 496): up to 7 years and fine; enticing, taking away or detaining a married woman with criminal intent (s.84; IPC 498): up to 2 years or fine or both. Adultery (IPC 497) has been omitted after Joseph Shine (2018), so a husband has no criminal remedy against his wife's paramour, and vice versa.
  2. Bigamy ingredients: (i) accused has a living spouse; (ii) he marries again; (iii) the second marriage is void by reason of the spouse's being alive; (iv) the first marriage is valid and subsisting. Both marriages must be proved with valid ceremonies: Bhaurao Shankar Lokhande v State of Maharashtra (1965): 'solemnised' means celebrated with proper ceremonies and in due form; mere admission is insufficient. Priya Bala Ghosh v Suresh Chandra Ghosh (1971) and Kanwal Ram v Himachal Pradesh Administration (1966): essential ceremonies (saptapadi) must be proved. Sarla Mudgal v UoI (1995) and Lily Thomas v UoI (2000): conversion to Islam to marry again does not dissolve the first marriage; the second marriage is void and bigamy applies. Exception: marriage declared void by a court of competent jurisdiction; spouse continually absent for seven years and not heard of as alive.
  3. Section 85 (IPC 498A): husband or relative of husband subjecting a woman to cruelty: up to 3 years and fine. Section 86 defines cruelty: (a) wilful conduct likely to drive the woman to commit suicide or to cause grave injury or danger to life, limb or health, mental or physical; (b) harassment of the woman where such harassment is with a view to coercing her or any person related to her to meet any unlawful demand for property or valuable security, or on account of failure by her or her relatives to meet such demand. Offence is cognizable, non-bailable and non-compoundable under the CrPC schedule; the Supreme Court in Gian Singh v State of Punjab (2012) and B.S. Joshi v State of Haryana (2003) has permitted the High Court to quash matrimonial cases under its inherent powers where the parties settle.
  4. 'Relative of the husband' under 498A: includes blood relatives and persons related by marriage; U. Suvetha v State (2009): a woman living with the husband as a concubine is not a 'relative'; Vijeta Gajra v State (NCT of Delhi) (2010): a woman living in a live-in relationship is not a 'relative' for 498A (this was resolved differently in subsequent cases; the BNS Explanation does not extend it) Verify.
  5. Misuse of 498A and safeguards: Sushil Kumar Sharma v Union of India (2005) upheld constitutionality but called misuse 'legal terrorism'; Arnesh Kumar v State of Bihar (2014): arrests only after satisfaction that the grounds in s.41 CrPC (BNSS 35) exist; the magistrate must not authorise detention mechanically; notice of appearance in cases where punishment is up to 7 years; Rajesh Sharma v State of UP (2017) directed Family Welfare Committees, which was set aside in Social Action Forum for Manav Adhikar v UoI (2018); Preeti Gupta v State of Jharkhand (2010): the courts should be cautious about omnibus allegations against distant relatives; Kahkashan Kausar v State of Bihar (2022): the vague, general allegations against in-laws are insufficient; Achin Gupta v State of Haryana (2024): the Supreme Court observed that cases cannot be continued on bald allegations Verify; Mahmood Ali v State of UP (2023): reiterated that FIR quashing is justified if the criminal proceeding is used for personal vendetta; Dara Lakshmi Narayana v State of Telangana (2024): reiterated protection against general and omnibus allegations in dowry and cruelty complaints Verify. Parliament has not carried forward any gender-neutral amendment; the Parliamentary Standing Committee in 2023 recommended a gender-neutral provision in the context of rape and unnatural offences but the Sanhita retained the woman-victim framework for ss.63-99 Verify.
  6. Dowry death (s.80; IPC 304B): ingredients: (i) death of a woman caused by burns or bodily injury or otherwise than under normal circumstances; (ii) within seven years of her marriage; (iii) soon before her death she was subjected to cruelty or harassment by her husband or any relative of her husband; (iv) the cruelty or harassment was for, or in connection with, any demand for dowry. Explanation: 'dowry' has the same meaning as in Section 2 of the Dowry Prohibition Act, 1961. Punishment: imprisonment not less than 7 years, which may extend to imprisonment for life. Presumption: where it is shown that soon before her death the woman was subjected to cruelty or harassment for dowry, the court shall presume that such person caused the dowry death (BSA s.118; Evidence Act s.113B). Burden shifts to the accused; the presumption is rebuttable.
  7. Interplay: Section 304B and Section 302 are not mutually exclusive; if homicidal death with dowry harassment is proved, the court can convict for murder and dowry death in the alternative but cannot punish twice for the same act (Satbir Singh; Kaliyaperumal v State of TN, 2003). Suicide within seven years with dowry harassment may amount to both 304B and abetment of suicide (s.108) with presumption under BSA s.117 (Evidence Act 113A: presumption as to abetment of suicide by a married woman within seven years of marriage, if she was subjected to cruelty). The words 'soon before' do not fix a time limit: Kans Raj; Rajinder Singh v State of Punjab (2015): there must be a proximate and live link between the effect of cruelty and the death.
  8. Dowry Prohibition Act, 1961: giving, taking or abetting the giving or taking of dowry (s.3): imprisonment not less than 5 years and fine not less than Rs 15,000 or the value of the dowry, whichever is more; demanding dowry (s.4): imprisonment not less than 6 months extendable to 2 years and fine up to Rs 10,000; advertisement offering share in property as consideration for marriage (s.4A); burden of proof on the person who takes or abets the taking of dowry (s.8A); dowry given for the benefit of the wife must be transferred to her within three months (s.6); offences under the Act are cognizable, non-bailable and non-compoundable (s.8). Gifts given at the time of marriage without demand and entered in a list are excluded from dowry (s.3(2)). Dowry Prohibition Officers are appointed by State Governments (s.8B). Pratibha Rani v Suraj Kumar (1985): stridhan remains the wife's absolute property; a husband or in-laws who dishonestly misappropriate it commit CBT (s.316, previously IPC 406). Rashmi Kumar v Mahesh Kumar Bhada (1997) confirmed entrustment of stridhan to the husband.
  9. Protection of Women from Domestic Violence Act, 2005: civil law remedies - protection orders, residence orders (right to shared household: S.R. Batra v Taruna Batra (2007); Satish Chander Ahuja v Sneha Ahuja (2020): shared household includes a household in which the woman lived with her husband, not limited to property owned by him), monetary relief, custody and compensation. Breach of a protection order is a criminal offence (s.31: imprisonment up to 1 year and fine up to Rs 20,000). 'Domestic relationship' includes live-in relationships ('relationship in the nature of marriage': D. Velusamy v D. Patchaiammal, 2010; Indra Sarma v V.K.V. Sarma, 2013: the criteria of duration, shared household, pooling of resources etc.). 'Respondent' includes male adult only (the proviso allows woman respondent in the case of a relative); Hiral P. Harsora v Kusum Narottamdas Harsora (2016) struck down the words 'adult male' to allow complaints against women relatives. Maintenance: Section 125 CrPC (BNSS s.144) is available to wives, children and parents; Rajnesh v Neha (2020) lays down guidelines on maintenance (disclosure of assets and liabilities affidavit); Mohd. Ahmed Khan v Shah Bano Begum (1985) and Danial Latifi v UoI (2001) for Muslim women's maintenance; Shabana Bano v Imran Khan (2009); Mohd. Abdul Samad v State of Telangana (2024): a divorced Muslim woman can claim maintenance under s.125 CrPC and not only under the 1986 Act Verify.
  10. Marital rape: Exception 2 to s.63: sexual intercourse by a man with his own wife, the wife not being under 18, is not rape. Independent Thought v UoI (2017) read down the earlier 15-year limit to 18. The Delhi High Court delivered a split verdict in RIT Foundation v UoI (May 2022); the Karnataka High Court (Hrishikesh Sahoo v State of Karnataka, 2022) allowed a rape charge to be framed against a husband; the Supreme Court has the challenge pending Verify · current status. Separately, s.67 (IPC 376B) penalises sexual intercourse with a wife living separately under a decree of separation or otherwise, without her consent: 2 to 7 years and fine.
  11. Section 84 (IPC 498): enticing or taking away or detaining with criminal intent a married woman, not the husband: the offence is against the husband's right and not the wife; since Joseph Shine (2018) the gender-biased scheme is criticised, though the provision remains; the offence requires the woman to be 'taken away' or 'enticed' with the intent that she may have illicit intercourse with any person.
  12. Related procedural points: BNSS provides for medical examination of rape victims, recording of statements by a woman magistrate or officer, and video recording of statements (BNSS s.173(1) read with s.176: audio-video recording of search and statement of rape victims); trials in camera (BNSS s.366(2)); time limit of two months for completing investigation in rape cases (BNSS s.193(2)) and trial within two months of framing charges (CrPC 309: BNSS 346); zero FIR (BNSS s.173(1)); e-FIR (BNSS s.173(1)(ii)) Verify · references.

⚖Landmark cases

Sarla Mudgal v Union of India

1995

Conversion to Islam does not dissolve the first marriage; second marriage is void and bigamy is attracted.

Lily Thomas v Union of India

2000

Bigamy cannot be escaped by conversion; the Court recognised the need for a uniform civil code.

Bhaurao Shankar Lokhande v State of Maharashtra

1965

'Solemnised' in the bigamy provision means performed with due ceremonies; mere proof of marriage ceremony is required.

Arnesh Kumar v State of Bihar

2014

No automatic arrest under 498A; police must record reasons; magistrate must examine reasons for detention.

Preeti Gupta v State of Jharkhand

2010

Courts should be careful about exaggerated or omnibus allegations in matrimonial cases.

Kahkashan Kausar @ Sonam v State of Bihar

2022

Proceedings against relatives based on general allegations are an abuse of process.

Rajinder Singh v State of Punjab

2015

'Soon before her death' depends on facts; proximity and live link are required.

Hiral P. Harsora v Kusum Narottamdas Harsora

2016

The words 'adult male' in s.2(q) of the DV Act struck down; women relatives can be respondents.

S.R. Batra v Taruna Batra

2007

'Shared household' meaning narrowly interpreted; later reversed in Satish Chander Ahuja (2020).

Pratibha Rani v Suraj Kumar

1985

Stridhan is the wife's exclusive property; refusal to return amounts to criminal breach of trust.

Indra Sarma v V.K.V. Sarma

2013

Live-in relationships and the 'relationship in the nature of marriage' guidelines.

Rajnesh v Neha

2020

Guidelines on interim maintenance: affidavit of disclosure, overlapping jurisdiction, arrears.

!Exam traps

  • TrapDowry death needs death within SEVEN years of marriage; abetment-of-suicide presumption (BSA s.117) also uses seven years.
  • TrapMinimum sentence for dowry death is 7 years; minimum for taking dowry (Dowry Prohibition Act s.3) is 5 years and fine of Rs 15,000 or the value of the dowry, whichever is more.
  • TrapCruelty under 498A has two limbs; both are in s.86 BNS.
  • TrapAdultery is no longer an offence; bigamy remains.
