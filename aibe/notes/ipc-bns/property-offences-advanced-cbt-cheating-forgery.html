Property Offences Advanced: Theft, CBT, Cheating, Forgery and Mischief in Practice

iOverview

Illustrations that recur: (a) A cuts down a tree on Z's ground to dishonestly take it

★Must know

  1. Illustrations that recur: (a) A cuts down a tree on Z's ground to dishonestly take it: theft when the tree is severed; (b) A finds a purse on the road and keeps it believing the owner can be found: not theft but misappropriation if dishonest intention arises later; (c) A shopkeeper takes money entrusted to him for payment of a bill and uses it for himself: CBT; (d) a servant takes his master's money from the drawer: theft by servant (s.306) not CBT unless entrusted with possession or dominion; (e) a man says he will give a job for a bribe and takes money, knowing he cannot: cheating; (f) a man obtains a loan with a false statement of property: cheating by inducement; (g) A threatens to publish Z's secret unless paid: extortion; (h) A takes Z's cell phone by snatching from a rider of a two-wheeler: snatching (s.304).
  2. Theft: possession - the possession of servant, wife or clerk is that of the master, husband or employer (Explanation 3-5 to s.303); possession of the owner is theft even if the thief is the co-owner who takes the property out of the possession of the other (Explanation). A person who takes his own property from the possession of a bailee, pledgee or lien-holder commits theft. 'Dishonestly' requires wrongful gain or loss: taking property in a bona fide claim of right is not theft (Chandi Kumar Das Karmarkar v Abanidra Kumar Das, 1972). The taking must be 'without consent' - consent may be express or implied; consent obtained by fraud is no consent. Theft of electricity: the Electricity Act s.135 governs, not the IPC: a special law prevails (Avtar Singh v State of Punjab, 2002 on theft of electricity: Section 135 applies; IPC is excluded as the Electricity Act is a complete code).
  3. Extortion v Robbery: In extortion, the victim is put in fear and delivers the property. In robbery by extortion, the offender is present at the time of the delivery, puts the victim in fear of instant death, hurt or restraint, and obtains the property. Illustration: A holds a knife at Z and demands money; Z delivers: robbery. If A says, 'I will have your son killed next month unless you pay', and Z pays: extortion, not robbery. Fear must be of injury to person, reputation or property; the victim's consent is obtained by fear (R.S. Nayak v A.R. Antulay (1986) discussed the contention of extortion in the public servant context).
  4. Dacoity: the number is the gist; the offence is complete even if only the attempt exists, and the members who are present, aiding or attempting count (s.310). The gang need not be armed; the presence of five or more persons conjointly attempting is enough. A conviction for dacoity requires proof that at least five persons participated; the prosecution must prove that five or more persons took part; if fewer than five are proved to have participated, the offence is robbery and not dacoity Verify · authorities.
  5. Criminal misappropriation vs CBT: Dishonest misappropriation requires only 'possession' (innocent at first); CBT requires 'entrustment'. Illustration: A finds a ring and later sells it: misappropriation (s.314). A is given Z's ring for safe-keeping and sells it: CBT (s.316). Entrustment includes a bailment, trust, agency and dominion, even over money. Key authority: Jaswantrai Manilal Akhaney v State of Bombay (1956) - a director/bank officer's dominion over funds; Sudhir Shantilal Mehta v CBI (2009): the distinction between CBT and cheating: in CBT the entrustment is lawful at the beginning, whereas in cheating the dishonest intention is present at the time of inducement; Delhi Race Club (1940) Ltd v State of UP (2024): the Supreme Court held that cheating and CBT are different offences and that the same facts cannot ordinarily constitute both, since in cheating the dishonest intention exists at the inception, whereas in CBT the property is entrusted willingly; the Court quashed the summoning order for both offences in a case about breach of a contract Verify.
  6. Cheating vs breach of contract: Dishonest intention must exist at the time of making the promise: Hridaya Ranjan Prasad Verma; Vesa Holdings v State of Kerala (2015); Sarabjit Kaur v State of Punjab (2023): the court quashed a cheating complaint where the dispute was purely a failure to perform and dishonest intention at the inception was not established; Vijay Kumar Ghai v State of West Bengal (2022); Indian Oil Corporation v NEPC India Ltd (2006): the Supreme Court deprecated the growing tendency to give a criminal colour to commercial disputes; Mohd. Ibrahim v State of Bihar (2009): sale of the property of another is not forgery; but it may be cheating. In real estate disputes, the Supreme Court has repeatedly intervened to quash complaints that convert civil disputes into criminal cases.
  7. Cheating by personation (s.319), cheating and dishonestly inducing delivery of property or making, altering or destroying a valuable security (s.318(4)): includes an offender's inducing a bank to disburse a loan on forged documents. A lottery or chit fund fraud may also attract the Banning of Unregulated Deposit Schemes Act, 2019 (BUDS Act). Online cheating: cheating by personation using a computer resource is also an offence under IT Act s.66D (up to 3 years and fine up to Rs 1 lakh); BNS ss.318-319 apply along with the IT Act; the cyber-fraud provisions interplay with organised crime s.111 where cyber crimes are part of a syndicate's continuing unlawful activity.
  8. Mischief: Intent or knowledge of causing wrongful loss or damage; the act must cause destruction or diminution of value or utility; wrongful loss or damage is not required to be of monetary kind. Mischief by killing or maiming an animal; mischief by injury to works of irrigation, public roads, bridges, navigable rivers; mischief by fire or explosive substance with intent to destroy a house (life or up to 10 years). The Prevention of Damage to Public Property Act, 1984 is the special law. In Re: Destruction of Public and Private Properties v State of AP (2009): guidelines on liability of organisers of riots for damage to public property (reversing the burden); Kodungallur Film Society v UoI (2018): directions on mob violence damaging property.
  9. Criminal trespass: entry on property in the possession of another with intent to commit an offence, or to intimidate, insult or annoy; or unlawful remaining after lawful entry with such intent. House trespass: criminal trespass in a building used as a human dwelling, a place of worship or a place for the custody of property. Lurking house trespass: house trespass after taking precautions to conceal the entry; house-breaking: entering by specific methods (a passage made by the trespasser, opening a lock, etc.); by night means after sunset and before sunrise. Trespass is an offence against possession, not ownership: a true owner who forcibly enters a house possessed by a tenant is guilty of trespass if he has intent to annoy.
  10. Forgery: 'making a false document' (s.335; IPC 464) includes dishonestly or fraudulently making, signing, sealing or executing a document or part of it with the intention of causing it to be believed that it was made by the authority of a person by whom the maker knows it was not made; altering a document without authority after it has been made; causing a person who is incapable of knowing the contents to sign or alter. Electronic signatures and digital records are covered; a 'document' (s.2(8)) includes electronic and digital records. Forgery of a valuable security or will (s.338: life or up to 10 years); forgery for the purpose of cheating (s.336(3): up to 7 years). Using a forged document as genuine (s.340): punishment as if the user had forged it. Possession of a forged document with the intent to use it as genuine, making or possessing counterfeit seals (ss.338-343).
  11. Falsification of accounts (s.344; IPC 477A): clerk, officer or servant wilfully and with intent to defraud destroys, alters, mutilates or falsifies any book, electronic record, paper, writing, valuable security or account that belongs to or is in the possession of his employer: up to 7 years or fine or both. Interplay with Companies Act s.447 and Prevention of Corruption Act. Property mark offences (ss.345-349): using a false property mark with intent to deceive (up to 1 year), counterfeiting a property mark used by another, and the Trade Marks Act 1999 provisions (ss.103-107) now cover most commercial counterfeiting (which are more specific than the IPC).
  12. Offences relating to coins, banknotes and stamps (ss.178-188; IPC 230-263A): counterfeiting Indian coin (life or up to 10 years), counterfeiting currency notes or bank notes (s.179-180: life or up to 10 years and fine); possession of forged notes (s.181); and the newly added possession of counterfeit stamps. Recent emphasis on 'fake Indian currency notes (FICN)' as a terror finance concern; the BNS treats the counterfeiting of currency as an offence against the economic security of India under s.113 terrorist act where it affects economic security (s.113(1)(a) includes damage to the monetary stability of India by means of counterfeit or smuggled currency).

⚖Landmark cases

Jaswantrai Manilal Akhaney v State of Bombay

1956

CBT by bank director: 'dominion over property' suffices; entrustment can arise from the position.

Sudhir Shantilal Mehta v CBI

2009

Distinguished CBT and cheating; the relevance of intention at the inception.

Delhi Race Club (1940) Ltd v State of UP

2024

Cheating and CBT are antithetical on the same set of facts; civil disputes should not be criminalised.

Indian Oil Corporation v NEPC India Ltd

2006

Deprecated criminalisation of commercial disputes and the filing of criminal cases to put pressure on the other party.

Sarabjit Kaur v State of Punjab

2023

Breach of contract does not amount to cheating without fraudulent intention at the inception.

Chandi Kumar Das Karmarkar v Abanidra Kumar Das

1972

Bona fide claim of right negatives dishonest intention in theft.

In Re: Destruction of Public and Private Properties v State of AP

2009

Guidelines on liability for damage to public property in riots and agitations.

Pratibha Rani v Suraj Kumar

1985

Stridhan misappropriation is CBT.

Avtar Singh v State of Punjab

2002

Electricity theft is governed by the Electricity Act; IPC is displaced by the special law in that field.

!Exam traps

  • TrapTheft of electricity is governed by the Electricity Act, 2003 s.135, not the IPC/BNS theft provision.
  • TrapEntrustment is the dividing line between CBT and criminal misappropriation.
  • TrapCheating at inception vs breach of contract: the latter is a civil wrong.
  • TrapA servant's possession of master's goods is the master's possession: taking by servant is theft by servant (s.306) unless entrusted.
