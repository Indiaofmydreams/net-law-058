Criminal Liability Doctrines: Mens Rea, Strict Liability, Vicarious and Corporate Liability, Omissions, Causation

iOverview

Mens rea in Indian law is reflected in the words 'intentionally', 'knowingly', 'voluntarily', 'dishonestly', 'fraudulently', 'rashly', 'negligently', 'with intent to', 'having reason to believe' used in the definitions rather than as a general doctrine.

★Must know

  1. Mens rea in Indian law is reflected in the words 'intentionally', 'knowingly', 'voluntarily', 'dishonestly', 'fraudulently', 'rashly', 'negligently', 'with intent to', 'having reason to believe' used in the definitions rather than as a general doctrine. The Code defines the relevant mental states in BNS s.2.
  2. Intention vs knowledge: intention is the purpose or desire to produce a result; knowledge is awareness of the likely consequence without desire. Murder (s.101) clauses mix the two. Motive vs intention: motive is the reason; it is generally irrelevant to criminal liability but relevant to proof and sentencing. Intention is inferred from the nature of the act, the weapon, the part of the body, and the surrounding circumstances (Anda v State of Rajasthan, 1966).
  3. Rashness vs negligence: rashness is conscious taking of risk with the knowledge that the act may cause harm but hoping it will not; negligence is failure to exercise the care a reasonable man would exercise (Nidamarti Nagabhushanam v State of Karnataka, 2008). Gross negligence is required for criminal liability of professionals (Jacob Mathew) and in 304A (Kurban Hussein Mohammedali Rangwalla v State of Maharashtra, 1965: the act must be the proximate cause).
  4. Strict liability: presumption of mens rea in statutory offences is rebuttable by the words of the statute and the object (Sherras v De Rutzen, 1895; State of Maharashtra v M.H. George, 1965; Gammon (Hong Kong) v Attorney-General (1985)); typically in public welfare, food adulteration, customs and revenue statutes. The Indian Penal Code provisions themselves require mens rea, subject to a few exceptions (e.g., sale of adulterated food), while mens rea is generally irrelevant under the 'public welfare' statutes.
  5. Vicarious liability: ordinarily unknown in IPC/BNS offences; it arises only where the statute so provides (e.g., Food Safety and Standards Act, Negotiable Instruments Act s.141, Drugs and Cosmetics Act, Essential Commodities Act). Sham Sunder v State of Haryana (1989): partners cannot be vicariously liable for IPC offences without a specific allegation; Maksud Saiyed v State of Gujarat (2008): directors are not vicariously liable for IPC offences of the company unless the Code provides or specific overt acts are alleged; Sunil Bharti Mittal v CBI (2015): vicarious criminal liability of directors needs the company to be arraigned and the director to be the 'alter ego'.
  6. Corporate criminal liability: a company is a 'person' under BNS s.2 (the 'person' definition includes any company or association or body of persons, whether incorporated or not). Standard Chartered Bank v Directorate of Enforcement (2005): a company can be prosecuted even for offences requiring mandatory imprisonment; the court may impose the fine alone; Iridium India Telecom v Motorola (2011): the company is liable for offences involving mens rea through the 'directing mind and will' (identification doctrine); Aneeta Hada v Godfather Travels (2012): director cannot be prosecuted under NI Act s.141 without arraigning the company.
  7. Omissions: an 'act' includes a series of acts and 'illegal omission' (BNS s.2). Liability for omission arises where there is a legal duty to act (statute, contract, special relationship, assumption of duty, creating a danger): the parent who starves a child, the doctor under duty to treat, the gatekeeper who fails to alert. Illustrations: death by starvation of a ward by the guardian is murder or culpable homicide; omission to give food is covered by 'illegal omission'.
  8. Causation: the act must be the 'proximate' or 'direct' cause of the result; novus actus interveniens breaks the chain if the intervening act is independent and unforeseeable. Concurrent causes (causing death even if the victim was already weak: 'thin skull' rule; BNS s.100 Explanation 1: the death of a person suffering from a disease or bodily infirmity hastened by the act is homicide). Explanation 2: causing bodily injury which, with proper treatment, could have been prevented: the injurer is liable. Explanation 3: causing the death of a child in the mother's womb is not homicide, but the death of a living child, any part of which has been brought forth, may be culpable homicide even if the child is not completely born.
  9. Joint-property and group issues: the Code treats 'every act done by several persons' in BNS s.3(6); co-operation by doing one of several acts (s.3(7)); acts caused partly by an act and partly by omission (s.3(8)); cooperation by several persons in a criminal act (s.3(9)). Each of these explanations is expressly in the 'General Explanations' chapter.
  10. 'Voluntarily' (s.2): causing an effect by means whereby the person intended to cause it or by means which, at the time of employing them, he knew or had reason to believe to be likely to cause it. 'Good faith' (s.2): nothing is said to be done or believed in good faith which is done or believed without due care and attention.
  11. 'Dishonestly' (s.2) and 'fraudulently' (s.2): dishonesty needs wrongful gain or loss; fraud requires intention to defraud, which involves deceit and injury or risk of injury even if no wrongful gain (Dr Vimla v Delhi Administration; S. Dutt v State of UP, 1966).
  12. 'Public servant' (s.2): defined by a long list that includes every commissioned officer in the military, naval or air forces, every judge, every officer of a court, every arbitrator, every person authorised to perform election duties, every officer of the Government, and every officer in the service or pay of a local authority or of a corporation established by or under a Central, Provincial or State Act or a Government company; the Prevention of Corruption Act, 1988 has its own definition in s.2(c). Whether the staff of a body are public servants depends on whether they fall within the statutory list Verify · the clause number in BNS s.2.

⚖Landmark cases

Sherras v De Rutzen

1895

Presumption in favour of mens rea can be displaced only by clear words or necessary implication.

State of Maharashtra v M.H. George

1965

Strict liability under FERA; mens rea excluded where statute's object requires it.

Maksud Saiyed v State of Gujarat

2008

Directors' vicarious liability for the Company's IPC offences requires specific allegation of role; no automatic liability.

Standard Chartered Bank v Directorate of Enforcement

2005

A company may be prosecuted and fined even for offences carrying mandatory imprisonment.

Iridium India Telecom v Motorola

2011

The company is liable for mens rea offences through the person in control (identification doctrine).

Aneeta Hada v Godfather Travels & Tours

2012

The company must be arraigned to prosecute its directors by vicarious liability.

Kurban Hussein v State of Maharashtra

1965

For rash or negligent act under s.304A, the act must be the proximate and efficient cause; remote cause insufficient.

Anda v State of Rajasthan

1966

Intention is inferred from the circumstances such as the number, nature and place of injuries.

!Exam traps

  • TrapVicarious liability in IPC/BNS offences is not recognised unless the statute provides it; but corporate bodies themselves can be prosecuted.
  • TrapRashness involves consciousness of risk; negligence is want of reasonable care.
  • TrapIllegal omission needs a legal duty; a moral duty to rescue is not enough.
