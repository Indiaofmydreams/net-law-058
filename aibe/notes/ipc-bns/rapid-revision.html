iBNS at a glance

What changed
The Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita, 2023 replaced the Indian Penal Code, 1860 for offences committed on or after 1 July 2024. IPC continues to govern offences committed before that date (Article 20(1) and BNS s.531-type savings).
Note
Chapter ranges outside Chapters II-VI are approximate groupings; confirm exact boundaries from the bare act Verify.

#Headline facts about the BNS

  • 358 sections (IPC had 511) arranged in 20 chapters (IPC had 23).
  • About 20 new offences added; imprisonment increased for about 33 offences; fine increased for about 83; mandatory minimum punishment in about 23 offences; community service for 6 offences; about 19 provisions repealed.
  • Women and children offences are brought up front in Chapter V (ss.63-99); offences affecting the human body follow in Chapter VI (ss.100-146).
  • Sedition (IPC 124A) is not retained as such; BNS 152 punishes acts endangering sovereignty, unity and integrity of India.
  • Adultery (IPC 497) and unnatural offences between adults (IPC 377) are omitted, reflecting Joseph Shine (2018) and Navtej Johar (2018).
  • Attempt to commit suicide (IPC 309) is dropped; a narrow offence survives in BNS 226 (attempt to commit suicide to compel or restrain a public servant).

#Punishment number sheet

  • Murder: death or life + fine (BNS 103)
  • Culpable homicide not amounting to murder: Part I life or up to 10 years + fine; Part II up to 10 years or fine or both
  • Dowry death: minimum 7 years, up to life
  • Rape: minimum 10 years, up to life; under 16: minimum 20 years, up to natural life; under 12: 20 years to life or death
  • Gang rape: minimum 20 years to life; of woman under 18: life or death
  • Acid attack: minimum 10 years to life; throwing/attempt: 5 to 7 years
  • Cruelty (498A/BNS 85): up to 3 years + fine
  • Stalking: up to 3 years (first), up to 5 (repeat)
  • Abetment of suicide: up to 10 years + fine; of a child or person of unsound mind: death, life or minimum 10 years
  • Attempt to murder: up to 10 years + fine; life if hurt caused to life convicts etc. (see s.109)
  • Theft: up to 3 years; snatching: up to 3 years; extortion: up to 7 years
  • Robbery: up to 10 years (14 if on highway at night); dacoity: life or up to 10 years; dacoity with murder: death, life or minimum 10 years
  • CBT: up to 5 years; clerk/servant or carrier: up to 7; public servant/banker/agent: life or up to 10
  • Cheating: up to 3 years; dishonest delivery of property: up to 7 years
  • Forgery: up to 2 years; for cheating: up to 7 years
  • Defamation: simple imprisonment up to 2 years or fine or community service
  • Criminal intimidation: up to 2 years; threat of death or grievous hurt: up to 7 years
  • BNS 152 (sedition replacement): life or up to 7 years + fine
  • Mob lynching: death or life + fine
  • Organised crime: death or life if death results (fine minimum Rs 10 lakh); otherwise 5 years to life (fine minimum Rs 5 lakh)
  • Terrorist act: death or life if death results; otherwise 5 years to life
  • Fraud under Companies Act s.447: 6 months to 10 years + fine of 1 to 3 times the amount involved

§Special criminal laws snapshot

Scheduled Castes and Scheduled Tribes (Prevention of Atrocities) Act, 1989 (as amended 2015 and 2018)

  • Section 3 lists atrocities committed by a person who is not a member of an SC or ST against a member of an SC or ST: forcing to eat inedible substances, dumping excreta, parading naked, wrongful occupation of land, compelling to do forced labour, denial of access to water, sexual exploitation, and others; punishment generally 6 months to 5 years and fine; s.3(2)(v): offence under IPC punishable with 10 years or more against a member of an SC/ST knowing him to be so, punishable with imprisonment for life and fine; s.3(1)(r) and (s): intentional insult or abuse in any place within public view.
  • Anticipatory bail is barred (s.18); the Supreme Court in Subhash Kashinath Mahajan v State of Maharashtra (2018) diluted the provisions (preliminary inquiry, approval for arrest of public servants), but Parliament restored the original position by the 2018 Amendment (s.18A) and the Supreme Court upheld the amendment in Prathvi Raj Chauhan v Union of India (2020); Hitesh Verma v State of Uttarakhand (2020): the insult must occur 'within public view' and the 'place within public view' is an open place seen or heard by the public.
  • Special Courts and Exclusive Special Courts; speedy trial; relief and rehabilitation; the offence is non-compoundable though the courts have quashed on settlement in suitable cases under inherent powers Verify.

Prevention of Corruption Act, 1988 (amended 2018)

  • 'Public servant' defined in s.2(c); s.7: public servant taking undue advantage: 3 to 7 years and fine; s.7A: taking undue advantage to influence a public servant by corrupt or illegal means: 3 to 7 years; s.8: giving or promising to give undue advantage to a public servant: up to 7 years or fine or both (a bribe-giver is also liable); s.9: offence relating to bribing a public servant by a commercial organisation; s.13: criminal misconduct by a public servant (now confined to dishonest or fraudulent misappropriation of property entrusted and disproportionate assets): 4 to 10 years and fine; s.17A: prior approval of the competent authority is necessary for an inquiry or investigation into an offence alleged to have been committed by a public servant in relation to a recommendation or decision in discharge of official functions (not for cases of arrest on the spot on a charge of accepting or attempting to accept undue advantage).
  • Sanction for prosecution (s.19): required for taking cognizance against a public servant; Subramanian Swamy v Manmohan Singh (2012): the sanction should be decided within three months (four with the Attorney General's advice); Neeraj Dutta v State (Govt of NCT of Delhi) (2022): a Constitution Bench held that a demand and acceptance of illegal gratification can be proved by circumstantial evidence in the absence of direct evidence if the complainant turns hostile or dies; the Supreme Court in State of Telangana v Managipet (2019) discussed the effect of the 2018 amendment on proof of demand Verify. P. Satyanarayana Murthy v State of AP (2015): proof of demand is indispensable for s.7 and s.13(1)(d).

Unlawful Activities (Prevention) Act, 1967

  • Defines 'unlawful activity', 'terrorist act' (s.15), 'terrorist organisation' (First Schedule), and 'unlawful association' (s.2(p)); punishments for raising funds for terrorist acts (s.17), conspiracy (s.18), membership of a terrorist organisation (s.38) and support (s.39). Bail is restricted by s.43D(5): the court cannot grant bail if there are reasonable grounds for believing that the accusation against the person is prima facie true (NIA v Zahoor Ahmad Shah Watali, 2019; contrast Union of India v K.A. Najeeb (2021): constitutional courts can grant bail where there is prolonged incarceration and the trial is unlikely to conclude soon; Vernon v State of Maharashtra (2023): a prima facie standard for ss.15, 17 and 18 requires specific evidence; Thwaha Fasal v UoI (2021); Gurwinder Singh v State of Punjab (2024): upheld the Watali approach in a restrictive way) Verify.
  • Overlap with BNS s.113 (terrorist act): police officers of the rank of Superintendent of Police have the discretion whether to proceed under BNS s.113 or UAPA; the Supreme Court has held that the UAPA, as a special law, prevails to the extent of inconsistency. Arup Bhuyan: mere membership is not an offence for a banned organisation unless active involvement is shown (2011); the Government's review of that reading-down was heard by a larger Bench in 2023 Verify · outcome.

Arms Act, 1959 and Immoral Traffic (Prevention) Act, 1956 and Indecent Representation of Women (Prohibition) Act, 1986

  • Arms Act s.25: possession of prohibited arms or ammunition without licence: 3 to 7 years and fine (s.25(1A)); s.27: use of arms: 3 to 7 years for use of arms or ammunition in contravention of s.5, and death or life imprisonment if the use results in death (s.27(3) mandatory death penalty struck down in State of Punjab v Dalbir Singh, 2012). Possession of a firearm in a public place without licence (e.g., in a car) attracts both the Arms Act and BNS provisions.
  • ITPA 1956: punishes keeping a brothel (s.3), living on the earnings of prostitution (s.4), procuring or inducing for prostitution (s.5), detaining a person in premises where prostitution is carried on (s.6), prostitution in or near public places (s.7), seducing or soliciting for purposes of prostitution (s.8); trafficking is now under BNS s.143; Gaurav Jain v Union of India (1997); Budhadev Karmaskar v State of West Bengal (2011; 2022): sex workers have a right to dignity under Art. 21 and police must not harass adult consenting sex workers (2022 directions).
  • Indecent Representation of Women (Prohibition) Act: prohibits publication or distribution of indecent representation of women through advertisements, publications, writings, paintings, figures or in any other manner; punishment up to 2 years and fine of Rs 2,000 to Rs 5,000 for first conviction; 2 to 7 years and fine of Rs 10,000 to Rs 1 lakh for subsequent convictions.

Juvenile Justice (Care and Protection of Children) Act, 2015

  • Child in conflict with law (below 18); heinous offences (minimum punishment seven years or more), serious offences (3 to 7 years), petty offences (up to 3 years); preliminary assessment by the Juvenile Justice Board for a child aged 16-18 who commits a heinous offence, covering mental and physical capacity, ability to understand the consequences, and the circumstances; the Board may then transfer the case to the Children's Court for trial as an adult; the maximum stay in a place of safety is up to age 21; death penalty and life imprisonment without release are prohibited (s.21); records are to be destroyed after the completion of the appeal period (s.24: removal of disqualification attached to conviction).
  • Age determination (s.94): matriculation certificate or equivalent, birth certificate from school, or birth certificate from a corporation, municipal authority or panchayat; in the absence of these, ossification test or other latest medical age determination test; Jarnail Singh v State of Haryana (2013): the same rule applies to child victims; Ashwani Kumar Saxena v State of MP (2012); Pratap Singh v State of Jharkhand (2005): the relevant date for determining juvenility is the date of the offence; Hari Ram v State of Rajasthan (2009). Juvenility claim may be raised at any stage, even after final disposal (s.9(2); Abuzar Hossain v State of WB, 2012).

Prevention of Damage to Public Property Act, 1984 and Mob-Violence Jurisprudence

  • Section 3: mischief causing damage to public property: imprisonment up to 5 years and fine; s.4: mischief causing damage to public property by fire or explosive substance: 1 to 10 years. The Supreme Court in Re: Destruction of Public and Private Properties (2009) and Kodungallur Film Society v UoI (2018) laid down directions, including setting up of liability on the organisers.
  • Tehseen S. Poonawalla v Union of India (2018): the Supreme Court condemned mob lynching and 'cow vigilantism' and issued preventive, remedial and punitive guidelines: appointment of a nodal officer in each district; FIR registration; fast-track trials; victim compensation; and directed Parliament to create a separate offence of lynching. BNS s.103(2) is Parliament's response; s.117(4) addresses mob grievous hurt. Subsequent follow up: Supreme Court in Nizam v Union of India (2024) reviewed compliance Verify.

?Practice scenarios

Application-style problems of the type AIBE uses. Each has a model answer with the rule and the reason.

Scenario: A fires at B intending to kill him. The bullet misses B and kills C, a bystander.

Answer: A is guilty of murder of C. Doctrine of transferred malice: BNS 102 (IPC 301) treats the offence as if the person intended to be killed had been killed. Punishment under BNS 103.

Rule: BNS 101, 102, 103

Scenario: A and B quarrel suddenly in a market. B draws a knife; A snatches it and stabs B once in the chest, causing death. There was no premeditation.

Answer: Exception 4 to BNS 101 (sudden fight) applies if A did not take undue advantage or act cruelly; A is liable for culpable homicide not amounting to murder (BNS 105). If A had inflicted several blows after B was disarmed, undue advantage would be shown and the exception fails.

Rule: BNS 101 Exception 4; Surinder Kumar

Scenario: A learns that his wife is having an affair. Three hours later, after a long reflection, he kills her.

Answer: Murder. Grave and sudden provocation requires that the offender had no time to cool down; the passage of time and deliberation defeat the exception (Nanavati).

Rule: BNS 101 Exception 1

Scenario: A shopkeeper is asked by Z to keep Rs 50,000 safely. He uses it to pay his own debts.

Answer: Criminal breach of trust (BNS 316): entrustment plus dishonest use in violation of the trust.

Rule: BNS 316(2)

Scenario: A finds a gold chain on the road, takes it home and a week later, knowing the owner, sells it.

Answer: Dishonest misappropriation of property (BNS 314): possession was innocent; dishonest intention arose later. If he knew the owner from the beginning and picked it up with dishonest intention, it is theft.

Rule: BNS 314 v 303

Scenario: Four armed men break into a house and rob the owner at knifepoint.

Answer: Robbery (BNS 309), not dacoity, because dacoity requires five or more persons.

Rule: BNS 309, 310

Scenario: A sends a message to Z: 'Pay Rs 5 lakh or I will release your private photographs.' Z pays.

Answer: Extortion (BNS 308): putting a person in fear of injury (to reputation) and dishonestly inducing delivery of property; also criminal intimidation (BNS 351). If the threat is of sexual nature with images of a woman, voyeurism or IT Act provisions may also apply.

Rule: BNS 308, 351

Scenario: A doctor performs a procedure with a recognised technique, but the patient dies from a known, unavoidable complication.

Answer: No criminal liability: Jacob Mathew requires gross negligence or recklessness. BNS 106(1) proviso caps punishment at 2 years for a registered medical practitioner even where negligence is proved; simple negligence gives rise to civil liability only.

Rule: BNS 106(1); Jacob Mathew

Scenario: A drunk driver hits a pedestrian and flees without informing the police or magistrate. The pedestrian dies.

Answer: BNS 106(2) (hit and run): causing death by rash and negligent driving not amounting to culpable homicide and escaping without reporting: up to 10 years and fine. If the driver had knowledge that death was likely (e.g., extreme drunken driving at high speed in a crowded area), culpable homicide not amounting to murder (BNS 105 Part II) may be charged (Alister Anthony Pareira). Note the position on the implementation of s.106(2) Verify.

Rule: BNS 106(2), 105

Scenario: A and B have been in a consensual relationship for two years. A promised marriage honestly but later marries someone else under family pressure. B alleges rape.

Answer: No rape on these facts. Consent given on a promise made in good faith that later was broken is not obtained by a 'misconception of fact'; the promise must have been false from the inception (Uday; Pramod Pawar; Naim Ahamed). BNS 69 requires deceitful means including a promise made without any intention of fulfilling it.

Rule: BNS 63, 69

Scenario: Within 5 years of marriage a woman dies of burns at her matrimonial home. Her family says the husband and his parents demanded a car repeatedly until a week before the death.

Answer: Dowry death (BNS 80(2)): death otherwise than in normal circumstances within seven years, with cruelty for dowry soon before death. The court presumes that the husband and relatives caused the dowry death (BSA s.118). Minimum 7 years, up to life. Cruelty (BNS 85) may also be charged; abetment of suicide (BNS 108) is an alternative with the presumption in BSA s.117.

Rule: BNS 80, 85; BSA 117-118

Scenario: Six persons stop a vehicle on suspicion that it carries beef, drag out the driver and beat him to death.

Answer: Mob lynching: BNS 103(2) applies where five or more persons acting in concert commit murder on the ground of race, caste, community, sex, place of birth, language, personal belief or any similar ground. Death or life imprisonment and fine. Also unlawful assembly/rioting (BNS 189-191).

Rule: BNS 103(2); Tehseen Poonawalla

Scenario: A mixes poison in B's tea intending to kill him. B notices the smell and does not drink it.

Answer: Attempt to murder (BNS 109): the act of mixing poison with intent to kill is a step beyond preparation; if the tea had been handed to B, the last act would be done; either way the act is proximate. Up to 10 years and fine.

Rule: BNS 109; Abhayanand Mishra

Scenario: A child aged 6 takes a toy from a shop without paying.

Answer: No offence: a child below 7 has absolute immunity (BNS 20).

Rule: BNS 20

Scenario: A in a state of unsoundness of mind, believing he is cutting a log, kills B.

Answer: Protected by BNS 22 (insanity) if, at the time of the act, he was incapable of knowing the nature of the act. The burden is on A to prove unsoundness at the time of the act by a preponderance of probabilities; legal, not medical, insanity is required.

Rule: BNS 22

Scenario: A, at gunpoint, is ordered by robbers to drive their getaway car.

Answer: Compulsion (BNS 32): a threat of instant death excuses the act except murder and offences against the State punishable with death; the threat must be present and continuing, and the person must not have voluntarily placed himself in the situation.

Rule: BNS 32

Scenario: A buys goods from a company on the basis of a contract signed by the managing director, though the board's resolution authorising it was never passed.

Answer: The company is bound if A acted in good faith and had no notice of the irregularity: the rule of indoor management (Royal British Bank v Turquand). It does not protect A if he had notice, or if the document was forged (Ruben v Great Fingall).

Rule: Companies Act; Turquand

Scenario: A public company held its last AGM on 30 September 2025. It wants to hold the next AGM on 15 January 2027.

Answer: Not permissible: the gap between two AGMs cannot exceed 15 months (s.96), and the AGM for FY ending 31 March 2026 must be held within six months (by 30 September 2026). The Registrar may extend the time by up to three months for reasons shown, but not beyond that.

Rule: Companies Act s.96

Scenario: An operational creditor is owed Rs 80 lakh and wants to initiate CIRP.

Answer: Not maintainable at present: the minimum amount of default is Rs 1 crore (notification dated 24 March 2020). The creditor may pursue ordinary recovery remedies.

Rule: IBC s.4

Scenario: A private company refuses to register a transfer of shares without giving reasons.

Answer: A private company's articles can restrict transfer, but if the refusal is in contravention of the articles or the Act, the transferor or transferee may appeal to the NCLT within 30 days of the refusal or within 60 days from the date of delivery of the instrument if the company has not responded (s.58(3)-(4)).

Rule: Companies Act s.58

Scenario: Directors of a listed company approve a loan of Rs 10 crore to the managing director's brother's firm without disclosing the relationship.

Answer: Breach of s.185 (loans to directors and persons in whom a director is interested) and s.184 (disclosure of interest) subject to the exceptions requiring a special resolution; fines of Rs 5 lakh to Rs 25 lakh and imprisonment up to six months for the recipient director or person who receives the loan.

Rule: Companies Act ss.184-185

✓Ten-minute checklist

  • Five exceptions to murder: provocation, private defence excess, public servant, sudden fight, consent.
  • Four clauses of murder (s.101): intention to kill; injury known likely to cause death; injury sufficient in ordinary course; imminently dangerous act.
  • Stages: intention, preparation, attempt, accomplishment.
  • Common intention (two or more; prior meeting of minds) versus common object (five or more; no prior concert).
  • Theft to extortion to robbery to dacoity: add force, add five or more persons.
  • CBT needs entrustment; cheating needs deception at inception.
  • BNS numbers: 63 rape, 64 punishment, 80 dowry death, 85 cruelty, 100 culpable homicide, 101 murder, 103 punishment, 303 theft, 304 snatching, 309 robbery, 310 dacoity, 316 CBT, 318 cheating, 356 defamation.
  • New offences: mob lynching 103(2), organised crime 111, petty organised crime 112, terrorism 113, hit and run 106(2), deceitful sexual intercourse 69, snatching 304, endangering sovereignty 152.
  • Community service: six provisions (202, 209, 226, 303(2), 355, 356(2)).
  • Company law: 7/2/1 members; AGM within 6 months and not more than 15 months gap; 4 board meetings with 120 days gap; special resolution 75%; IBC threshold Rs 1 crore; CIRP 180+90 days; fraud s.447.

≠Frequent confusions

PairThe difference
Theft vs extortionTheft: taking without consent. Extortion: consent obtained through fear of injury.
Kidnapping vs abductionKidnapping: from lawful guardianship (minors) or from India; abduction: force or deceit to compel a person to go; abduction is not an offence by itself unless for a specified purpose.
Hurt vs grievous hurtGrievous hurt belongs to the closed list of eight kinds in s.116.
Assault vs criminal forceAssault: apprehension of force; criminal force: actual use of force.
Rape vs BNS 69Rape needs absence of valid consent; BNS 69 covers sexual intercourse induced by deceit, not amounting to rape.
Defamation vs insultDefamation harms reputation by publication to a third person; insult is addressed to the person to provoke a breach of the peace.

☰Using the open book well

  • AIBE permits bare Acts. Mark the first page of each Chapter and the index of BNS, BNSS, BSA and the Companies Act with tabs; add a page of your own mapping table inside the bare act if permitted by the latest BCI rules Verify · the current rule on annotated bare acts.
  • Do not use the bare act to search for a section you have never seen; use it to confirm the number, the minimum or maximum punishment and the wording of exceptions.
  • For MCQs naming a case, link the case to a one-line rule; for those naming a section, check the bare act in seconds, then move on.
  • Attempt all questions; there is no negative marking in the pattern as understood Verify.
  • Time: about 3 hours for 100 questions: roughly 1.8 minutes per question; park the hard ones and return.

☰Elimination tactics for MCQs

  • Extreme words (always, never, only) often signal a wrong option.
  • When two options differ only in number (5 years v 7 years), recall the structure of the offence (basic v aggravated).
  • For 'which is not', test each option against the definition; the exception usually comes from a neighbouring section.
  • For statements, mark each as true or false before reading the combinations.
