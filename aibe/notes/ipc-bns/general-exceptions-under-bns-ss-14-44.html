Indian Penal Code / Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita · Chapter 2 of 19

General Exceptions (Chapter III, BNS ss.14-44)

High yield1–2 Qs expected6 min readBNS Ss. 14-44 (IPC Ss. 76-106)

In this chapter

  1. Overview
  2. Must know (18 points)
  3. Landmark cases (10)
  4. Exam traps

Chapter highlights →My highlights →

Tip: select any text to highlight it or ask a doubt.

iOverview

Structure: judicial and legal acts (14-16), mistake of fact (14, 17), accident (18), necessity (19), infancy (20-21), insanity (22), intoxication (23-24), consent (25-31), compulsion (32), trifling harm (33), private defence (34-44).

★Must know

  1. Structure: judicial and legal acts (14-16), mistake of fact (14, 17), accident (18), necessity (19), infancy (20-21), insanity (22), intoxication (23-24), consent (25-31), compulsion (32), trifling harm (33), private defence (34-44).
  2. Mistake of fact (s.14, 17) protects a person who by reason of mistake of FACT (not law) believes himself bound by law or justified. Ignorantia juris non excusat.
  3. Judicial acts: judge acting judicially in exercise of power he in good faith believes he has (s.15); acts done pursuant to a judgment or order of court (s.16).
  4. Accident (s.18): act done by accident or misfortune, without criminal intention or knowledge, in doing a lawful act in a lawful manner by lawful means with proper care. Tunda v Rex (1950): death in a sudden fight with lawful blow is not accident if intention is present.
  5. Necessity (s.19): act likely to cause harm but done without criminal intent to prevent other harm to person or property is no offence, if the harm prevented is greater and there is good faith. Example: ship captain steers to save more lives. Dudley & Stephens (1884): necessity is no defence to murder.
  6. Infancy: below 7 years: absolute immunity (s.20); 7-12 years: immunity if the child has not attained sufficient maturity to understand the nature and consequence of conduct (s.21). Above 12 years: full liability under BNS, but juvenile justice law (JJ Act 2015) governs persons below 18.
  7. Insanity (s.22): at the time of doing the act, by reason of unsoundness of mind, the person is incapable of knowing the nature of the act, or that it is wrong or contrary to law. The M'Naghten rules. Legal insanity, not medical insanity. Burden of proof is on the accused (Evidence Act/BSA), but only on preponderance of probability (Dahyabhai Chhaganbhai Thakkar, 1964). Irresistible impulse is not recognised.
  8. Intoxication: involuntary intoxication (administered without knowledge or against will) is a defence if it makes the person incapable of knowing the nature of the act (s.23). Voluntary intoxication is not a defence, but intention or knowledge is assessed as if sober for knowledge; for specific intent, the court may consider whether intoxication prevented the formation of intent (Basdev v State of PEPSU, 1956).
  9. Consent: s.25 (consent to suffer harm short of death, e.g., boxing, surgery), s.26 (act in good faith for benefit of a person with consent, even if risk of death: surgery), s.27 (child under 12 or person of unsound mind: guardian's consent), s.28 (consent under fear of injury or misconception of fact is no consent), s.29 (acts offences independent of harm: abortion cannot be justified by consent), s.30 (act in good faith for benefit without consent when it is impossible to get consent), s.31 (communication in good faith, e.g., doctor informing patient).
  10. Consent limits: no consent can justify causing death (except private defence, and surgery done in good faith with consent under s.26). Consent given by a person below 12 or of unsound mind is invalid. Consent for sexual acts: BNS rape provisions fix 18 years as age of consent.
  11. Compulsion (s.32): threat of instant death compels act, except murder and offences against the State punishable by death. Duress must be present, not future. Example: a person forced at gunpoint to join a robbery cannot claim s.32 if he voluntarily placed himself in the situation.
  12. Trifling harm (s.33): harm so slight that no person of ordinary sense and temper would complain (de minimis).
  13. Private defence (ss.34-44): right to defend one's own body and property and those of others against offences (s.34-35). No right against lawful acts of public servants acting in good faith under colour of office (s.37), except where there is reasonable apprehension of death or grievous hurt.
  14. Private defence of body extends to causing death only against: (1) assault causing reasonable apprehension of death, (2) grievous hurt, (3) rape, (4) [IPC 100 also listed an assault with intent to gratify unnatural lust; BNS omits it because IPC 377 was dropped] Verify, (5) kidnapping or abduction, (6) wrongful confinement with apprehension of inability to seek public aid, (7) acid attack (s.38).
  15. Private defence of property extends to causing death against robbery, house-breaking by night, arson (mischief by fire to human dwelling), theft/mischief/house-trespass causing apprehension of death or grievous hurt (s.41).
  16. Right commences when reasonable apprehension of danger arises and continues as long as apprehension continues. Right does not include inflicting more harm than necessary (s.34/35; excess leads to murder Exception 2 if death results).
  17. No right of private defence when there is time to have recourse to public authorities (s.37(4)).
  18. Section 44 (IPC 106): when the right of private defence of life is exercised against a deadly assault, the defender may run the risk of harm to an innocent person if he cannot otherwise exercise the right.

⚖Landmark cases

Darshan Singh v State of Punjab

2010

Supreme Court laid down ten guidelines on private defence: it is preventive not punitive, the accused need not prove it beyond reasonable doubt (preponderance suffices), danger must be real and imminent, and weapon injuries are assessed on the apprehension, not on precise measurement.

Vidhya Singh v State of MP

1971

Burden on accused to prove private defence is lighter; the right cannot be weighed in golden scales.

Jai Dev v State of Punjab

1963

Right of private defence cannot be exercised once the threat has ceased; right is available only to repel aggression.

Mohinder Pal Jolly v State of Punjab

1979

Firing at a mob, when the danger to property alone existed and no death apprehension, exceeds private defence.

Dahyabhai Chhaganbhai Thakkar v State of Gujarat

1964

Burden of proving insanity is on accused, but only to the standard of preponderance of probabilities; unsoundness must exist at the time of act.

Hari Singh Gond v State of MP

2008

Legal insanity vs. medical insanity: only cognitive incapacity at the time of the act matters.

Basdev v State of PEPSU

1956

Voluntary drunkenness is not a defence; intention must be assessed taking account of the effect of drink on the capacity to form it.

Dudley & Stephens

1884

Necessity is no defence to murder.

Tunda v Rex

1950

A blow in a sudden quarrel is not 'accident' where intent to cause injury is present.

Reg v Govinda

1876

Mistake of fact vs. intention in culpable homicide (classic exposition of s.299 vs 300).

!Exam traps

  • TrapMistake of fact is a defence; mistake of law is not.
  • TrapInsanity: the test is incapacity to know nature of act or that it is wrong/contrary to law; irresistible impulse and moral insanity are not enough.
  • TrapThe right of private defence never extends to causing more harm than necessary, and for property alone it extends to death only in the listed offences (robbery, house-breaking by night, arson, theft/mischief/trespass causing death/grievous hurt apprehension).
  • TrapNo private defence against lawful acts of public servants even if the act may not be strictly justifiable, if done in good faith under colour of office.
  • TrapCompulsion under s.32 is not a defence to murder or offences against the State punishable by death.
