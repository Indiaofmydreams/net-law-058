iOverview

Rape under BNS s.63: sexual intercourse with a woman in the seven circumstances

★Must know

  1. Rape under BNS s.63: sexual intercourse with a woman in the seven circumstances; penetration to any extent is sufficient; Explanation: penetration includes insertion of any object or a part of the body (not being the penis) into the vagina, urethra or anus; manipulation of body parts; application of mouth. Penetration suffices; emission or rupture of the hymen is not necessary (State of UP v Babul Nath, 1994).
  2. Consent: Explanation 2 to s.63: consent means an unequivocal voluntary agreement when the woman by words, gestures or any form of verbal or non-verbal communication communicates willingness to participate in the specific sexual act; a woman who does not physically resist shall not by the reason only of that fact be regarded as consenting. Cases: Uday v State of Karnataka (2003): consent obtained on a promise of marriage is 'under a misconception of fact' only if the promise was false from the start and the woman's decision was caused by it; Deepak Gulati v State of Haryana (2013): distinction between rape and consensual sex; the court must examine whether the accused had the intention to marry at the time; Pramod Suryabhan Pawar (2019); Naim Ahamed v State (NCT of Delhi) (2023): a long-standing consensual relationship cannot be converted into rape on a failed marriage promise; the court cautioned about the abuse of the rape law where parties were in a consensual relationship and the allegation arises after the breakdown Verify. BNS s.69 now separately criminalises a promise of marriage made with no intention to fulfil it.
  3. Prosecutrix testimony: the conviction can be based on the sole testimony of the prosecutrix if it inspires confidence (State of Punjab v Gurmit Singh (1996); State of UP v Chhoteylal (2011)); corroboration is not a rule of law but a rule of prudence; the court should not insist on medical evidence if the testimony is reliable; delay in lodging FIR is not fatal where explained (State of Himachal Pradesh v Shree Kant Shekari, 2004); a prior sexual history is irrelevant (BSA s.151(4); Evidence Act s.146 proviso) - questioning on the victim's character or previous sexual experience to establish consent is prohibited.
  4. 'Two-finger test' is unconstitutional and violates the right to privacy and dignity: Lillu @ Rajesh v State of Haryana (2013); State of Jharkhand v Shailendra Kumar Rai (2022) directed that those conducting it be treated as guilty of misconduct. Medical examination of the victim under BNSS 184 (CrPC 164A) is to be done by a registered medical practitioner with consent, within 24 hours of the receipt of information.
  5. Mathura rape case (Tukaram v State of Maharashtra, 1979) triggered the Criminal Law (Second Amendment) Act, 1983: introduction of s.376(2) aggravated rape (custodial rape), s.114A of the Evidence Act (presumption as to absence of consent), in camera trials and prohibition on disclosing victim identity; the Criminal Law (Amendment) Act 2013 (post-Nirbhaya) widened the definition, added acid attack, stalking, voyeurism, sexual harassment; the Criminal Law (Amendment) Act 2018 (Kathua and Unnao) added death penalty for rape of girls under 12 and minimum punishment of 20 years for girls below 16.
  6. Gang rape (s.70): where a woman is raped by one or more persons constituting a group or acting in furtherance of a common intention, each of those persons is deemed to have committed gang rape; no separate proof of individual penetration is needed; punishment 20 years to life (natural life) and a fine just and reasonable to meet medical expenses and rehabilitation of the victim, which shall be paid to the victim; gang rape of a woman under 18: life (natural life) or death. Mukesh (Nirbhaya, 2017): gang-rape and murder.
  7. Persons in authority and custody (s.64(2); s.68): rape by a police officer within the limits of the police station, public servant, member of armed forces deployed in an area, jail or remand home staff, management or staff of a hospital, a relative, guardian or teacher, a person in a position of trust or authority; committing rape during communal or sectarian violence; rape on a woman knowing her to be pregnant; rape of a woman incapable of giving consent; repeated rape of the same woman; causing grievous bodily harm, maiming, disfiguring or endangering life; rape of a mentally or physically disabled woman. Minimum 10 years, may extend to life for the remainder of natural life. Custodial rape: State of Maharashtra v Chandraprakash Kewalchand Jain (1990).
  8. Victim identity and compensation: s.72 (IPC 228A): disclosure of the name or any matter which may identify the victim is punishable with up to 2 years and fine; the exception is where disclosure is authorised by the victim in writing, by the next of kin if she is dead or a minor, or on the authority of the police officer investigating; the printing of court proceedings relating to such offences without the permission of the court is punishable under s.73. Nipun Saxena v Union of India (2018) extended the bar to all documents and held that even in judgments of the High Court the victim's name should not be disclosed; the next of kin cannot authorise disclosure except to a recognised welfare institution. Compensation: Nirbhaya Fund; Section 357A CrPC (BNSS 396) State schemes; the Supreme Court in Delhi Domestic Working Women's Forum v UoI (1995) directed the establishment of a Criminal Injuries Compensation Board.
  9. Protection of Children from Sexual Offences Act, 2012 (POCSO): applies to a 'child', any person below 18 years (s.2(d)); it is gender neutral; it covers penetrative sexual assault (s.3), punishable under s.4 with imprisonment not less than 10 years, extendable to life and fine (for a child below 16: not less than 20 years, extendable to life, meaning the remainder of natural life); aggravated penetrative sexual assault (s.5) punishable under s.6 with not less than 20 years to life or death (after the 2019 amendment); sexual assault (s.7), punishable under s.8 with 3 to 5 years; aggravated sexual assault (s.9) punishable under s.10 with 5 to 7 years; sexual harassment (s.11), punishable under s.12 with up to 3 years; use of child for pornographic purposes (s.13), punishable under s.14 with not less than 5 years (7 years on subsequent conviction); storage of pornographic material involving a child (s.15) with fines up to Rs 5,000 for first conviction, and 3 years for sharing, etc.
  10. POCSO procedure and presumptions: mandatory reporting of knowledge or apprehension of an offence (s.19); failure to report is punishable with up to 6 months (s.21); the Special Court (s.28); child-friendly procedures: statement of the child at the residence or a place of choice, by a woman sub-inspector, in plain clothes, with the support of a parent or person of the child's trust (s.24); no child shall be detained in the police station at night (s.24(4)); the identity of the child is to be protected (s.23); presumption as to the commission of the offence (s.29) and a presumption of culpable mental state (s.30), subject to the standard of proof beyond a reasonable doubt for the accused's rebuttal by preponderance; speedy trial: the Special Court is to complete the trial within one year (s.35(2)). Special Court may order interim compensation (s.33(8)).
  11. Key POCSO cases: Attorney General for India v Satish (2021): 'skin to skin contact' is not required for sexual assault under s.7; the touching of a child's body parts with sexual intent is sufficient; the Bombay High Court judgment (Pushpa Ganediwala) was set aside; Independent Thought (2017): the interplay between POCSO and the marital rape exception for a girl between 15 and 18; Just Rights for Children Alliance v S. Harish (2024): the Supreme Court held that storing, possessing or viewing child pornography is an offence under s.15 POCSO and s.67B of the IT Act even without transmission, and it directed that the term 'child sexual exploitative and abuse material (CSEAM)' replace 'child pornography' in law Verify; Sakshi v Union of India (2004): in-camera and screen-protected testimony for child victims; State of Rajasthan v Om Prakash (2002).
  12. Romantic relationships between adolescents: the Supreme Court and High Courts have noted the 'Romeo-Juliet' problem (consensual acts between persons aged 16-18) and recommended judicial sensitivity (Madras High Court in Vijaylakshmi v State (2021) suggested reconsidering the age of consent; Law Commission 283rd Report (2023) recommended retaining 18 years but providing judicial discretion in sentencing for tacit approval cases of 16-18 years) Verify. In Nipun Saxena and Independent Thought, the Court insisted on harmonising the Code with POCSO.
  13. Juvenile justice linkages: Juvenile Justice (Care and Protection of Children) Act, 2015: a 'child' is below 18; heinous offences (minimum sentence 7 years or more) by a child aged 16-18 may be tried as an adult after a preliminary assessment by the Juvenile Justice Board; serious offences (punishable with 3 to 7 years) and petty offences (up to 3 years); no death penalty or life imprisonment without possibility of release for children; Shilpa Mittal (2020); Mukesh v State: the juvenile in Nirbhaya was tried under the 2000 Act and given the maximum three years in a special home before the 2015 Act was enacted.
  14. Missing from BNS: the Sanhita omits the unnatural offences provision (IPC 377), which survived Navtej Singh Johar (2018) only for non-consensual acts, acts with minors and bestiality. This leaves male and transgender victims of non-consensual penetrative acts without a specific provision under the Sanhita (apart from other provisions like s.74-type assault) except POCSO for children; the Parliamentary Standing Committee had recommended retention in a gender-neutral form; the Government did not accept it. Hot topic for exam: Navtej Singh Johar decriminalised consensual same-sex acts between adults. The Supreme Court in Supriyo @ Supriya Chakraborty v UoI (2023) declined to recognise a right to same-sex marriage but recognised that the State must not discriminate and set up a committee on rights of queer couples. The Transgender Persons (Protection of Rights) Act, 2019 defines transgender persons and prescribes imprisonment of 6 months to 2 years for sexual abuse; BNS defines 'gender' to include a transgender person (s.2(10)).

⚖Landmark cases

State of Punjab v Gurmit Singh

1996

Sole testimony of a rape victim can ground a conviction; courts must be sensitive; in camera trial.

Lillu @ Rajesh v State of Haryana

2013

The 'two-finger test' violates dignity and privacy; consent cannot be inferred from it.

Nipun Saxena v Union of India

2018

Victim identity must be protected; guidelines on non-disclosure.

Attorney General for India v Satish

2021

Skin-to-skin contact not necessary for sexual assault under POCSO.

Just Rights for Children Alliance v S. Harish

2024

Viewing, storing child sexual exploitative material is an offence even without sharing.

Uday v State of Karnataka

2003

Promise to marry and consent: the misconception of fact must be proximate and the promise false at the inception.

Independent Thought v Union of India

2017

Sexual intercourse with a wife below 18 is rape; harmonious reading of POCSO and IPC.

Sakshi v Union of India

2004

Safeguards for testimony of child victims; broader reading of rape definition declined.

State of Maharashtra v Madhukar Narayan Mardikar

1991

Even a woman of easy virtue is entitled to privacy and cannot be raped by anyone.

Delhi Domestic Working Women's Forum v UoI

1995

Directions for legal assistance, anonymity, and compensation to rape victims.

!Exam traps

  • TrapPOCSO is gender neutral and applies to anyone below 18; consent of a child is irrelevant.
  • TrapSkin-to-skin contact is not required for POCSO sexual assault (Satish, 2021).
  • TrapThe victim's past sexual history is irrelevant; the 'two-finger test' is unconstitutional.
  • TrapMinimum punishment under POCSO s.4 is 10 years; for a child below 16, 20 years.
