Abetment, Criminal Conspiracy and Attempt (BNS ss.45-62)

High yield1 Qs expected5 min readBNS Ss. 45-62 (IPC Ss. 107-120B, 511)

  3. Landmark cases (8)

iOverview

Abetment (s.45; IPC 107): a person abets by (1) instigation, (2) engaging in a conspiracy (with an act or illegal omission in pursuance), (3) intentional aiding by act or illegal omission.

★Must know

  1. Abetment (s.45; IPC 107): a person abets by (1) instigation, (2) engaging in a conspiracy (with an act or illegal omission in pursuance), (3) intentional aiding by act or illegal omission. Explanations: wilful misrepresentation or wilful concealment of a material fact is instigation; facilitating commission of the act counts as aiding.
  2. Abettor (s.46): one who abets an offence or who abets the doing of an act that would be an offence if done by a person capable of law or with the same intent/knowledge. Abetment of abetment is also an offence. Abetment is complete even if the act abetted is not committed.
  3. Punishment (s.49; IPC 109): where the act abetted is committed in consequence and no express provision exists, the abettor gets the same punishment as for the offence.
  4. Abettor present when offence is committed (s.54; IPC 114) is deemed to have committed the offence.
  5. Abetment of offence punishable with death or life imprisonment (s.55; IPC 115): 7 years and fine if the offence is not committed; 14 years if hurt caused.
  6. Abetment of offence punishable with imprisonment (s.56; IPC 116): one-fourth of the longest term (half if the abettor is a public servant).
  7. Abetment by public (s.57; IPC 117): abetting commission of an offence by the public generally or by more than ten persons: imprisonment up to 3 years, fine, or both.
  8. Concealing design to commit offence punishable with death/life (s.58) and by public servant (s.59) (IPC 118, 119).
  9. Criminal conspiracy (s.61; IPC 120A-120B): agreement between two or more persons to do an illegal act or a legal act by illegal means. For agreement to commit an offence, no overt act is needed (except where the agreement is to do an illegal act that is not an offence; then an overt act is required). Punishment: same as abetment for serious offences; 6 months for other conspiracies.
  10. Conspiracy cases: Kehar Singh v State (Delhi Admin), 1988: conspiracy is rarely proved by direct evidence; inference from circumstances. Ram Narain Poply v CBI (2003): conspiracy is a continuing offence. State v Nalini (Rajiv Gandhi assassination, 1999): the court explained how acts of conspirators are admissible under the Evidence Act s.10 (BSA s.8). R. Venkatkrishnan v CBI (2009): mere knowledge is not agreement.
  11. Attempt (s.62; IPC 511): attempt to commit offences punishable with imprisonment (including life): up to half of the longest term for the offence, or the prescribed fine, or both. General attempt provision applies only where no specific attempt provision exists (e.g., attempt to murder in s.109).
  12. Tests of attempt: proximity test (Eagleton, 1855): last act necessary for the offence; locus poenitentiae; Indian courts follow the 'substantial step' or 'unequivocal act' approach. Om Prakash v State of Punjab (1961): attempt begins when preparation is complete and the person commences the actual act. State of Maharashtra v Mohd. Yakub (1980): carrying silver in a truck near the sea at night, held to be attempt to smuggle. Malkiat Singh v State of Punjab (1991): carrying paddy to the border in a truck is preparation, not attempt. Abhayanand Mishra: false application and attempt to cheat.
  13. Impossibility: attempt is punishable even if the act was impossible (pick-pocket with empty pocket: attempt to commit theft). Illustration under BNS 62 / s.511 IPC.
  14. Abetment of suicide: s.108 (IPC 306): up to 10 years and fine. s.107: abetment of suicide of a child or person of unsound mind: death or life or not less than 10 years. Explanations: need for active act of instigation (Gurcharan Singh v State of Punjab, 2020; Chitresh Kumar Chopra v State (NCT of Delhi), 2009; Amalendu Pal v State of WB, 2010; M. Arjunan v State, 2019). Mere harassment or insult is not instigation unless there is mens rea to push the person to suicide. Recent focus: Supreme Court insistence on proximity and clear mens rea, avoiding mechanical invocation (e.g., Mahendra Awase v State of MP, 2025) Verify.

⚖Landmark cases

Kehar Singh v State (Delhi Admin)

1988

Conspiracy: agreement is the gist; proof may be circumstantial; the conspiracy continues as long as the object is not achieved.

State v Nalini

1999

Rajiv Gandhi assassination: Evidence Act s.10 and the rule of agency in conspiracy.

Abhayanand Mishra v State of Bihar

1961

Attempt starts when preparation is complete and the first step is taken.

Malkiat Singh v State of Punjab

1991

Mere carrying of goods towards the border is preparation, not attempt.

Aman Kumar v State of Haryana

2004

Attempt to murder: an overt act showing proximity to the offence is required; injury is not necessary.

Gian Kaur v State of Punjab

1996

Right to life does not include right to die; abetment of suicide (and attempt, under then s.309) is constitutionally valid.

Ramesh Kumar v State of Chhattisgarh

2001

Instigation means to goad, urge forward, provoke or incite; wilful act required.

Sanju @ Sanjay Singh Sengar v State of MP

2002

A quarrel in which the accused said 'go and die' is not abetment without intention to bring about death.

!Exam traps

  • TrapAbetment of suicide requires positive instigation; harassment alone or one remark in a quarrel is insufficient.
  • TrapAttempt to suicide (IPC 309) no longer an offence in BNS, except the narrow BNS 226 (to compel or restrain public servant).
  • TrapConspiracy requires two or more persons; one cannot conspire alone, and a spouse-only conspiracy remains debated; courts accept that the two must be separately capable.
  • TrapSpecific attempt provisions (s.109 attempt to murder; s.110 attempt to commit culpable homicide) override the general s.62.
