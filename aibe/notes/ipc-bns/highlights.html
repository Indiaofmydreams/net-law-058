1

Fundamentals: Territorial Jurisdiction, Mens Rea, Stages of Crime, Definitions

High yieldRead the full chapter →

Cases in one line

  • State of Maharashtra v M.H. George 1965 Mens rea is not required where a statute (FERA/RBI notification) imposes strict liability; but mens rea is presumed unless excluded by clear words.
  • Nathulal v State of MP 1966 Mens rea is the essence of a crime; for storing foodgrains without licence, honest belief in compliance mattered.
  • Aher Raja Khima v State of Saurashtra 1955 Even where mens rea is a requirement, 'knowledge' includes the capacity to foresee consequences.
  • Abhayanand Mishra v State of Bihar 1961 Preparation ends and attempt begins when the accused commits the first overt act towards the offence (false representation in an application).

Exam traps

  • TrapPreparation is not punishable unless the Code says so; the first positive step towards the offence is attempt.
  • TrapA child is anyone under 18 under BNS, but the doli incapax age for criminal responsibility is still 7 (and 7-12 with immaturity).
  • Trap'Community service' appears only six times: do not select it as a punishment for ordinary theft or hurt.
2

General Exceptions (Chapter III, BNS ss.14-44)

High yieldRead the full chapter →

Cases in one line

  • Darshan Singh v State of Punjab 2010 Supreme Court laid down ten guidelines on private defence: it is preventive not punitive, the accused need not prove it beyond reasonable doubt (preponderance suffices), danger must be real and imminent, and weapon injuries are assessed on the apprehension, not on precise measurement.
  • Vidhya Singh v State of MP 1971 Burden on accused to prove private defence is lighter; the right cannot be weighed in golden scales.
  • Jai Dev v State of Punjab 1963 Right of private defence cannot be exercised once the threat has ceased; right is available only to repel aggression.
  • Mohinder Pal Jolly v State of Punjab 1979 Firing at a mob, when the danger to property alone existed and no death apprehension, exceeds private defence.
  • Dahyabhai Chhaganbhai Thakkar v State of Gujarat 1964 Burden of proving insanity is on accused, but only to the standard of preponderance of probabilities; unsoundness must exist at the time of act.
  • Hari Singh Gond v State of MP 2008 Legal insanity vs. medical insanity: only cognitive incapacity at the time of the act matters.
  • Basdev v State of PEPSU 1956 Voluntary drunkenness is not a defence; intention must be assessed taking account of the effect of drink on the capacity to form it.
  • Dudley & Stephens 1884 Necessity is no defence to murder.
  • Tunda v Rex 1950 A blow in a sudden quarrel is not 'accident' where intent to cause injury is present.
  • Reg v Govinda 1876 Mistake of fact vs. intention in culpable homicide (classic exposition of s.299 vs 300).

Exam traps

  • TrapMistake of fact is a defence; mistake of law is not.
  • TrapInsanity: the test is incapacity to know nature of act or that it is wrong/contrary to law; irresistible impulse and moral insanity are not enough.
  • TrapThe right of private defence never extends to causing more harm than necessary, and for property alone it extends to death only in the listed offences (robbery, house-breaking by night, arson, theft/mischief/trespass causing death/grievous hurt apprehension).
  • TrapNo private defence against lawful acts of public servants even if the act may not be strictly justifiable, if done in good faith under colour of office.
  • TrapCompulsion under s.32 is not a defence to murder or offences against the State punishable by death.
3

Joint and Constructive Liability: Common Intention (s.3(5)) and Unlawful Assembly / Common Object (s.190)

High yieldRead the full chapter →

Cases in one line

  • Barendra Kumar Ghosh v King Emperor 1925 Participation need not be in the commission of the act; standing and waiting (sentry) is participation.
  • Mahbub Shah v King-Emperor 1945 Common intention needs a prior meeting of minds; distinct from similar intention.
  • Pandurang v State of Hyderabad 1955 Common intention may develop at the spot but must precede the act; prior concert shown by circumstances.
  • Krishna Govind Patil v State of Maharashtra 1964 Section 34 applies only if common intention shown; where doubts exist, benefit goes to the accused.
  • Masalti v State of UP 1965 Where a large number of persons are accused, conviction under s.149 requires at least five proved to be members; the court should be cautious.
  • Lalji v State of UP 1989 Common object: liability under s.149 attaches to all members for offences likely in prosecution of the object; membership is a matter of fact.

Exam traps

  • TrapSection 3(5)/34 = common intention (any number from two); Section 190/149 = common object (five or more).
  • TrapMere presence at the scene does not make one liable under s.34, but in s.149 a member continues to be liable even if he did not actually commit the offence.
  • TrapSection 34 does not create a separate offence; s.149 does not either, but s.189/191 (unlawful assembly/rioting) do.
4

Abetment, Criminal Conspiracy and Attempt (BNS ss.45-62)

High yieldRead the full chapter →

Cases in one line

  • Kehar Singh v State (Delhi Admin) 1988 Conspiracy: agreement is the gist; proof may be circumstantial; the conspiracy continues as long as the object is not achieved.
  • State v Nalini 1999 Rajiv Gandhi assassination: Evidence Act s.10 and the rule of agency in conspiracy.
  • Abhayanand Mishra v State of Bihar 1961 Attempt starts when preparation is complete and the first step is taken.
  • Malkiat Singh v State of Punjab 1991 Mere carrying of goods towards the border is preparation, not attempt.
  • Aman Kumar v State of Haryana 2004 Attempt to murder: an overt act showing proximity to the offence is required; injury is not necessary.
  • Gian Kaur v State of Punjab 1996 Right to life does not include right to die; abetment of suicide (and attempt, under then s.309) is constitutionally valid.
  • Ramesh Kumar v State of Chhattisgarh 2001 Instigation means to goad, urge forward, provoke or incite; wilful act required.
  • Sanju @ Sanjay Singh Sengar v State of MP 2002 A quarrel in which the accused said 'go and die' is not abetment without intention to bring about death.

Exam traps

  • TrapAbetment of suicide requires positive instigation; harassment alone or one remark in a quarrel is insufficient.
  • TrapAttempt to suicide (IPC 309) no longer an offence in BNS, except the narrow BNS 226 (to compel or restrain public servant).
  • TrapConspiracy requires two or more persons; one cannot conspire alone, and a spouse-only conspiracy remains debated; courts accept that the two must be separately capable.
  • TrapSpecific attempt provisions (s.109 attempt to murder; s.110 attempt to commit culpable homicide) override the general s.62.

Culpable Homicide, Murder, Death by Negligence, Hit and Run, Mob Lynching, Organised Crime

High yieldRead the full chapter →

Cases in one line

  • Virsa Singh v State of Punjab 1958 Four-fold test for s.300 clause 3 (now BNS 101(c)): (i) injury present; (ii) injury of the kind intended; (iii) sufficient in ordinary course to cause death; (iv) injury inflicted was intended.
  • K.M. Nanavati v State of Maharashtra 1962 Grave and sudden provocation requires the reasonable man test, and the cooling-time test; discussed alongside the jury trial system.
  • State of Andhra Pradesh v R. Punnayya 1976 Method for distinguishing culpable homicide and murder: determine whether it is culpable homicide, then whether any clause of s.300 applies.
  • Bachan Singh v State of Punjab 1980 Death penalty constitutional; applied only in the rarest of rare cases after balancing aggravating and mitigating circumstances.
  • Machhi Singh v State of Punjab 1983 Categories of rarest of rare: manner of commission, motive, anti-social nature, magnitude, personality of victim.
  • Mithu v State of Punjab 1983 Mandatory death sentence under IPC 303 violates Articles 14 and 21.
  • Jacob Mathew v State of Punjab 2005 Gross negligence (not ordinary) is needed for criminal liability of professionals; guidelines on prosecution of doctors.
  • Alister Anthony Pareira v State of Maharashtra 2012 Drunken driving causing deaths: s.304A vs 304 Part II; knowledge may be inferred where driving was grossly reckless.
  • Kans Raj v State of Punjab 2000 'Soon before her death' requires a proximate and live link between cruelty and death.
  • Satbir Singh v State of Haryana 2021 Dowry death: presumption under s.113B and interplay of ss.304B and 498A.
  • Reg v Govinda 1876 Distinction between culpable homicide and murder; intention to cause a bodily injury likely to cause death.
  • Mukesh v State (NCT of Delhi) 2017 Death penalty for Nirbhaya gang-rape and murder, rarest of rare.

Exam traps

  • TrapMurder = culpable homicide + one of four clauses; not every death by intent is murder if an exception applies.
  • TrapThe five Exceptions are tested directly: provocation, private defence excess, public servant, sudden fight, consent.
  • TrapSection 304A (negligence) is outside the s.100 to 101 family; it covers rash or negligent acts that do not amount to culpable homicide.
  • TrapMob lynching: five or more persons, listed grounds, punishment death or life (BNS 103(2)); students confuse it with unlawful assembly rioting.
  • TrapDowry death minimum is 7 years; punishment may go to life.
  • TrapOrganised crime fine minimum: Rs 10 lakh if death; Rs 5 lakh otherwise.

Hurt, Grievous Hurt, Acid Attack, Wrongful Restraint/Confinement, Force, Assault

Medium yieldRead the full chapter →

Exam traps

  • Trap'Grievous hurt' list is exhaustive (eight kinds); fracture or dislocation of a bone or tooth counts even if it heals.
  • Trap20-day rule: severe bodily pain or inability to follow ordinary pursuits for 20 days.
  • TrapMere words never amount to assault; a gesture plus apprehension is required.
  • TrapAcid attack minimum: 10 years; throwing/attempt: 5 to 7 years.

Offences against Women and Children (Chapter V): Rape, Sexual Harassment, Stalking, Voyeurism, Cruelty, Kidnapping, Trafficking

High yieldRead the full chapter →

Cases in one line

  • Tukaram v State of Maharashtra (Mathura rape case) 1979 Acquittal on 'consent' reasoning led to 1983 amendments: presumption of no consent on proof of sexual intercourse where victim says she did not consent (BSA s.120).
  • State of Punjab v Gurmit Singh 1996 Conviction in rape may rest on sole testimony of the prosecutrix; courts must be sensitive; in-camera trials.
  • Independent Thought v Union of India 2017 Exception 2 to s.375 read down: sexual intercourse with a wife under 18 is rape.
  • Mukesh v State (NCT of Delhi) 2017 Death sentence confirmed for Nirbhaya convicts.
  • Vishaka v State of Rajasthan 1997 Guidelines against sexual harassment at workplace; led to the 2013 Act.
  • Rupan Deol Bajaj v KPS Gill 1995 Modesty of a woman: the ultimate test is whether the action offends the sense of decency of a woman.
  • Sarla Mudgal v Union of India 1995 Second marriage after conversion remains bigamy.
  • Joseph Shine v Union of India 2018 Adultery (IPC 497) unconstitutional; women not chattel.
  • Arnesh Kumar v State of Bihar 2014 498A arrests must follow checklist under s.41 CrPC (now BNSS 35); no mechanical arrest.
  • Laxmi v Union of India 2014 Acid sale regulation and compensation for acid-attack victims.
  • Pramod Suryabhan Pawar v State of Maharashtra 2019 Consent obtained on a false promise of marriage vitiates consent only if the promise was false from inception.
  • S. Varadarajan v State of Madras 1965 Taking is not the same as allowing a minor to accompany; kidnapping requires active inducement.

Exam traps

  • TrapAge of consent for sexual intercourse is 18 years; exception for wife is also 18 (Independent Thought).
  • TrapFalse promise of marriage: BNS 69 is now a standalone offence (up to 10 years), distinct from rape.
  • TrapStalking, voyeurism and harassment: remember repeat-offender enhancements.
  • TrapCruelty under s.85 has two limbs (health/suicide-driving and dowry harassment).
  • TrapGang rape of a minor under 18: life (natural life) or death; for adult woman: 20 years to life.
  • TrapDisclosure of victim's identity is an offence (up to 2 years and fine), including through publication of court records without leave.

Offences against the State, Public Tranquillity, Hate Speech and Religion

Medium yieldRead the full chapter →

Cases in one line

  • Kedar Nath Singh v State of Bihar 1962 Sedition limited to acts having a tendency to create public disorder or incitement to violence.
  • Shreya Singhal v Union of India 2015 Incitement vs discussion and advocacy; s.66A IT Act struck down as vague.
  • Amish Devgan v Union of India 2020 Hate speech test: effect on the targeted group, context, intention; reasonable person standard.
  • Ramji Lal Modi v State of UP 1957 s.295A upheld: only deliberate and malicious insult to religion is punishable.
  • Arup Bhuyan v State of Assam 2011 Mere membership of a prohibited organisation is not an offence without active incitement or violence.
  • Masalti v State of UP 1965 Unlawful assembly and the five-member rule.

Exam traps

  • TrapBNS has no section titled 'sedition'; s.152 is the corresponding offence with a wider textual reach (secession, armed rebellion, subversive activities, electronic communication) and life imprisonment or up to 7 years.
  • TrapSection 153A requires promotion of enmity between GROUPS on specified grounds; 295A requires deliberate and malicious intent to outrage RELIGIOUS feelings of a class.

Offences against Property: Theft, Snatching, Extortion, Robbery, Dacoity, Criminal Misappropriation, Criminal Breach of Trust, Cheating, Stolen Property, Mischief, Trespass

High yieldRead the full chapter →

Cases in one line

  • Pyare Lal Bhargava v State of Rajasthan 1963 Temporary removal of a file to copy it is theft if dishonest intention exists.
  • K.N. Mehra v State of Rajasthan 1957 Taking a plane with dishonest intention is theft even if the property is to be returned later.
  • R.K. Dalmia v Delhi Administration 1962 CBT by a person with dominion over the property of a company; 'property' needs no specific identification.
  • Velji Raghavji Patel v State of Maharashtra 1965 A partner cannot be liable for CBT of partnership property unless a specific entrustment is shown; partners are co-owners.
  • Hridaya Ranjan Prasad Verma v State of Bihar 2000 Fraudulent or dishonest intention at the inception is necessary for cheating; breach of contract is a civil wrong.
  • Iridium India Telecom Ltd v Motorola 2011 A company can be prosecuted for offences requiring mens rea through its directing mind.
  • Sunil Bharti Mittal v CBI 2015 Directors cannot be vicariously liable unless alter ego or active role is established.
  • Dr Vimla v Delhi Administration 1963 Distinction between 'dishonestly' and 'fraudulently'; fraud does not require wrongful gain or loss.
  • Mohd Ibrahim v State of Bihar 2009 Execution of a document by a person claiming title that he does not hold is not forgery; false documents must claim to be executed by another.

Exam traps

  • TrapRobbery is an aggravated form of theft or extortion; dacoity is robbery by five or more persons.
  • TrapIn CBT the property is ENTRUSTED; in criminal misappropriation the property comes into possession innocently; in theft it is taken out of possession.
  • TrapCheating requires deception at the inception; later failure to perform a promise is not cheating.
  • TrapSnatching is a new offence: up to 3 years and fine.
  • TrapDacoity with murder: death, life or rigorous imprisonment 10 years or more.
  • TrapTheft of immovable property is not theft; but severed things become movable.

Defamation, Criminal Intimidation, Insult, Obscenity, Public Nuisance, False Evidence, Public Health and Safety

Medium yieldRead the full chapter →

Cases in one line

  • Subramanian Swamy v Union of India 2016 Criminal defamation under IPC 499 and 500 is constitutional; reputation is part of Article 21.
  • Aveek Sarkar v State of West Bengal 2014 Hicklin test replaced by the community standards test; Boris Becker photograph not obscene.
  • Ranjit Udeshi v State of Maharashtra 1965 Lady Chatterley's Lover; Hicklin test applied; obscenity assessed by tendency to deprave and corrupt.
  • Bobby Art International v Om Pal Singh Hoon 1996 Context and message of the film: nudity in the context of a social message is not obscene.
  • Shreya Singhal v UoI 2015 Section 66A (offensive messages) unconstitutional; incitement as against advocacy.
  • Iqbal Singh Marwah v Meenakshi Marwah 2005 Bar under s.195(1)(b)(ii) CrPC applies where the forgery occurs after the document is produced in court.

Exam traps

  • TrapDefamation: truth is a defence ONLY if published for the public good (Exception 1).
  • TrapIntimidation requires a threat with intent to cause alarm; mere abusive words are insult under s.352 only if intended to provoke breach of the peace.
  • TrapObscenity today: community standards test, not the Hicklin test.
  • TrapFalse evidence is punishable only if the person is legally bound to state the truth.
11

Transition from IPC to BNS: Temporal Application, Savings and Constitutional Limits

High yieldRead the full chapter →

Cases in one line

  • Rattan Lal v State of Punjab 1964 Ameliorative legislation (reducing punishment or giving probation) can be applied retrospectively to the accused's benefit; Art. 20(1) protects only against harsher penalties.
  • T. Barai v Henry Ah Hoe 1983 A reduction of punishment by a later law operates for the benefit of the accused where the prosecution is still pending.
  • Soni Devrajbhai Babubhai v State of Gujarat 1991 Art. 20(1) forbids enhanced sentence by a later law; the maximum is that applicable on the date of the offence.
  • Ravinder Singh v State of Haryana 1975 Penalty in force at the time of the offence governs, even if the law subsequently changed.
  • Maru Ram v Union of India 1981 Constitutional validity of retrospective remission restrictions: Art. 20(1) read with Art. 21; the benefit of the then-existing remission regime.

Exam traps

  • TrapA murder committed on 30 June 2024 is tried under IPC 302 even if the trial happens in 2026; one on 1 July 2024 is tried under BNS 103.
  • TrapArt. 20(1) bars retrospective criminal laws and enhanced punishment; it does not bar retrospective ameliorative provisions.
  • TrapPending proceedings under CrPC continue under CrPC by virtue of BNSS s.531.

Punishments in Depth: Death, Life Imprisonment, Remission, Fine, Forfeiture and Community Service

High yieldRead the full chapter →

Cases in one line

  • Gopal Vinayak Godse v State of Maharashtra 1961 Life imprisonment means imprisonment for the whole of the remaining life unless remitted by the Government.
  • Mohd. Munna v Union of India 2005 Life imprisonment is not 14 or 20 years; remission is at the Government's discretion.
  • Swamy Shraddananda (2) v State of Karnataka 2008 Courts may order life imprisonment without remission as a middle path between death and ordinary life.
  • Union of India v V. Sriharan 2015 Constitution Bench: modified sentences (fixed term or no remission) valid only if imposed by High Court or Supreme Court.
  • Sunil Batra v Delhi Administration 1978 Prison reforms; solitary confinement on death-sentenced prisoners unconstitutional; Art. 21 survives incarceration.
  • Shatrughan Chauhan v Union of India 2014 Inordinate delay in executing the death sentence and supervening mental illness are grounds for commutation.
  • Santosh Kumar Satishbhushan Bariyar v State of Maharashtra 2009 Death penalty requires a full assessment of the offender; sentencing hearing; cannot follow only the crime test.
  • Ankush Shivaji Gaikwad v State of Maharashtra 2013 Courts should consider victim compensation in every criminal case.
  • Deena v Union of India 1983 Hanging as a mode of execution is constitutional; Lachma Devi (1986) prohibits public hanging.

Exam traps

  • TrapLife imprisonment = whole life (subject to remission), not 20 years; 20 years is only for fractions of terms.
  • TrapCommunity service is not a free-standing punishment for all offences; only six provisions.
  • TrapCommutation under s.5 can be done by the appropriate Government without the offender's consent; Presidential and Governor's powers under Arts. 72/161 are constitutional and independent.

Criminal Liability Doctrines: Mens Rea, Strict Liability, Vicarious and Corporate Liability, Omissions, Causation

Medium yieldRead the full chapter →

Cases in one line

  • Sherras v De Rutzen 1895 Presumption in favour of mens rea can be displaced only by clear words or necessary implication.
  • State of Maharashtra v M.H. George 1965 Strict liability under FERA; mens rea excluded where statute's object requires it.
  • Maksud Saiyed v State of Gujarat 2008 Directors' vicarious liability for the Company's IPC offences requires specific allegation of role; no automatic liability.
  • Standard Chartered Bank v Directorate of Enforcement 2005 A company may be prosecuted and fined even for offences carrying mandatory imprisonment.
  • Iridium India Telecom v Motorola 2011 The company is liable for mens rea offences through the person in control (identification doctrine).
  • Aneeta Hada v Godfather Travels & Tours 2012 The company must be arraigned to prosecute its directors by vicarious liability.
  • Kurban Hussein v State of Maharashtra 1965 For rash or negligent act under s.304A, the act must be the proximate and efficient cause; remote cause insufficient.
  • Anda v State of Rajasthan 1966 Intention is inferred from the circumstances such as the number, nature and place of injuries.

Exam traps

  • TrapVicarious liability in IPC/BNS offences is not recognised unless the statute provides it; but corporate bodies themselves can be prosecuted.
  • TrapRashness involves consciousness of risk; negligence is want of reasonable care.
  • TrapIllegal omission needs a legal duty; a moral duty to rescue is not enough.

Death Penalty and Sentencing Jurisprudence: Rarest of Rare, Mitigation, Mercy Petitions

High yieldRead the full chapter →

Cases in one line

  • Jagmohan Singh v State of UP 1973 Death penalty constitutionally valid; the choice rests on judicial discretion.
  • Rajendra Prasad v State of UP 1979 Special reasons must relate to the offender, not just the crime.
  • Bachan Singh v State of Punjab 1980 Rarest of rare; aggravating and mitigating circumstances; life is the rule, death the exception.
  • Machhi Singh v State of Punjab 1983 Five categories for application of rarest of rare.
  • Mithu v State of Punjab 1983 Mandatory death sentence violates Arts. 14 and 21.
  • Triveniben v State of Gujarat 1989 Delay in execution is not an automatic ground for commutation; courts may review in light of Art. 21.
  • Shatrughan Chauhan v Union of India 2014 Unexplained delay, mental illness and other supervening circumstances justify commutation.
  • Mohd. Arif v Registrar, Supreme Court 2014 Death-sentence review petitions are heard in open court by three judges.
  • Manoj v State of MP 2022 Trial courts must collect mitigation material (psychiatric and psychological reports, prison conduct, social background) before awarding death.
  • Epuru Sudhakar v State of Andhra Pradesh 2006 Exercise of mercy power is subject to limited judicial review for arbitrariness and mala fides.

Exam traps

  • TrapBachan Singh laid down the doctrine; Machhi Singh gave the categories; Mithu struck down the mandatory death penalty.
  • TrapMercy power is exercised on the advice of the Council of Ministers; judicial review is limited.
  • TrapJuveniles (below 18) cannot be given the death penalty.

Murder, Culpable Homicide and Allied Offences: Advanced Doctrines and Case Analysis

High yieldRead the full chapter →

Cases in one line

  • Pulicherla Nagaraju v State of Andhra Pradesh 2006 Factors to decide whether the case is murder or culpable homicide not amounting to murder: nature of weapon, part of body, force, premeditation.
  • Rajwant Singh v State of Kerala 1966 Third clause of s.300: intended injury objectively sufficient in the ordinary course of nature.
  • Ghapoo Yadav v State of MP 2003 Grave and sudden provocation test applies to the reasonable man of the same class; provocation must be sudden.
  • Surinder Kumar v UT Chandigarh 1989 Sudden fight: no undue advantage or cruelty; the offender must not have taken unfair advantage.
  • Gurmukh Singh v State of Haryana 2009 Exception 4 to s.300 explained: heat of passion, sudden quarrel, no premeditation.
  • Aruna Ramchandra Shanbaug v Union of India 2011 Passive euthanasia permitted with court supervision in the absence of a law; active euthanasia is illegal.
  • Common Cause v Union of India 2018 Right to die with dignity is part of Art. 21; advance directives and 'living wills' recognised.
  • Kans Raj v State of Punjab 2000 'Soon before' requires proximity; cruelty for dowry must be established.
  • Hira Lal v State (Govt of NCT Delhi) 2003 304B and 498A: the court must examine the existence of cruelty and the presumption in section 113B of the Evidence Act.

Exam traps

  • TrapException 5 (consent) requires the deceased to be above 18 years.
  • TrapA person who provokes the fight or takes undue advantage cannot claim Exception 4.
  • TrapExplanation 3 to s.100: a child in the womb does not count as a human being unless a part is born.
16

Marriage, Dowry and Domestic Cruelty: BNS ss.80-86 with Dowry Prohibition Act and Domestic Violence Act

High yieldRead the full chapter →

Cases in one line

  • Sarla Mudgal v Union of India 1995 Conversion to Islam does not dissolve the first marriage; second marriage is void and bigamy is attracted.
  • Lily Thomas v Union of India 2000 Bigamy cannot be escaped by conversion; the Court recognised the need for a uniform civil code.
  • Bhaurao Shankar Lokhande v State of Maharashtra 1965 'Solemnised' in the bigamy provision means performed with due ceremonies; mere proof of marriage ceremony is required.
  • Arnesh Kumar v State of Bihar 2014 No automatic arrest under 498A; police must record reasons; magistrate must examine reasons for detention.
  • Preeti Gupta v State of Jharkhand 2010 Courts should be careful about exaggerated or omnibus allegations in matrimonial cases.
  • Kahkashan Kausar @ Sonam v State of Bihar 2022 Proceedings against relatives based on general allegations are an abuse of process.
  • Rajinder Singh v State of Punjab 2015 'Soon before her death' depends on facts; proximity and live link are required.
  • Hiral P. Harsora v Kusum Narottamdas Harsora 2016 The words 'adult male' in s.2(q) of the DV Act struck down; women relatives can be respondents.
  • S.R. Batra v Taruna Batra 2007 'Shared household' meaning narrowly interpreted; later reversed in Satish Chander Ahuja (2020).
  • Pratibha Rani v Suraj Kumar 1985 Stridhan is the wife's exclusive property; refusal to return amounts to criminal breach of trust.
  • Indra Sarma v V.K.V. Sarma 2013 Live-in relationships and the 'relationship in the nature of marriage' guidelines.
  • Rajnesh v Neha 2020 Guidelines on interim maintenance: affidavit of disclosure, overlapping jurisdiction, arrears.

Exam traps

  • TrapDowry death needs death within SEVEN years of marriage; abetment-of-suicide presumption (BSA s.117) also uses seven years.
  • TrapMinimum sentence for dowry death is 7 years; minimum for taking dowry (Dowry Prohibition Act s.3) is 5 years and fine of Rs 15,000 or the value of the dowry, whichever is more.
  • TrapCruelty under 498A has two limbs; both are in s.86 BNS.
  • TrapAdultery is no longer an offence; bigamy remains.

Sexual Offences: Evidence, Consent, Child Victims (POCSO), Victim Protection and Recent Law

High yieldRead the full chapter →

Cases in one line

  • State of Punjab v Gurmit Singh 1996 Sole testimony of a rape victim can ground a conviction; courts must be sensitive; in camera trial.
  • Lillu @ Rajesh v State of Haryana 2013 The 'two-finger test' violates dignity and privacy; consent cannot be inferred from it.
  • Nipun Saxena v Union of India 2018 Victim identity must be protected; guidelines on non-disclosure.
  • Attorney General for India v Satish 2021 Skin-to-skin contact not necessary for sexual assault under POCSO.
  • Just Rights for Children Alliance v S. Harish 2024 Viewing, storing child sexual exploitative material is an offence even without sharing.
  • Uday v State of Karnataka 2003 Promise to marry and consent: the misconception of fact must be proximate and the promise false at the inception.
  • Independent Thought v Union of India 2017 Sexual intercourse with a wife below 18 is rape; harmonious reading of POCSO and IPC.
  • Sakshi v Union of India 2004 Safeguards for testimony of child victims; broader reading of rape definition declined.
  • State of Maharashtra v Madhukar Narayan Mardikar 1991 Even a woman of easy virtue is entitled to privacy and cannot be raped by anyone.
  • Delhi Domestic Working Women's Forum v UoI 1995 Directions for legal assistance, anonymity, and compensation to rape victims.

Exam traps

  • TrapPOCSO is gender neutral and applies to anyone below 18; consent of a child is irrelevant.
  • TrapSkin-to-skin contact is not required for POCSO sexual assault (Satish, 2021).
  • TrapThe victim's past sexual history is irrelevant; the 'two-finger test' is unconstitutional.
  • TrapMinimum punishment under POCSO s.4 is 10 years; for a child below 16, 20 years.
18

Property Offences Advanced: Theft, CBT, Cheating, Forgery and Mischief in Practice

High yieldRead the full chapter →

Cases in one line

  • Jaswantrai Manilal Akhaney v State of Bombay 1956 CBT by bank director: 'dominion over property' suffices; entrustment can arise from the position.
  • Sudhir Shantilal Mehta v CBI 2009 Distinguished CBT and cheating; the relevance of intention at the inception.
  • Delhi Race Club (1940) Ltd v State of UP 2024 Cheating and CBT are antithetical on the same set of facts; civil disputes should not be criminalised.
  • Indian Oil Corporation v NEPC India Ltd 2006 Deprecated criminalisation of commercial disputes and the filing of criminal cases to put pressure on the other party.
  • Sarabjit Kaur v State of Punjab 2023 Breach of contract does not amount to cheating without fraudulent intention at the inception.
  • Chandi Kumar Das Karmarkar v Abanidra Kumar Das 1972 Bona fide claim of right negatives dishonest intention in theft.
  • In Re: Destruction of Public and Private Properties v State of AP 2009 Guidelines on liability for damage to public property in riots and agitations.
  • Pratibha Rani v Suraj Kumar 1985 Stridhan misappropriation is CBT.
  • Avtar Singh v State of Punjab 2002 Electricity theft is governed by the Electricity Act; IPC is displaced by the special law in that field.

Exam traps

  • TrapTheft of electricity is governed by the Electricity Act, 2003 s.135, not the IPC/BNS theft provision.
  • TrapEntrustment is the dividing line between CBT and criminal misappropriation.
  • TrapCheating at inception vs breach of contract: the latter is a civil wrong.
  • TrapA servant's possession of master's goods is the master's possession: taking by servant is theft by servant (s.306) unless entrusted.

Public Servants, Contempt of Lawful Authority, False Evidence and Public Justice (Chapters X-XIII in Practice)

Medium yieldRead the full chapter →

Cases in one line

  • Anuradha Bhasin v Union of India 2020 Restrictions under s.144 must be proportional, reasoned and reviewable; internet access linked to Arts. 19(1)(a) and 19(1)(g).
  • Iqbal Singh Marwah v Meenakshi Marwah 2005 Bar on cognizance under s.195(1)(b)(ii) CrPC applies only to documents forged after production in court.
  • Pritish v State of Maharashtra 2002 Court must form a prima facie opinion that it is expedient in the interest of justice to initiate perjury proceedings.
  • Lily Thomas v Union of India 2013 Section 8(4) RPA struck down; a legislator convicted with two years or more is disqualified immediately.
  • Abhiram Singh v C.D. Commachen 2017 Appeal for votes on the ground of religion, race, caste, community or language of anyone is a corrupt practice.
  • Union of India v Association for Democratic Reforms 2002 Voters have the right to know about candidates' antecedents.

Exam traps

  • TrapPerjury prosecution generally requires the court's complaint; private complaint is barred under the Code of Criminal Procedure/BNSS.
  • TrapGiving false evidence requires that the person be legally bound to state the truth.
  • TrapHarbouring by a spouse is excepted.
