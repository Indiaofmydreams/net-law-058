Death Penalty and Sentencing Jurisprudence: Rarest of Rare, Mitigation, Mercy Petitions

iOverview

Jagmohan Singh v State of UP (1973): the Supreme Court upheld the death penalty under Arts.

★Must know

  1. Jagmohan Singh v State of UP (1973): the Supreme Court upheld the death penalty under Arts. 14, 19 and 21, saying sentencing discretion is guided by judicial discretion after the trial. Rajendra Prasad v State of UP (1979): death only in extraordinary cases where the community cannot be protected; individual-centric. Bachan Singh v State of Punjab (1980): Constitution Bench (4:1) upheld the death penalty, but only in the 'rarest of rare' cases when the alternative of life imprisonment is unquestionably foreclosed; the court must weigh aggravating and mitigating circumstances (the 'balance sheet' approach), with the mitigating being generously considered. Dissent: Justice P.N. Bhagwati (1982 reported) found the death penalty unconstitutional.
  2. Machhi Singh v State of Punjab (1983): five categories - manner of commission (extreme brutality), motive (depravity), anti-social or socially abhorrent nature (e.g., bride burning or dowry death), magnitude (multiple murders), personality of the victim (child, helpless woman, public figure). Both tests: the 'crime test' and 'criminal test' are to be satisfied (Sangeet v State of Haryana, 2013 criticised the categorisation approach).
  3. Santosh Kumar Bariyar (2009): the court must consider the circumstances of the criminal as well as the crime; the 'rarest of rare' test is not subjective. Sangeet v State of Haryana (2013): the aggravating and mitigating circumstances cannot be balanced in the abstract; the court must look at the offender. Manoj v State of Madhya Pradesh (2022): three-judge Bench laid down that the trial court must call for psychiatric and psychological evaluation reports, prison conduct reports and a social investigation report before sentencing in capital cases; sentencing is a separate, individualised exercise. Irfan v State of MP (2023) followed Manoj on mitigation; a Constitution Bench reference on the procedure for death-sentencing (In Re: Framing Guidelines Regarding Potential Mitigating Circumstances) has been pending Verify · current status.
  4. Mithu v State of Punjab (1983): mandatory death penalty under IPC s.303 is unconstitutional (Arts. 14 and 21). Subsequent cases struck down mandatory death penalty in other statutes (e.g., Section 27(3) Arms Act in State of Punjab v Dalbir Singh, 2012; NDPS s.31A in Indian Harm Reduction Network v UoI, 2011 (Bombay HC) read down).
  5. Mercy petition and delay: Triveniben v State of Gujarat (1989): delay may be a ground to commute but only where it is inordinate and unexplained; Shatrughan Chauhan v UoI (2014): inordinate delay in disposal of mercy petitions, solitary confinement, mental illness - grounds for commutation; Vatheeswaran v State of TN (1983) delay of two years sufficient (overruled in Sher Singh and in Triveniben). The President and Governor exercise the mercy power on the aid and advice of the Council of Ministers (Maru Ram v UoI, 1981; Kehar Singh v UoI, 1989: the President can examine the evidence afresh; the courts cannot review on merits, only on limited grounds: Epuru Sudhakar v State of AP, 2006).
  6. Review and curative petitions: Mohd. Arif @ Ashfaq v Registrar, Supreme Court (2014): review petitions in death cases are to be heard in open court by a Bench of at least three judges; execution is stayed pending. Yakub Memon (2015), Nirbhaya (2020) - curative petitions and the use of 'serial' mercy petitions.
  7. Aggravating circumstances commonly accepted: murder of a child, rape-murder, premeditated multiple murders, murder for gain, torture and mutilation, murder of a public servant, terrorism; mitigating: young age (though not decisive), no prior record, probability of reformation, good conduct in jail, mental or emotional disturbance, circumstantial evidence only (the courts have repeatedly said that a death sentence on circumstantial evidence requires extra caution, but it is not a bar: Bishnu Prasad Sinha v State of Assam, 2007).
  8. Executions and notable post-2000 cases: Dhananjoy Chatterjee (2004, executed for rape-murder), Ajmal Kasab (2012 SC; executed Nov 2012), Afzal Guru (2013), Yakub Memon (2015), the four Nirbhaya convicts (March 2020). Recent trend: the Supreme Court has commuted several death sentences into fixed-term life imprisonment, emphasising reformation and mitigation Verify · recent cases.
  9. Constitutional questions pending: whether the method of hanging violates dignity (Rishi Malhotra's PIL, 2023 - SC asked the Union to examine alternatives such as lethal injection) Verify · status. Statistics: Project 39A's annual report on death penalty in India (national law university) is the standard source; trial courts impose large numbers, the Supreme Court confirms very few.
  10. AIBE angle: questions typically ask: which case laid down the rarest of rare doctrine; which struck down mandatory death sentence; who may commute; what is the effect of delay; the mode of execution; whether juvenile can be sentenced to death (no).

⚖Landmark cases

Jagmohan Singh v State of UP

1973

Death penalty constitutionally valid; the choice rests on judicial discretion.

Rajendra Prasad v State of UP

1979

Special reasons must relate to the offender, not just the crime.

Bachan Singh v State of Punjab

1980

Rarest of rare; aggravating and mitigating circumstances; life is the rule, death the exception.

Machhi Singh v State of Punjab

1983

Five categories for application of rarest of rare.

Mithu v State of Punjab

1983

Mandatory death sentence violates Arts. 14 and 21.

Triveniben v State of Gujarat

1989

Delay in execution is not an automatic ground for commutation; courts may review in light of Art. 21.

Shatrughan Chauhan v Union of India

2014

Unexplained delay, mental illness and other supervening circumstances justify commutation.

Mohd. Arif v Registrar, Supreme Court

2014

Death-sentence review petitions are heard in open court by three judges.

Manoj v State of MP

2022

Trial courts must collect mitigation material (psychiatric and psychological reports, prison conduct, social background) before awarding death.

Epuru Sudhakar v State of Andhra Pradesh

2006

Exercise of mercy power is subject to limited judicial review for arbitrariness and mala fides.

!Exam traps

  • TrapBachan Singh laid down the doctrine; Machhi Singh gave the categories; Mithu struck down the mandatory death penalty.
  • TrapMercy power is exercised on the advice of the Council of Ministers; judicial review is limited.
  • TrapJuveniles (below 18) cannot be given the death penalty.
