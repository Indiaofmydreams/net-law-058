Culpable Homicide, Murder, Death by Negligence, Hit and Run, Mob Lynching, Organised Crime

iOverview

Culpable homicide (BNS 100; IPC 299): causing death by an act done (a) with the intention of causing death, or (b) with the intention of causing such bodily injury as is likely to cause death, or (c) with the knowledge that the act is likely to cause death.

★Must know

  1. Culpable homicide (BNS 100; IPC 299): causing death by an act done (a) with the intention of causing death, or (b) with the intention of causing such bodily injury as is likely to cause death, or (c) with the knowledge that the act is likely to cause death.
  2. Murder (BNS 101; IPC 300): culpable homicide is murder if the act is done (1) with intention of causing death; (2) with intention of causing bodily injury the offender knows likely to cause death of the person harmed; (3) with intention of causing bodily injury sufficient in the ordinary course of nature to cause death; (4) with knowledge that the act is so imminently dangerous that it must in all probability cause death or such injury, and without any excuse for incurring the risk.
  3. Exceptions to murder (culpable homicide not amounting to murder): (1) grave and sudden provocation; (2) exceeding the right of private defence in good faith; (3) public servant exceeding powers for public good; (4) sudden fight in the heat of passion without premeditation and without undue advantage or cruelty; (5) consent of the victim above 18 years.
  4. Provocation tests (Nanavati, 1962): the provocation must be grave and sudden; the reasonable man would be so provoked; the accused must have been deprived of self-control; no time to cool down; the act must be done under the heat of passion. Nanavati: killing paramour after cooling time was murder; the Governor's pardon and jury system are historical notes.
  5. Difference between s.100 (culpable homicide) and s.101 (murder): degree of probability of death. Culpable homicide is the genus and murder the species (Reg v Govinda; State of AP v R. Punnayya, 1976). Clause (3) of s.300 (Virsa Singh v State of Punjab, 1958): injury intended + sufficient in ordinary course of nature to cause death: objective inquiry; accused need not know the injury would be fatal.
  6. Transferred malice (s.101 illustration; IPC 301 = BNS 102): if A intends to kill B but kills C, A is liable as if he had killed B (culpable homicide and murder principles apply).
  7. Punishment for murder (s.103(1)): death or imprisonment for life and fine. s.103(2) is NEW: when a group of five or more persons acting in concert commits murder on the ground of race, caste or community, sex, place of birth, language, personal belief or any other similar ground, each member shall be punished with death or imprisonment for life and also fine, which is the statutory mob lynching offence.
  8. Murder by life convict (IPC 303, mandatory death) was struck down in Mithu v State of Punjab (1983); BNS s.104 (murder by a person already under sentence of imprisonment for life) is punishable with death or imprisonment for life meaning the remainder of natural life; the mandatory death sentence of IPC 303 is not retained Verify · wording.
  9. Culpable homicide not amounting to murder (s.105; IPC 304): Part I (intent/knowledge of likely death): imprisonment for life or up to 10 years plus fine. Part II (knowledge without intention): imprisonment up to 10 years, or fine, or both. BNS 105 retains the structure; minimum term unchanged Verify.
  10. Dowry death (s.80(2); IPC 304B): death of a woman within seven years of marriage by burns/bodily injury or otherwise than under normal circumstances, and soon before death subjected to cruelty or harassment by husband or relatives for dowry. Punishment: imprisonment not less than 7 years, may extend to life. Presumption under BSA s.118 (Evidence Act 113B). Key case: Kans Raj v State of Punjab (2000): 'soon before' means proximate and live link; Satbir Singh v State of Haryana (2021): the presumption under Evidence Act applies; courts must read ss.304B and 498A together. Cruelty + dowry demand is the essence.
  11. Causing death by negligence (s.106(1); IPC 304A): up to 5 years plus fine; for registered medical practitioners performing a medical procedure up to 2 years plus fine. Jacob Mathew v State of Punjab (2005): gross negligence or recklessness is required for doctors; simple lack of care insufficient.
  12. Hit and run (s.106(2)): causing death by rash and negligent driving not amounting to culpable homicide and escaping without reporting to police or magistrate soon after: up to 10 years and fine. The provision generated nationwide transporter protests; its operation was held in abeyance pending notification Verify · current status.
  13. Abetment of suicide: s.107, s.108 (see 4.4). Attempt to murder (s.109; IPC 307): up to 10 years and fine; if hurt is caused, life or up to 10 years; life convict attempting murder: death or life (s.109(2)). Attempt to commit culpable homicide (s.110; IPC 308): up to 3 years or fine; with hurt up to 7 years.
  14. Organised crime (s.111, new): continuing unlawful activity (kidnapping, robbery, vehicle theft, extortion, land grabbing, contract killing, economic offences, cyber crimes, trafficking of persons, drugs, weapons, illicit goods or services, human trafficking for prostitution or ransom) by a member of an organised crime syndicate, singly or jointly, by use of violence, threat, intimidation, coercion, or other unlawful means to obtain direct or indirect material benefit including financial benefit. Punishment: if it results in death, death or life and fine not less than Rs 10 lakh; otherwise 5 years to life and fine not less than Rs 5 lakh. Members, harbouring, possession of property derived from organised crime, abetment are also punished.
  15. Petty organised crime (s.112): 1 to 7 years and fine for acts by gang such as theft, snatching, cheating, unauthorised selling of tickets, unauthorised betting or gambling, selling public examination question papers.
  16. Terrorist act (s.113): act with intent to threaten unity, integrity, security or economic security of India, or strike terror in people, using bombs, dynamite, explosives, firearms, or other lethal substances; causing death: death or life imprisonment and fine; otherwise 5 years to life and fine. Overlaps with UAPA; the Sanhita attracts the 'which law to apply' discretion of a police officer of rank of SP.
  17. Rarest of rare doctrine: Bachan Singh v State of Punjab (1980) upheld constitutionality of death penalty subject to special reasons (s.354(3) CrPC, BNSS 393(3)); Machhi Singh v State of Punjab (1983) classified categories; Mukesh v State (NCT Delhi) (2017) Nirbhaya; recent emphasis on mitigating factors and sentencing hearing (Manoj v State of MP, 2022); Shatrughan Chauhan (2014) delay in mercy petitions.

⚖Landmark cases

Virsa Singh v State of Punjab

1958

Four-fold test for s.300 clause 3 (now BNS 101(c)): (i) injury present; (ii) injury of the kind intended; (iii) sufficient in ordinary course to cause death; (iv) injury inflicted was intended.

K.M. Nanavati v State of Maharashtra

1962

Grave and sudden provocation requires the reasonable man test, and the cooling-time test; discussed alongside the jury trial system.

State of Andhra Pradesh v R. Punnayya

1976

Method for distinguishing culpable homicide and murder: determine whether it is culpable homicide, then whether any clause of s.300 applies.

Bachan Singh v State of Punjab

1980

Death penalty constitutional; applied only in the rarest of rare cases after balancing aggravating and mitigating circumstances.

Machhi Singh v State of Punjab

1983

Categories of rarest of rare: manner of commission, motive, anti-social nature, magnitude, personality of victim.

Mithu v State of Punjab

1983

Mandatory death sentence under IPC 303 violates Articles 14 and 21.

Jacob Mathew v State of Punjab

2005

Gross negligence (not ordinary) is needed for criminal liability of professionals; guidelines on prosecution of doctors.

Alister Anthony Pareira v State of Maharashtra

2012

Drunken driving causing deaths: s.304A vs 304 Part II; knowledge may be inferred where driving was grossly reckless.

Kans Raj v State of Punjab

2000

'Soon before her death' requires a proximate and live link between cruelty and death.

Satbir Singh v State of Haryana

2021

Dowry death: presumption under s.113B and interplay of ss.304B and 498A.

Reg v Govinda

1876

Distinction between culpable homicide and murder; intention to cause a bodily injury likely to cause death.

Mukesh v State (NCT of Delhi)

2017

Death penalty for Nirbhaya gang-rape and murder, rarest of rare.

!Exam traps

  • TrapMurder = culpable homicide + one of four clauses; not every death by intent is murder if an exception applies.
  • TrapThe five Exceptions are tested directly: provocation, private defence excess, public servant, sudden fight, consent.
  • TrapSection 304A (negligence) is outside the s.100 to 101 family; it covers rash or negligent acts that do not amount to culpable homicide.
  • TrapMob lynching: five or more persons, listed grounds, punishment death or life (BNS 103(2)); students confuse it with unlawful assembly rioting.
  • TrapDowry death minimum is 7 years; punishment may go to life.
  • TrapOrganised crime fine minimum: Rs 10 lakh if death; Rs 5 lakh otherwise.
