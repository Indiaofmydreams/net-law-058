Delegated Legislation

iOverview

Constitutional limits on delegation, 'essential legislative function', conditional legislation, sub-delegation and control. Frequently asked.

★Must know

  1. Delegated legislation = law made by an executive authority under power delegated by the Legislature. Forms: rules, regulations, bye-laws, orders, notifications, schemes. Reasons: pressure on parliamentary time, technicality, flexibility, emergencies, experimentation. DELEGATUS NON POTEST DELEGARE: a delegate cannot further delegate (sub-delegation requires express or implied authority).
  2. CONSTITUTIONAL LIMITS: Legislature can delegate but cannot abdicate. IN RE DELHI LAWS ACT (1951, Special Reference 1 of 1951): the Supreme Court (7-judge bench, separate opinions) held that the Legislature may delegate ancillary or subsidiary powers but cannot delegate its ESSENTIAL LEGISLATIVE FUNCTION (laying down the legislative policy and enacting it as a binding rule); power to repeal or modify laws ('Henry VIII clause') beyond limited extent is excessive; conditional legislation is valid. Harishankar Bagla v State of Madhya Pradesh (1954): essential legislative function consists in the determination or choosing of the legislative policy and of formally enacting that policy into a binding rule of conduct; the legislature must declare the policy and lay down the standard; delegation is valid if policy is laid down; Hamdard Dawakhana v Union of India (1960): the Legislature cannot leave to the executive the decision on what constitutes a 'disease' or what is an objectionable advertisement - delegation was excessive as the Act (Drugs and Magic Remedies (Objectionable Advertisements) Act 1954) left the central government to add diseases without any guidance; Vasu Dev Singh v Union of India (2006); Gwalior Rayon Silk Mfg v Assistant Commissioner of Sales Tax (1974): power to fix rate of tax by the Government valid if guidelines exist; Municipal Corporation of Delhi v Birla Cotton Spinning and Weaving Mills (1968): taxation power - essential legislative function includes the imposition of tax; the delegation of power to fix rate within limits is valid; Devi Das Gopal Krishnan v State of Punjab (1967): excessive delegation to fix tax rate struck.
  3. CONDITIONAL LEGISLATION vs DELEGATED LEGISLATION: in conditional legislation the Legislature makes the law complete and leaves to the executive the determination of the time/ place/ manner/ fulfilment of a condition for bringing it into operation (Queen v Burah, 1878 PC: power to determine the area to which the Act applies is conditional legislation; the Indian legislature is not a delegate of the British Parliament; Rajnarain Singh v Chairman, Patna Administration Committee, 1954: the Legislature cannot delegate power to modify essential provisions). In delegated legislation, the delegate frames subsidiary rules and has some law-making discretion. Cases: Sardar Inder Singh v State of Rajasthan (1957); Hamdard; Registrar of Co-operative Societies v K. Kunjabmu (1980).
  4. CLASSIFICATION: by delegator - parent Act; by purpose - supplementary, contingent, ancillary; by nature - enabling, extending, modifying (Henry VIII clause: authorises the executive to amend the statute itself), exemption, taxation, commencement; by authority - sub-delegated legislation (e.g., rules by Government, bye-laws by local authorities, orders by Commissioner). Judicial notice: delegated legislation made by the Central Government is judicially noticed.
  5. CONTROL OF DELEGATED LEGISLATION: (A) JUDICIAL CONTROL - (1) SUBSTANTIVE ULTRA VIRES: the rule is beyond the scope of the parent Act or violates the Constitution (fundamental rights) or repugnant to the parent Act or unreasonable; (2) PROCEDURAL ULTRA VIRES: failure to follow the prescribed procedure (consultation, publication; Raza Buland Sugar Co v Municipal Board Rampur (1965): failure to follow the prescribed procedure makes the bye-law invalid); (3) UNREASONABLENESS: Kruse v Johnson (1898): bye-laws made by local authorities may be struck down if they are manifestly unjust, oppressive or partial; Indian Express Newspapers (Bombay) Pvt Ltd v Union of India (1985): subordinate legislation can be challenged on grounds applicable to ordinary executive action and also on grounds of arbitrariness, unreasonableness and violation of Art 14 (and that subordinate legislation is not shielded by a presumption of constitutionality enjoyed by plenary legislation); State of Tamil Nadu v P. Krishnamurthy (2006): the grounds on which delegated legislation can be challenged: lack of legislative competence; violating the parent Act; violating Constitution; and arbitrariness/ unreasonableness in the narrow sense - an unreasonable bye-law may be struck down only if it is 'manifestly arbitrary'; Cellular Operators Association of India v TRAI (2003). (B) LEGISLATIVE/ PARLIAMENTARY CONTROL: laying of rules before the House (whether laying is mandatory or directory depends on the statute - Atlas Cycle Industries v State of Haryana (1979) treated it as directory in that case); Committees on Subordinate Legislation (Lok Sabha, Rajya Sabha); scrutiny; the power of the Houses to annul; (C) PUBLICATION: delegated legislation must be published (Harla v State of Rajasthan, 1951: laws must be published in the official Gazette/ made known to the people before becoming effective). (D) CONSULTATION with affected interests and pre-publication of drafts.
  6. SUB-DELEGATION: allowed only if the parent Act authorises it expressly or by necessary implication (Ganpati Sitaram Balvalkar v Waman Shripad Mage, 1981; Sahni Silk Mills v ESIC, 1994: power to sub-delegate must be conferred). Henry VIII clauses are viewed with suspicion; Court may read them down.
  7. ORDINANCES (Art 123/ 213) and EMERGENCY regulations: executive legislative power, subject to approval; not delegated legislation in the strict sense but similar; Krishna Kumar Singh v State of Bihar (2017): re-promulgation of ordinances is a fraud on the Constitution. ADMINISTRATIVE DIRECTIONS: circulars, guidelines, manuals - non-statutory, binding on subordinate officers only; courts apply them if they fill gaps and do not conflict with law; Union of India v Azadi Bachao Andolan (2003): circular in tax context; State of UP v Chandra Prakash Pandey (2001).

⚖Landmark cases

In re Delhi Laws Act

1951

Limits of delegation; essential legislative function cannot be delegated.

Harishankar Bagla v State of MP

1954

Policy and standard must be laid down; delegation of ancillary function valid.

Hamdard Dawakhana v Union of India

1960

Excessive delegation without guidance is invalid.

Queen v Burah

1878

Conditional legislation.

Indian Express Newspapers v Union of India

1985

Subordinate legislation may be struck down for arbitrariness/ unreasonableness.

State of Tamil Nadu v P. Krishnamurthy

2006

Grounds of challenge to delegated legislation.

Kruse v Johnson

1898

Unreasonable bye-laws can be struck down.

Raza Buland Sugar Co v Municipal Board Rampur

1965

Non-compliance with prescribed procedure invalidates the rule.

Harla v State of Rajasthan

1951

A law must be published/ made known before it takes effect.

✎Memory aids

  • Delegated legislation challenge = S-P-U: Substantive ultra vires, Procedural ultra vires, Unreasonableness.

!Exam traps

  • TrapEssential legislative function (policy-making) cannot be delegated; ancillary/ subsidiary can.
  • TrapHenry VIII clause = power to amend the parent Act - strictly viewed.
  • TrapConditional legislation is NOT delegation of law-making; it's delegation of the power to determine when/ where the law operates.
  • TrapSubordinate legislation does not enjoy the presumption of constitutionality that Acts enjoy.

?Likely questions

  1. Essential legislative functions: In re Delhi Laws Act.
  2. Hamdard Dawakhana: excessive delegation.
  3. Delegated legislation challengeable for: ultra vires, unreasonableness, procedural violation.
  4. Henry VIII clause: power to amend the Act itself.
