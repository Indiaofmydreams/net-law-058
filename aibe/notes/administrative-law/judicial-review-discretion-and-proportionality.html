iOverview

Grounds (illegality, irrationality, procedural impropriety, proportionality), abuse of discretion, legitimate expectation and promissory estoppel.

★Must know

  1. SOURCE: Art 32 (Supreme Court - enforcement of fundamental rights; itself a fundamental right) and Art 226 (High Courts - any person or authority, for enforcement of fundamental rights and 'for any other purpose'), Art 227 (superintendence over subordinate courts and tribunals), Art 136 (special leave), Art 142. Judicial review is part of the basic structure (L. Chandra Kumar v Union of India, 1997).
  2. GROUNDS - Lord Diplock in Council of Civil Service Unions v Minister for the Civil Service (GCHQ case, 1985): (1) ILLEGALITY - the decision-maker must correctly understand the law and give effect to it (acting ultra vires, error of law, delegation/ dictation, fettering of discretion, irrelevant considerations, improper purposes, non-application of mind); (2) IRRATIONALITY (Wednesbury unreasonableness) - a decision so outrageous in its defiance of logic or of accepted moral standards that no sensible person who had applied his mind to the question could have arrived at it; (3) PROCEDURAL IMPROPRIETY - failure to observe basic rules of natural justice or procedural fairness or statutory procedure; and (4) PROPORTIONALITY (a further ground that Lord Diplock foresaw). Associated Provincial Picture Houses v Wednesbury Corporation (1948): the court can interfere only if the authority took into account irrelevant considerations or ignored relevant ones, or reached a decision so unreasonable that no reasonable authority could ever have come to it.
  3. ABUSE OF DISCRETION: (a) improper purpose (Sharp v Wakefield; Padfield v Minister of Agriculture, 1968 - discretion must be exercised to promote the policy and objects of the Act; Barium Chemicals Ltd v Company Law Board, 1967 - subjective satisfaction is reviewable if there are no material facts); (b) mala fides (State of Punjab v Gurdial Singh, 1980: mala fide exercise of power for acquiring land; S. Pratap Singh v State of Punjab, 1964); (c) irrelevant considerations/ ignoring relevant ones (Rohtas Industries v S.D. Agarwal, 1969); (d) non-application of mind/ acting under dictation (Commissioner of Police v Gordhandas Bhanji, 1952: order issued under dictation of a Minister invalid); (e) fettering of discretion by rigid policies (British Oxygen Co v Board of Trade); (f) unreasonableness/ arbitrariness (Maneka Gandhi; Shrilekha Vidyarthi v State of UP, 1991: arbitrary termination of government counsel - Art 14 applies to government contractual and appointment decisions); (g) unstructured discretion violates Art 14 (State of West Bengal v Anwar Ali Sarkar, 1952; Maneka Gandhi: power of impounding passport without guidelines). Subjective satisfaction: where the statute says 'if the authority is satisfied' or 'in the opinion of the authority', courts still examine whether the opinion was formed on relevant material.
  4. PROPORTIONALITY: whether the means used are suitably related and no more than necessary to achieve the aim (R v Secretary of State for the Home Department, ex p Daly, 2001). India: Om Kumar v Union of India (2001): proportionality applies strictly to cases involving fundamental rights, while the Wednesbury standard applies to other administrative decisions; Ranjit Thakur v Union of India (1987): punishment so disproportionate as to shock the conscience of the court is open to judicial review (court-martial); Union of India v G. Ganayutham (1997): the proportionality principle in administrative law; Modern Dental College v State of MP (2016) and K.S. Puttaswamy (Aadhaar, 2018) adopted the four-prong proportionality test (legitimate aim; rational connection; necessity/ least restrictive alternative; balancing); Anuradha Bhasin v Union of India (2020): restrictions on the internet must satisfy proportionality; Chairman, All India Railway Rec. Board v K. Shyam Kumar (2010): 'Wednesbury' and proportionality.
  5. JUDICIAL REVIEW IN CONTRACTS AND TENDERS: Tata Cellular v Union of India (1994): judicial review of administrative action in tenders is limited to the decision-making process, not the decision; the Government may choose the contractor; the Court examines whether there was illegality, irrationality or procedural impropriety; Jagdish Mandal v State of Orissa (2007): two tests - whether the process or the decision is mala fide/ intended to favour someone, and whether the process is so arbitrary and irrational that no responsible authority could have reached it; Michigan Rubber v State of Karnataka (2012); Air India Ltd v Cochin International Airport (2000). Art 14 applies to government contracts (Ramana Dayaram Shetty).
  6. LEGITIMATE EXPECTATION: a person may have a legitimate expectation of being treated in a certain way by an administrative authority due to a promise, representation, or a consistent past practice (Council of Civil Service Unions v Minister, 1985; Attorney General for Hong Kong v Ng Yuen Shiu, 1983). Procedural legitimate expectation (a right to a hearing/ consultation) is recognised; substantive legitimate expectation (a substantive benefit) is recognised in India with limits. Indian cases: Navjyoti Co-operative Group Housing Society v Union of India (1992): legitimate expectation of consideration in seniority-based allotment; Food Corporation of India v Kamdhenu Cattle Feed Industries (1993): fair play; a legitimate expectation is not a right - it's a ground for judicial review; Punjab Communications Ltd v Union of India (1999): changes of policy can defeat a legitimate expectation if it's based on public interest and the change is not arbitrary; Union of India v Hindustan Development Corporation (1993); Jitendra Kumar v State of Haryana (2008): legitimate expectation cannot be claimed against a statutory provision; Sethi Auto Service Station v DDA (2009): the doctrine is not an independent substantive right but a ground; Official Liquidator v Dayanand (2008); Hira Tikkoo v Union Territory of Chandigarh (2004). The doctrine is a part of Art 14 and rule of law.
  7. PROMISSORY ESTOPPEL IN PUBLIC LAW: Central London Property Trust v High Trees House (1947); Indian: Union of India v Indo-Afghan Agencies (1968): the Government is bound by its representation if the other party has acted to its detriment (altered position); Motilal Padampat Sugar Mills v State of UP (1979): no need to show detriment - only alteration of position; the doctrine is applicable against the Government, even in exercise of governmental functions, but it cannot be invoked to compel the Government to act contrary to law, or to the detriment of public interest (public interest overrides - e.g., when a policy changes in public interest the court weighs). Doctrine of estoppel does not apply against the legislature; Union of India v Anglo Afghan Agencies. Contrast with legitimate expectation (legitimate expectation does not need a promise and doesn't give a right of enforcement).
  8. REASONED DECISIONS: duty to give reasons is a rule of natural justice and a fundamental of good administration: Siemens Engineering and Manufacturing Co v Union of India (1976) (quasi-judicial orders must be reasoned); Maneka Gandhi; S.N. Mukherjee v Union of India (1990): reasons must be recorded in administrative orders where required by law and for appellate/ revisional review; Mohinder Singh Gill v Chief Election Commissioner (1978): public orders publicly made, in exercise of a statutory power, must be judged by the objective texts or the reasons contained in them and cannot be supplemented by fresh reasons in the shape of affidavit or otherwise. Judicial review of policy decisions: courts do not interfere with policy unless arbitrary, mala fide or violate fundamental rights (Balco Employees Union v Union of India, 2002; State of Madhya Pradesh v Narmada Bachao Andolan, 2011). 'Fair play in action' and bias in selection.

!Exam traps

  • TrapWednesbury: 'unreasonable' decision; proportionality is stricter and used where fundamental rights are involved.
  • TrapLegitimate expectation is NOT a legal right; it is a ground for review; promissory estoppel requires a promise and alteration of position.
  • TrapPromissory estoppel cannot be pleaded to compel illegal acts or against public interest.
  • TrapIn tenders, the court reviews the decision-making process, not the merits.

?Likely questions

  1. Wednesbury unreasonableness is a ground of: irrationality.
  2. Proportionality in India: Om Kumar.
  3. Legitimate expectation: Navjyoti/ FCI v Kamdhenu.
  4. Which case laid down the GCHQ grounds? CCSU v Minister for the Civil Service.
