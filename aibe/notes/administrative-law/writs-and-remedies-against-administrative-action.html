Writs and Remedies against Administrative Action

iOverview

Five prerogative writs, conditions for issuance, alternative remedy rule, locus standi and PIL.

★Must know

  1. ART 32 (Supreme Court) - a fundamental right itself; for enforcement of fundamental rights only; Art 226 (High Courts) - wider: 'for the enforcement of any of the rights conferred by Part III and for any other purpose' (statutory/ legal rights); territorial jurisdiction (Art 226(2) - cause of action arises in whole or part). FIVE WRITS: (1) HABEAS CORPUS ('you may have the body') - against illegal detention by State or private persons; locus - next friend permitted; res judicata does not apply (Sunil Batra; Kanu Sanyal v District Magistrate, 1973: the court examines the legality of the detention at the time of the return); (2) MANDAMUS ('we command') - to compel performance of a public duty (statutory duty): conditions - a legal right to the performance, a public duty, demand and refusal; not issued against the President/ Governor personally (Art 361), against private individuals (except to perform public duty), to compel discretionary acts (unless abuse), or to enforce purely contractual duties (Sohan Lal v Union of India (1957)); (3) PROHIBITION - against inferior courts/ tribunals acting without or in excess of jurisdiction, issued before the decision is made (to stop proceedings); (4) CERTIORARI ('to be certified') - to quash orders already made by inferior courts/ tribunals/ authorities exercising judicial or quasi-judicial functions for want or excess of jurisdiction, violating natural justice or error of law apparent on the face of the record (Hari Vishnu Kamath v Syed Ahmad Ishaque, 1955: the grounds; Syed Yakoob v K.S. Radhakrishnan, 1964: certiorari corrects errors of jurisdiction, not mere errors of fact or law that are not apparent; T.C. Basappa v T. Nagappa, 1954: certiorari against a tribunal; Naresh Shridhar Mirajkar v State of Maharashtra, 1966: certiorari doesn't lie against a High Court's judicial order); (5) QUO WARRANTO ('by what authority') - to challenge usurpation of a public office; conditions: the office must be public, created by statute or Constitution, substantive; the holder must have been appointed contrary to law (University of Mysore v C.D. Govinda Rao, 1964; any person may file as the remedy is not a personal remedy; Kumar Padma Prasad v Union of India (1992): the appointment of a judge without qualifications invalid). Also: Art 226 can issue 'directions, orders' - broad (e.g., continuing mandamus).
  2. SCOPE: Writs against 'State' and 'any person or authority' performing public functions (Andi Mukta, Binny Ltd v V. Sadasivan (2005): the public law element). Not available against private contracts unless a public law element; not generally against private schools/ cooperative societies, unless discharging public functions (Federal Bank v Sagar Thomas, 2003; K. Krishnamacharyulu v Sri Venkateswara Hindu College, 1997). Writ of mandamus cannot enforce an order made by a court of law; the Court will not interfere in political questions.
  3. CONDITIONS: (a) Locus standi - the petitioner must be a person aggrieved - the traditional rule is relaxed in public interest litigation: S.P. Gupta v Union of India (1981) (Judges' Transfer Case: any member of the public having a sufficient interest may file; PIL by letter); Bandhua Mukti Morcha v Union of India (1984); M.C. Mehta; People's Union for Democratic Rights v Union of India (1982); Dr. Subramanian Swamy; caution - Guruvayur Devaswom Managing Committee v C.K. Rajan (2003): PIL guidelines; State of Uttaranchal v Balwant Singh Chaufal (2010): guidelines to prevent abuse; PIL is not for personal gain/ publicity/ political motives ('publicity interest litigation'). (b) Alternative remedy rule: ordinarily a High Court will not entertain a writ if an effective alternative remedy exists; but the rule is one of discretion and exceptions: where fundamental rights are violated; where there's a violation of principles of natural justice; where the order is wholly without jurisdiction; where the vires of the statute is challenged (Whirlpool Corporation v Registrar of Trade Marks, Mumbai, 1998; Harbanslal Sahnia v Indian Oil Corporation, 2003; State of UP v Indian Hume Pipe Co, 1977; Union of India v State of Haryana, 2000; Commissioner of Income Tax v Chhabil Dass Agarwal, 2013: an alternative statutory remedy should be exhausted before writ in revenue matters). (c) Delay/ laches: no strict limitation, but unreasonable delay may disentitle (State of M.P. v Nandlal Jaiswal, 1986). (d) Res judicata: applies to writ petitions (Daryao v State of UP, 1961: a decision in a writ petition on merits bars a fresh petition under Art 32 on the same facts; Amalgamated Coalfields v Janapada Sabha, 1963); (e) Clean hands and suppression of material facts. (f) Writs are discretionary relief.
  4. REMEDIES OTHER THAN WRITS: declaration, injunction (Specific Relief Act Secs 34, 38-42); damages (tort/ public law compensation); statutory appeal and revision; ordinary civil suit (Sec 9 CPC); Sec 80 CPC: notice of two months before instituting a suit against the Government or a public officer for an act done in his official capacity (Sec 80(2) allows a suit for urgent or immediate relief without notice, with leave of the court); the notice must clearly identify the claim; Bhagchand Dagadusa v Secretary of State (1927) - strict compliance; State of Andhra Pradesh v Pioneer Builders (2006): the notice requirement is mandatory but not hyper-technical; the Bar of suits (Sec 11 and Sec 9 CPC); administrative remedies - departmental appeal/ revision; and inquiries.
  5. ART 300 and Contract: Art 299 - contracts made in the exercise of the executive power of the Union/ State must be expressed to be made by the President/ Governor, and executed by persons authorised and in such manner as the President/ Governor may direct or authorise; a contract not complying with Art 299 is void and not enforceable against the Government (K.P. Chowdhury v State of M.P., 1967; Bhikraj Jaipuria v Union of India, 1962; Mulamchand v State of M.P., 1968: Art 299 is mandatory, but the Government's liability to restore benefits under Sec 70 Contract Act is not barred; State of West Bengal v B.K. Mondal (1962)). Government contracts are subject to Art 14 (Ramana Dayaram Shetty; Kumari Shrilekha Vidyarthi); blacklisting needs a hearing (Erusian Equipment & Chemicals v State of West Bengal, 1975; Gorkha Security Services v Government (NCT of Delhi), 2014: the show-cause notice must state the grounds for blacklisting).
  6. PUBLIC SERVANTS: Art 309 (recruitment and conditions of service - Acts and rules), Art 310 (tenure - 'pleasure of the President/ Governor' subject to the Constitution), Art 311 (safeguards: (1) no dismissal or removal by an authority subordinate to the appointing authority; (2) no dismissal, removal or reduction in rank except after an inquiry in which he has been informed of the charges and given a reasonable opportunity of being heard; exceptions in the second proviso: (a) conviction on a criminal charge; (b) where the authority is satisfied that for some reason, to be recorded in writing, it is not reasonably practicable to hold the inquiry; (c) where the President/ Governor is satisfied that in the interest of the security of the State it is not expedient to hold the inquiry) - Union of India v Tulsiram Patel (1985); Parshotam Lal Dhingra v Union of India (1958): 'punishment' test; Moti Ram Deka v General Manager, North East Frontier Railway (1964); doctrine of pleasure; Samsher Singh v State of Punjab (1974): the President/ Governor exercises power on the aid and advice of the Council of Ministers. Disciplinary proceedings: principle of proportionality in punishment (Ranjit Thakur; B.C. Chaturvedi v Union of India, 1995: the court may interfere if the punishment shocks the conscience). Departmental inquiry and criminal trial can proceed simultaneously (Capt. M. Paul Anthony v Bharat Gold Mines, 1999: stay in grave cases involving complex questions of fact/ law).

✎Memory aids

  • Writs = H-M-P-C-Q: Habeas, Mandamus, Prohibition, Certiorari, Quo warranto.

!Exam traps

  • TrapHabeas corpus does not require strict locus; mandamus needs a public duty; certiorari is for quashing, prohibition for preventing.
  • TrapQuo warranto lies for a PUBLIC office only.
  • TrapAlternative remedy is a rule of discretion with exceptions (FR violation, natural justice breach, lack of jurisdiction, vires challenge).
  • TrapSec 80 CPC: two months' notice - mandatory subject to Sec 80(2) leave for urgent relief.

?Likely questions

  1. Writ for the quashing of an order: certiorari.
  2. Writ to challenge a person's authority to hold a public office: quo warranto.
  3. Art 299: government contracts.
  4. Sec 80 CPC notice period: two months.
