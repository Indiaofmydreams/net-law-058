Administrative Tribunals and Quasi-Judicial Bodies

iOverview

Arts 323A/323B, L. Chandra Kumar, tribunal independence cases and the Tribunals Reforms Act 2021.

★Must know

  1. Tribunals = adjudicatory bodies outside the regular courts, with expertise and speed. Art 323A (inserted by the 42nd Amendment 1976): Parliament may by law provide for adjudication or trial by administrative tribunals of disputes relating to recruitment and conditions of service of persons appointed to public services of the Union/ States/ local authorities/ corporations; Art 323B: tribunals for other matters (taxation, foreign exchange, industrial and labour disputes, land reforms, ceiling on urban property, elections to Parliament and State Legislatures (excluded), food stuffs, rent and tenancy). Administrative Tribunals Act 1985 (Central Administrative Tribunal (CAT) at the Centre; State Administrative Tribunals; some States have abolished). Other tribunals: NCLT/ NCLAT, NGT, ITAT, CESTAT, SAT, TDSAT, AFT, DRT/ DRAT, Railway Claims Tribunal, NCDRC (quasi-judicial commissions), Appellate Tribunal for Electricity, etc.
  2. Distinction: courts (inherent jurisdiction, binding precedent, procedure under CPC) vs tribunals (statutory jurisdiction; may be bound by natural justice but not strict CPC procedure; may have technical members). Quasi-judicial bodies: authority with duty to act judicially - determination of rights after hearing (Ridge v Baldwin; Cooper). Test of quasi-judicial: the Cooper test / the 'two-fold test' - legal authority to determine questions affecting rights of subjects + duty to act judicially (R v Electricity Commissioners, 1924, Atkin LJ); Province of Bombay v Khushaldas Advani (1950) - the lis inter partes theory; Indian courts have expanded after A.K. Kraipak.
  3. S.P. Sampath Kumar v Union of India (1987): upheld the Administrative Tribunals Act 1985 and the exclusion of HC jurisdiction on the basis that the tribunal is an effective alternative institutional mechanism; L. CHANDRA KUMAR v UNION OF INDIA (1997, 7-judge Bench): overruled Sampath Kumar to the extent that it excluded the jurisdiction of High Courts under Arts 226/ 227 and of the Supreme Court under Art 32: the power of judicial review under Arts 226/227 and 32 is part of the BASIC STRUCTURE and cannot be excluded; tribunals are competent to hear matters where the vires of statutory provisions are questioned (but not the vires of their parent statutes); tribunals act as courts of first instance; decisions of tribunals are subject to scrutiny before a Division Bench of the High Court within whose territorial jurisdiction the tribunal falls; direct appeal to the Supreme Court under Art 136 is retained; the Tribunal can examine constitutional validity of subordinate legislation. Union of India v R. Gandhi (Madras Bar Association case, 2010) (NCLT/ NCLAT) - the Court laid down the qualifications, tenure and independence safeguards for tribunals; Madras Bar Association v Union of India (2014, 2020 and later cases) and Rojer Mathew v South Indian Bank (2019) struck down certain provisions of the Finance Act 2017 on tribunal conditions of service; the Tribunals Reforms Act 2021 abolished several appellate tribunals (including the IPAB, Film Certification Appellate Tribunal, etc.), transferred their functions to High Courts, and prescribed common qualifications/ tenure; in Madras Bar Association v Union of India (decided November 2025), the Supreme Court struck down key provisions of the 2021 Act (such as minimum age and short tenure conditions and restrictions on the search-cum-selection committees) and directed the setting up of a National Tribunals Commission (verify the current state of compliance).
  4. Features of an independent tribunal: judicial member/ independent selection committee; security of tenure (not shorter than five years); administrative independence (nodal Ministry of Law, not the parent Ministry); appeal to the High Court/ Supreme Court. Judicial review of tribunals: by the High Court under Art 226/227 (L. Chandra Kumar); only on grounds of jurisdictional error, violation of natural justice, error of law apparent on the face of the record, perversity.
  5. Other adjudicatory bodies: Commissions of Inquiry (Commissions of Inquiry Act 1952 - fact-finding, recommendatory; Sec 3 - Government appoints; Sec 3(4): Government must lay the report with a memorandum of action taken within six months before the House; Ram Krishna Dalmia v Justice S.R. Tendolkar (1958): a Commission of Inquiry is valid and not a discriminatory law; State of Karnataka v Union of India (1977)); statutory regulators - SEBI, TRAI, IRDAI, CCI, RBI; Lokpal; Information Commissions; National Human Rights Commission (Protection of Human Rights Act 1993). Ombudsman concept (Scandinavia - Sweden 1809; New Zealand 1962; India: Lokpal/ Lokayukta).

⚖Landmark cases

L. Chandra Kumar v Union of India

1997

Judicial review under Arts 226/227/32 is basic structure; tribunals subject to High Court scrutiny.

S.P. Sampath Kumar v Union of India

1987

Upheld Administrative Tribunals Act; overruled on HC exclusion by Chandra Kumar.

Union of India v R. Gandhi

2010

NCLT/ NCLAT; independence and qualifications of tribunal members.

Rojer Mathew v South Indian Bank

2019

Tribunal service conditions; Finance Act 2017 challenge.

Ram Krishna Dalmia v Justice S.R. Tendolkar

1958

Commissions of Inquiry; validity.

!Exam traps

  • TrapTribunals CANNOT exclude High Court's Art 226/227 jurisdiction (Chandra Kumar), but appeals lie to the High Court Division Bench (not directly to SC) except under Art 136.
  • TrapArt 323A: service matters; Art 323B: other subjects.

?Likely questions

  1. Tribunals appeals: first to the High Court Division Bench (Chandra Kumar).
  2. Art 323A relates to: administrative tribunals (service matters).
  3. Commissions of Inquiry Act: 1952.
