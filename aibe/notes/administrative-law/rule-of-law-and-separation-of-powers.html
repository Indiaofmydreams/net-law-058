Nature, Scope and Foundations: Rule of Law and Separation of Powers

iOverview

Conceptual base for 3 questions: definition, Dicey's rule of law, droit administratif, separation of powers in India.

★Must know

  1. Administrative law is the law relating to the powers and procedures of administrative agencies, including the law governing judicial review of administrative action (Ivor Jennings: the law relating to administration; K.C. Davis: the law concerning the powers and procedures of administrative agencies, including especially the law governing judicial review of administrative action). In India it has grown from the common law of judicial review, Arts 12-14, 32, 226, 300, 311, and statutes (tribunals, RTI, Lokpal). Administrative functions are classified as rule-making (legislative), rule-adjudication (judicial/ quasi-judicial), rule-application (executive/ administrative) and ministerial.
  2. RULE OF LAW (A.V. Dicey, 'Introduction to the Study of the Law of the Constitution'): three meanings - (1) supremacy of regular law as opposed to arbitrary power; (2) equality before the law; (3) the constitution is the result of ordinary law of the land (rights flow from judicial decisions). Indian position: the rule of law is part of the basic structure (Indira Nehru Gandhi v Raj Narain, 1975); Art 14 prohibits arbitrariness (E.P. Royappa v State of Tamil Nadu, 1974: equality is antithetic to arbitrariness; Maneka Gandhi v Union of India, 1978: any action must be just, fair and reasonable). Shayara Bano (2017) applied manifest arbitrariness to legislation. ADM Jabalpur v Shivkant Shukla (1976) (Habeas Corpus case) was overruled by K.S. Puttaswamy (2017).
  3. DROIT ADMINISTRATIF (French administrative law): a separate body of public law applied by the Conseil d'Etat administrative courts to disputes involving the administration; Dicey criticised it as inconsistent with the rule of law; in India the ordinary courts review administrative action; administrative tribunals are subject to constitutional courts (L. Chandra Kumar). Position of the administrative law in USA: Administrative Procedure Act 1946.
  4. SEPARATION OF POWERS: not rigidly followed in India (Ram Jawaya Kapur v State of Punjab, 1955: the Indian Constitution has not recognised the doctrine of separation of powers in its absolute rigidity but the functions of the different parts or branches of the Government have been sufficiently differentiated); Indira Gandhi v Raj Narain: separation of powers is part of the basic structure; Kesavananda Bharati v State of Kerala (1973): basic structure doctrine; Delegation, tribunals and the administrative adjudication are accommodated within the framework; In re Delhi Laws Act (1951). Judicial review is itself a basic feature (L. Chandra Kumar; Minerva Mills v Union of India, 1980).
  5. Sources of administrative law: Constitution; statutes (e.g., Administrative Tribunals Act 1985, RTI Act 2005, CVC Act 2003, Lokpal and Lokayuktas Act 2013, Commissions of Inquiry Act 1952); delegated legislation; administrative directions/ circulars (binding only in some cases - Union of India v Azadi Bachao Andolan (2003): circulars issued by CBDT under statutory power binding on tax authorities); judicial decisions.
  6. 'STATE' under Art 12 and public law remedies: 'authority' includes Government, Parliament, State legislatures, local authorities and 'other authorities' (Ramana Dayaram Shetty v International Airport Authority, 1979 - instrumentality test; Ajay Hasia v Khalid Mujib, 1981 - tests for an instrumentality or agency of the State; Pradeep Kumar Biswas v Indian Institute of Chemical Biology, 2002 - 'financial, functional and administrative domination'; Zee Telefilms v Union of India (2005)). Writ jurisdiction under Art 226 extends to 'any person or authority' discharging public functions (Andi Mukta Sadguru v V.R. Rudani, 1989; K.K. Saksena v International Commission on Irrigation and Drainage, 2015; Federal Bank v Sagar Thomas, 2003).

⚖Landmark cases

E.P. Royappa v State of Tamil Nadu

1974

Arbitrariness is antithetical to Art 14 - new concept of equality.

Maneka Gandhi v Union of India

1978

Procedure must be fair, just and reasonable; natural justice is part of Art 21.

Ram Jawaya Kapur v State of Punjab

1955

India does not follow separation of powers in rigid form.

Ramana Dayaram Shetty v International Airport Authority

1979

Instrumentality of the State; Art 14 applies in government contracts.

Ajay Hasia v Khalid Mujib

1981

Tests for 'other authorities' under Art 12.

!Exam traps

  • TrapThe Indian Constitution does not follow strict separation of powers - but judicial review and independence are basic features.
  • TrapDicey's rule of law was criticised for denying droit administratif; Indian law accepts administrative tribunals subject to judicial review.

?Likely questions

  1. Which case introduced the 'new concept of equality' (arbitrariness)? E.P. Royappa.
  2. Rule of law as basic structure: Indira Gandhi v Raj Narain.
  3. Instrumentality test: Ramana Dayaram Shetty / Ajay Hasia.
