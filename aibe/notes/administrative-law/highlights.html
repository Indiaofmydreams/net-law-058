1

Nature, Scope and Foundations: Rule of Law and Separation of Powers

High yieldRead the full chapter →

Cases in one line

  • E.P. Royappa v State of Tamil Nadu 1974 Arbitrariness is antithetical to Art 14 - new concept of equality.
  • Maneka Gandhi v Union of India 1978 Procedure must be fair, just and reasonable; natural justice is part of Art 21.
  • Ram Jawaya Kapur v State of Punjab 1955 India does not follow separation of powers in rigid form.
  • Ramana Dayaram Shetty v International Airport Authority 1979 Instrumentality of the State; Art 14 applies in government contracts.
  • Ajay Hasia v Khalid Mujib 1981 Tests for 'other authorities' under Art 12.

Exam traps

  • TrapThe Indian Constitution does not follow strict separation of powers - but judicial review and independence are basic features.
  • TrapDicey's rule of law was criticised for denying droit administratif; Indian law accepts administrative tribunals subject to judicial review.
2

Delegated Legislation

Very high yieldRead the full chapter →

Cases in one line

  • In re Delhi Laws Act 1951 Limits of delegation; essential legislative function cannot be delegated.
  • Harishankar Bagla v State of MP 1954 Policy and standard must be laid down; delegation of ancillary function valid.
  • Hamdard Dawakhana v Union of India 1960 Excessive delegation without guidance is invalid.
  • Queen v Burah 1878 Conditional legislation.
  • Indian Express Newspapers v Union of India 1985 Subordinate legislation may be struck down for arbitrariness/ unreasonableness.
  • State of Tamil Nadu v P. Krishnamurthy 2006 Grounds of challenge to delegated legislation.
  • Kruse v Johnson 1898 Unreasonable bye-laws can be struck down.
  • Raza Buland Sugar Co v Municipal Board Rampur 1965 Non-compliance with prescribed procedure invalidates the rule.
  • Harla v State of Rajasthan 1951 A law must be published/ made known before it takes effect.

Memory aids

  • Delegated legislation challenge = S-P-U: Substantive ultra vires, Procedural ultra vires, Unreasonableness.

Exam traps

  • TrapEssential legislative function (policy-making) cannot be delegated; ancillary/ subsidiary can.
  • TrapHenry VIII clause = power to amend the parent Act - strictly viewed.
  • TrapConditional legislation is NOT delegation of law-making; it's delegation of the power to determine when/ where the law operates.
  • TrapSubordinate legislation does not enjoy the presumption of constitutionality that Acts enjoy.
3

Judicial Review of Administrative Action; Discretion; Legitimate Expectation; Proportionality

Very high yieldRead the full chapter →

Cases in one line

  • Associated Provincial Picture Houses v Wednesbury Corporation 1948 Unreasonableness standard.
  • Council of Civil Service Unions v Minister for the Civil Service 1985 GCHQ: illegality, irrationality, procedural impropriety, (proportionality).
  • Tata Cellular v Union of India 1994 Limited judicial review in government tenders.
  • Om Kumar v Union of India 2001 Proportionality in India.
  • Motilal Padampat Sugar Mills v State of UP 1979 Promissory estoppel against the Government.
  • Navjyoti Co-op Group Housing Society v Union of India 1992 Legitimate expectation recognised.
  • Mohinder Singh Gill v Chief Election Commissioner 1978 Reasons cannot be supplemented by affidavit.
  • Padfield v Minister of Agriculture 1968 Discretion to be exercised for the Act's purposes.
  • Barium Chemicals v Company Law Board 1967 Subjective satisfaction subject to review.
  • Shrilekha Vidyarthi v State of UP 1991 Art 14 applies to State's contractual/ appointment decisions.

Exam traps

  • TrapWednesbury: 'unreasonable' decision; proportionality is stricter and used where fundamental rights are involved.
  • TrapLegitimate expectation is NOT a legal right; it is a ground for review; promissory estoppel requires a promise and alteration of position.
  • TrapPromissory estoppel cannot be pleaded to compel illegal acts or against public interest.
  • TrapIn tenders, the court reviews the decision-making process, not the merits.
4

Principles of Natural Justice

Very high yieldRead the full chapter →

Cases in one line

  • A.K. Kraipak v Union of India 1969 Natural justice applies to administrative actions; bias in selection committee.
  • Maneka Gandhi v Union of India 1978 Natural justice part of Art 21; post-decisional hearing in urgency.
  • Ridge v Baldwin 1964 Right to a hearing in administrative dismissal.
  • R v Sussex Justices ex p McCarthy 1924 Justice must be seen to be done.
  • Dimes v Grand Junction Canal 1852 Pecuniary bias disqualifies automatically.
  • Gullapalli Nageswara Rao v APSRTC 1959 Subject-matter/ departmental bias; hearing officer cannot be a judge in own cause.
  • S.L. Kapoor v Jagmohan 1980 Useless formality theory.
  • Union of India v Tulsiram Patel 1985 Art 311(2) and the second proviso.
  • Kanungo & Co v Collector of Customs 1972 Disclosure and cross-examination.
  • In Re: Directions in the matter of demolition of structures 2024 Pan-India due process guidelines on demolitions.

Memory aids

  • Types of bias = P-P-S: Pecuniary, Personal, Subject-matter.

Exam traps

  • TrapPecuniary bias disqualifies automatically; other bias needs real likelihood/ reasonable apprehension.
  • TrapExceptions: urgency, legislative action, confidentiality/ security, academic evaluation, impracticability.
  • TrapPost-decisional hearing may satisfy natural justice in emergencies (Maneka Gandhi).
  • Trap'Useless formality' theory vs prejudice test - violation + prejudice.

Administrative Tribunals and Quasi-Judicial Bodies

High yieldRead the full chapter →

Cases in one line

  • L. Chandra Kumar v Union of India 1997 Judicial review under Arts 226/227/32 is basic structure; tribunals subject to High Court scrutiny.
  • S.P. Sampath Kumar v Union of India 1987 Upheld Administrative Tribunals Act; overruled on HC exclusion by Chandra Kumar.
  • Union of India v R. Gandhi 2010 NCLT/ NCLAT; independence and qualifications of tribunal members.
  • Rojer Mathew v South Indian Bank 2019 Tribunal service conditions; Finance Act 2017 challenge.
  • Ram Krishna Dalmia v Justice S.R. Tendolkar 1958 Commissions of Inquiry; validity.

Exam traps

  • TrapTribunals CANNOT exclude High Court's Art 226/227 jurisdiction (Chandra Kumar), but appeals lie to the High Court Division Bench (not directly to SC) except under Art 136.
  • TrapArt 323A: service matters; Art 323B: other subjects.
6

Writs and Remedies against Administrative Action

Very high yieldRead the full chapter →

Cases in one line

  • Hari Vishnu Kamath v Syed Ahmad Ishaque 1955 Scope of certiorari.
  • S.P. Gupta v Union of India 1981 Liberalised locus standi; PIL.
  • Daryao v State of UP 1961 Res judicata in writ petitions.
  • Whirlpool Corporation v Registrar of Trade Marks 1998 Alternative remedy rule and its exceptions.
  • University of Mysore v C.D. Govinda Rao 1964 Quo warranto.
  • K.P. Chowdhury v State of MP 1967 Art 299 - mandatory formalities for government contracts.
  • Union of India v Tulsiram Patel 1985 Art 311(2) second proviso.
  • Parshotam Lal Dhingra v Union of India 1958 Punishment tests in termination.

Memory aids

  • Writs = H-M-P-C-Q: Habeas, Mandamus, Prohibition, Certiorari, Quo warranto.

Exam traps

  • TrapHabeas corpus does not require strict locus; mandamus needs a public duty; certiorari is for quashing, prohibition for preventing.
  • TrapQuo warranto lies for a PUBLIC office only.
  • TrapAlternative remedy is a rule of discretion with exceptions (FR violation, natural justice breach, lack of jurisdiction, vires challenge).
  • TrapSec 80 CPC: two months' notice - mandatory subject to Sec 80(2) leave for urgent relief.
7

Accountability and Transparency Institutions: Lokpal, Lokayukta, CVC, RTI Act

High yieldRead the full chapter →

Cases in one line

  • Vineet Narain v Union of India 1998 CVC's statutory basis; insulating CBI from political interference.
  • Subramanian Swamy v Director, CBI 2014 Single Directive/ Sec 6A DSPE Act unconstitutional.
  • Secretary General, Supreme Court v Subhash Chandra Agarwal 2019 CJI office is a 'public authority' under RTI.
  • CBSE v Aditya Bandopadhyay 2011 Evaluated answer sheets accessible; fiduciary exemption limited.
  • RBI v Jayantilal N. Mistry 2015 Bank inspection reports must be disclosed - public interest.
  • Anjali Bhardwaj v Union of India 2019 Timely appointments in Information Commissions.

Exam traps

  • TrapRTI: PIO reply in 30 days (48 hours for life/ liberty); first appeal 30 days; second appeal 90 days.
  • TrapSec 20 penalty: Rs 250 per day, maximum Rs 25,000.
  • TrapSec 8(1)(j): personal information exemption; now altered by DPDP Act.
  • TrapLokpal: 8 members + Chairperson; 50% judicial; PM covered subject to restrictions; 7-year limit for complaints.
