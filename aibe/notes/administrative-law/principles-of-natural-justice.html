iOverview

Audi alteram partem, nemo judex in causa sua, reasoned decisions, exceptions and post-decisional hearing; Maneka Gandhi and A.K. Kraipak are must-know.

★Must know

  1. Natural justice = fairness in procedure, implicit in Art 14 and Art 21 (Maneka Gandhi v Union of India, 1978: natural justice is part of Art 21's 'procedure established by law'; the 'procedure' must be fair, just and reasonable). A.K. Kraipak v Union of India (1969): the dividing line between administrative and quasi-judicial functions has become thin and is being gradually obliterated; natural justice principles apply to administrative actions that affect rights/ civil consequences; also a selection board member who was a candidate sat in the selection - bias. Ridge v Baldwin (1964): natural justice applies to administrative dismissal; Cooper v Wandsworth Board of Works (1863): even where the statute is silent, the right to hearing is implied ('the justice of the common law will supply the omission of the legislature'); State of Orissa v Dr (Miss) Binapani Dei (1967): where an order has civil consequences, an opportunity to be heard must be given; Swadeshi Cotton Mills v Union of India (1981): even in cases of exigency, a post-decisional hearing may be provided; Maneka Gandhi: post-decisional hearing is permissible in urgent cases (impounding of passport).
  2. TWO RULES: (1) NEMO JUDEX IN CAUSA SUA - no man shall be a judge in his own cause (rule against bias). Types of bias: (a) PECUNIARY BIAS - even a small pecuniary interest disqualifies (Dr. Bonham's case, 1610; Dimes v Grand Junction Canal, 1852 - Lord Chancellor held shares in the company; decree set aside; Jeejeebhoy v Assistant Collector, Thana (1965): the disqualification applies to a pecuniary interest); (b) PERSONAL BIAS - hostility, friendship, relationship (Mineral Development Ltd v State of Bihar, 1960; A.K. Kraipak); (c) SUBJECT-MATTER BIAS - a direct interest in the subject matter or the policy - not ordinary (Gullapalli Nageswara Rao v Andhra Pradesh SRTC, 1959: the Secretary who heard objections was the one who proposed the scheme - disqualified; the Minister's role - departmental bias); (d) OFFICIAL/ DEPARTMENTAL BIAS (R v Sussex Justices ex p McCarthy, 1924: 'it is not merely of some importance but is of fundamental importance that justice should not only be done, but should manifestly and undoubtedly be seen to be done'); (e) PREJUDGMENT. TEST: 'REAL LIKELIHOOD OF BIAS' vs 'REASONABLE SUSPICION' - Indian courts follow the real likelihood test (Manak Lal v Dr Prem Chand Singhvi, 1957; S. Parthasarathi v State of Andhra Pradesh, 1973: the test is whether a reasonable man would think there was a likelihood of bias; Ranjit Thakur v Union of India, 1987: bias of a commanding officer who was the complainant and the judge; Rattan Lal Sharma v Managing Committee Dr Hari Ram Co-ed Higher Secondary School, 1993). In India the 'reasonable apprehension of bias' test is applied; the actual proof of bias is not necessary. DOCTRINE OF NECESSITY: a biased authority may act if no other authority is competent (Election Commission of India v Dr. Subramaniam Swamy, 1996; Ashok Kumar Yadav v State of Haryana, 1985 - members of the Public Service Commission who had relatives among candidates must withdraw).
  3. (2) AUDI ALTERAM PARTEM - hear the other side: elements - (a) NOTICE: adequate, clear, stating the grounds/ charges (Kanda v Government of Malaya); Canara Bank v Debasis Das (2003): natural justice: notice, an opportunity to explain, impartial, reasoned order; (b) DISCLOSURE of materials relied on - Kanungo & Co v Collector of Customs (1972): disclosure of the material relied upon and the right to cross-examine is part of a fair hearing; Hira Nath Mishra v Principal, Rajendra Medical College (1973): in-camera enquiry - a fair hearing - cross-examination can be dispensed with if the witnesses fear victimisation and the substance of evidence is disclosed; (c) HEARING - oral hearing is not always required - written representation may suffice (Union of India v J.P. Mitter, 1971); (d) CROSS-EXAMINATION and legal representation are not necessary in every case (J.K. Aggarwal v Haryana Seeds Development Corporation, 1991; Board of Trustees of Port of Bombay v Dilip Kumar Raghavendranath Nadkarni, 1983: a right to representation by a lawyer is not absolute - fairness where the employer is represented by a legally trained person); (e) REASONED DECISION; (f) EVIDENCE.
  4. 'USELESS FORMALITY' / PREJUDICE THEORY: S.L. Kapoor v Jagmohan (1980): where the facts are admitted or undisputed and only one conclusion is possible, a hearing may be dispensed with as it would be a useless formality; M.C. Mehta v Union of India (1999); State of UP v Sudhir Kumar Singh (2020): the Supreme Court summarised that the violation of natural justice must cause prejudice to the aggrieved - 'prejudice' must be demonstrated; in the case of procedural violations the courts examine whether the omitted procedure would have made any difference. Contrasting view: in cases of denial of a hearing, the order is void ab initio (Nawabkhan Abbaskhan v State of Gujarat, 1974 - prejudice need not be shown where natural justice is breached in a fundamental way).
  5. EXCLUSION/ EXCEPTIONS: (1) by statute (but any such exclusion must be reasonable - Art 14 and 21); (2) emergency/ urgency (a post-decisional hearing: Maneka Gandhi; Liberty Oil Mills v Union of India, 1984); (3) legislative acts/ general policy (no hearing required for legislative action - Union of India v Cynamide India Ltd, 1987); (4) impracticability (mass cases); (5) confidentiality/ national security (Council of Civil Service Unions v Minister - national security exception); (6) academic judgments (Maharashtra State Board of Secondary and Higher Secondary Education v K.S. Gandhi, 1991: a hearing is not required to disqualify a candidate for copying where the examination authorities followed the process; Jawaharlal Nehru University v B.S. Narwal, 1980); (7) preliminary investigations (no hearing at the stage of a preliminary inquiry; but a show cause is needed at final stage - Union of India v W.N. Chadha, 1993: no hearing at the stage of investigation to issue letters rogatory); (8) 'no prejudice'.
  6. Specific contexts: (i) Departmental inquiry - Art 311(2): reasonable opportunity of being heard in respect of charges; exceptions in the second proviso (Union of India v Tulsiram Patel, 1985: the Court upheld the second proviso clauses (a), (b) and (c) - dismissal without inquiry when the conduct led to conviction on a criminal charge; where it's not reasonably practicable to hold an inquiry; or in the interest of the security of the State - but the reasons must be recorded and the order is subject to judicial review). (ii) Termination of employment of probationers or temporary employees: if the termination is a punishment (stigma), enquiry required (Parshotam Lal Dhingra v Union of India, 1958 - tests: whether the servant has a right to the post and whether the order involves evil consequences; Samsher Singh v State of Punjab, 1974). (iii) Compulsory retirement of public servants. (iv) Professional misconduct - Bar Councils. (v) Licensing and cancellation (Maneka Gandhi; Olga Tellis v Bombay Municipal Corporation, 1985: pavement dwellers - eviction needs fair procedure; right to livelihood part of Art 21). (vi) Demolitions: In Re: Directions in the matter of demolition of structures (13 November 2024) (the 'bulldozer justice' judgment) - Supreme Court issued pan-India guidelines: no demolition without a prior show-cause notice (at least 15 days to the owner/ occupier, served by registered post and affixed on the structure; contents - nature of unauthorised construction, details of the violation and grounds for demolition, date for personal hearing and the designated authority); a personal hearing with minutes recorded; a final reasoned order, with 15 days after the order for the owner to challenge/ remove; the order to be uploaded on a digital portal; demolition to be videographed with an inspection/ demolition report; violation of the directions is contempt and the officials are personally liable to restore the structure at their own cost and pay compensation; the directions do not apply to unauthorised structures in public places like roads, streets, footpaths, railway lines or water bodies or where a court has ordered demolition. In July 2026 the Supreme Court sent contempt petitions alleging violations of the judgment to the High Courts (verify).

✎Memory aids

  • Types of bias = P-P-S: Pecuniary, Personal, Subject-matter.

!Exam traps

  • TrapPecuniary bias disqualifies automatically; other bias needs real likelihood/ reasonable apprehension.
  • TrapExceptions: urgency, legislative action, confidentiality/ security, academic evaluation, impracticability.
  • TrapPost-decisional hearing may satisfy natural justice in emergencies (Maneka Gandhi).
  • Trap'Useless formality' theory vs prejudice test - violation + prejudice.
