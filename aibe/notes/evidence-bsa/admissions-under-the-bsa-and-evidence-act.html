Admissions (S.15-21 BSA approx.; IEA S.17-31)

iOverview

Admission is a statement, oral, documentary or electronic, that suggests any inference as to a fact in issue or a relevant fact, made by a person in the capacities stated in the Act. It is not conclusive proof but may operate as estoppel.

★Must know

  1. Statement may be oral, documentary or contained in electronic form (BSA expressly includes electronic form).
  2. Who can make admissions that bind a party: party to the proceeding; agent expressly or impliedly authorised; party suing in a representative character (only while holding that character); persons with a proprietary or pecuniary interest in the subject matter (while holding that interest); persons from whom the parties derive interest (assignor); persons whose position must be proved against the party; persons expressly referred to by the party.
  3. Admissions are relevant and may be proved against the maker or his representative, but cannot be proved by or on behalf of the maker except in specified cases (e.g. a statement relevant as a dying declaration, in the course of business, or showing state of mind).
  4. Admission v. confession: a confession is an admission in a criminal case that suggests the inference that the maker committed the crime; all confessions are admissions but not vice versa. Admissions are not subject to the stringent exclusions of confessions (S.22-23).
  5. Admissions are not conclusive proof but may operate as estoppel (IEA S.31). They are the best evidence against the maker but can be explained or shown to be wrong (Narayan Bhagwantrao Gosavi Balajiwale v. Gopal Vinayak Gosavi, 1960).
  6. Oral admissions as to the contents of documents are not relevant unless secondary evidence is permissible or the genuineness of the document is in question (IEA S.22). The same rule extends to electronic records (S.22A).
  7. Admissions in civil cases are not relevant if made upon an express condition that evidence shall not be given or in circumstances from which the Court can infer an agreement that it shall not be given (IEA S.23).
  8. Written admissions by a party need not be put to him to prove them; the party is entitled to rely on the whole statement and not just the unfavourable part (rule of completeness).

⚖Landmark cases

Bharat Singh v. Bhagirathi

1966

Admissions in pleadings and in other documents are substantive evidence; but the maker is entitled to prove the circumstances.

Nagubai Ammal v. B. Shama Rao

1956

Admission in an earlier document, if not explained, is relevant evidence against the maker.

Basant Singh v. Janki Singh

1967

Admissions in pleadings are binding; admissions of a co-defendant are not binding on others.

✎Memory aids

  • All confessions are admissions; not all admissions are confessions.

!Exam traps

  • TrapAn admission is not conclusive proof - it can be rebutted or explained.
  • TrapRepresentative character: admissions bind only while the character subsists.
  • TrapAdmission by agent binds principal only if made during the continuance of the agency and within authority.

?Likely questions

  1. Admission is conclusive proof? (No - evidentiary value, estoppel possible)
  2. Difference between admission and confession.
  3. Who can make an admission that binds a party?
