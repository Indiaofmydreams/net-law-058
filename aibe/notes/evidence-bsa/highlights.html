1

Electronic and Digital Evidence (S.2(1)(d)-(e), S.39(2), S.57, S.61-63 BSA; Schedule)

Very high yieldRead the full chapter →

Key provisions

S.63 BSASuccessor of S.65B IEA. Applies to civil and criminal proceedings.
S.65A IEAOld rule: contents of electronic records may be proved only per S.65B. Now S.62 BSA.
S.22A IEAOral admissions as to contents of electronic records irrelevant unless genuineness of the record is in question - retained in substance in the BSA.
S.81A, 85A, 85B, 85C, 88A, 90A IEAPresumptions: Gazette in electronic form; electronic agreements; secure electronic records and signatures; Electronic Signature Certificates; electronic messages (no presumption as to the sender); electronic records five years old. Continued in the BSA presumptions chapter.

Cases in one line

  • State (NCT of Delhi) v. Navjot Sandhu (Parliament attack case) 2005 Liberal view: even without a S.65B certificate, secondary evidence of electronic records (call records) could be given under S.63/65 IEA. Overruled on this point by Anvar.
  • Anvar P.V. v. P.K. Basheer 2014 Three-judge bench: S.65B is a special provision; a certificate under S.65B(4) is mandatory for secondary electronic evidence; oral evidence cannot substitute the certificate; Navjot Sandhu overruled.
  • Shafhi Mohammad v. State of H.P. 2018 Certificate not needed where the party does not have the device (procedural relaxation). Later declared wrongly decided.
  • Arjun Panditrao Khotkar v. Kailash Kushanrao Gorantyal 2020 Three-judge bench: Anvar affirmed, Shafhi overruled. Certificate is a mandatory condition for secondary electronic evidence; may be produced at any stage before the trial closes, and the Court can direct its production; no certificate is needed when the original device is itself produced and proved (primary evidence).
  • Pune Bar Association v. Union of India (W.P.(C) 599 of 2026), decided 22 May 2026 2026 Supreme Court upheld the constitutional validity of S.63(4) BSA and its Schedule - the hash-value disclosure and expert certification have a rational nexus with authenticity and integrity of electronic evidence. The Court held that Part B can be signed by any person with special skill in computer science and cyber forensics and is not confined to an Examiner of Electronic Evidence notified under S.79A IT Act; it did not treat the contrary Madras High Court view as binding precedent, though the question was kept open for fuller examination in a proper case.
  • R.M. Malkani v. State of Maharashtra 1973 Tape-recorded conversation is admissible if relevant, the voice is identified and accuracy is established; evidence obtained illegally is not excluded merely on that ground.
  • Ziyauddin Burhanuddin Bukhari v. Brijmohan Mehra 1976 Conditions for admissibility of tape records: voice identification, accuracy, absence of tampering, relevance.
  • Tomaso Bruno v. State of U.P. 2015 CCTV footage is relevant electronic evidence; failure to produce available CCTV can invite adverse inference.

Memory aids

  • A-A-P-P: Anvar (2014) -> Arjun (2020) -> Pune Bar (2026) - the certificate story.
  • S.63 = old 65B; S.62 = old 65A; S.61 = admissibility not denied.
  • Hash = digital fingerprint; Part A = operator, Part B = expert.

Exam traps

  • TrapS.63 certificate is needed for printouts, CDs, pen drives, screenshots, WhatsApp exports - but NOT when the original device is produced and proved.
  • TrapOral evidence cannot replace the S.63(4) certificate (Anvar, Arjun Panditrao).
  • TrapNew BSA requirement: hash value + TWO signatories (person in charge AND expert). Old S.65B(4) needed only one person in a responsible position.
  • TrapNavjot Sandhu = overruled (by Anvar). Shafhi Mohammad = overruled (by Arjun Panditrao). Do not pick these as 'good law'.
  • TrapElectronic evidence is not 'hearsay' by nature; admissibility and weight are different questions.
  • TrapS.63 applies to civil as well as criminal proceedings.
2

Confessions and Discovery (S.22-24 BSA; IEA S.24-30)

Very high yieldRead the full chapter →

Cases in one line

  • Pakala Narayana Swami v. Emperor 1939 Definition of confession; admission of incriminating fact is not confession.
  • Palvinder Kaur v. State of Punjab 1952 Confession must be taken as a whole; exculpatory part cannot be rejected selectively.
  • Pulukuri Kottaya v. Emperor 1947 Scope of discovery under S.27; only the information distinctly related to the discovered fact is provable.
  • State of U.P. v. Deoman Upadhyaya 1960 S.27 is a proviso to S.26; applies even if accused was not formally under arrest if in police custody; constitutional validity upheld.
  • State of Bombay v. Kathi Kalu Oghad 1961 Giving specimen handwriting/fingerprints is not testimonial compulsion under Art 20(3); S.27 information given by accused is not barred by Art 20(3).
  • Aghnoo Nagesia v. State of Bihar 1966 A confessional FIR lodged by the accused is hit by S.25 IEA and cannot be proved against him.
  • Kashmira Singh v. State of M.P. 1952 Co-accused confession cannot be the basis of conviction; only lends assurance.
  • Haricharan Kurmi v. State of Bihar 1964 Co-accused confession is not evidence in the strict sense of S.3; it is a factor to be considered after other evidence is examined.
  • Selvi v. State of Karnataka 2010 Involuntary narco-analysis, polygraph and BEAP tests violate Art 20(3) and Art 21; but facts discovered pursuant to voluntary tests may be admissible under S.27.
  • Tofan Singh v. State of Tamil Nadu 2020 NDPS officers are police officers; S.67 NDPS statements are inadmissible as confessions.

Memory aids

  • 22 = Pressure out, 23 = Police out (but discovery in), 24 = Partner's confession (co-accused).
  • Pulukuri = Place; Kathi Kalu = Kopy (handwriting/fingerprint, not testimony); Deoman = Detained.

Exam traps

  • TrapS.27 / S.23(2) proviso is not limited to confessions - 'whether it amounts to a confession or not'.
  • TrapThe information is admissible only if it leads to discovery of a fact; the portion before/after that is excluded.
  • TrapConfession by co-accused requires JOINT trial and same offence; it is not substantive evidence.
  • TrapA statement made to a Magistrate in custody is admissible only if it is a confession recorded in his immediate presence.
  • TrapS.22 requires the inducement to come from a person in authority; 'coercion' is the new word in the BSA.
  • TrapConfession to a police officer remains inadmissible even if the accused is not in custody when making it.
3

Burden of Proof, Presumptions and Estoppel (S.104-123 BSA; IEA S.101-117)

Very high yieldRead the full chapter →

Cases in one line

  • Woolmington v. DPP 1935 Golden thread: the prosecution must prove guilt beyond reasonable doubt; the accused's burden is only to raise a reasonable doubt.
  • Shambhu Nath Mehra v. State of Ajmer 1956 S.106 does not shift the burden of the prosecution; it applies only to facts specially within the accused's knowledge.
  • Sharad Birdhichand Sarda v. State of Maharashtra 1984 Five golden principles for conviction on circumstantial evidence (panchsheel); S.106 cannot fill gaps in the prosecution case; also on dying declaration.
  • Trimukh Maroti Kirkan v. State of Maharashtra 2006 In offences in the privacy of a home, the accused who is the only other person present must offer an explanation (S.106).
  • Dahyabhai Chhaganbhai Thakker v. State of Gujarat 1964 Burden on the accused under S.105 is by preponderance of probability.
  • Kans Raj v. State of Punjab 2000 Interpretation of 'soon before her death' in S.304B/113B - a proximate and live link is needed.
  • Satbir Singh v. State of Haryana 2021 Three-judge bench: the presumption under S.113B and S.113A; 'soon before' is flexible and not a fixed period.
  • Dukhtar Jahan v. Mohd. Farooq 1987 S.112 presumption is conclusive; rebuttal only by proof of non-access.
  • Goutam Kundu v. State of West Bengal 1993 Blood test cannot be ordered as a routine; strong prima facie case of non-access is needed.
  • Nandlal Wasudeo Badwaik v. Lata Nandlal Badwaik 2014 A reliable DNA test result prevails over the conclusive presumption of legitimacy (S.112) when the husband succeeds in proving non-access/paternity facts.
  • Aparna Ajinkya Firodia v. Ajinkya Arun Firodia 2023 DNA tests of a child cannot be ordered casually; Court must weigh the child's best interests and privacy.
  • Motilal Padampat Sugar Mills v. State of U.P. 1979 Promissory estoppel is an equitable doctrine distinct from S.115 estoppel; it is enforceable against the Government.

Memory aids

  • 'Soon before' = proximity, not a stopwatch.
  • 7-7-7: seven years (suicide presumption), seven years (not heard of), seven years (marriage period for dowry death).

Exam traps

  • TrapS.113A = 'may presume'; S.113B = 'shall presume'; S.114A = 'shall presume'. Examiners swap these words.
  • TrapPresumption of legitimacy is conclusive, but access/non-access is the only rebuttal.
  • TrapS.106 (special knowledge) never relieves the prosecution of proving the foundation facts.
  • TrapSeven-year presumption of death does not tell the date of death.
  • TrapBurden under general exceptions is on the accused but the standard is only a balance of probabilities.
  • TrapAccomplice: S.133 IEA says conviction on accomplice testimony is not illegal, S.114(b) says the Court may presume he is unworthy of credit - so corroboration is a rule of prudence (Bhiva Doulu Patil).
4

Dying Declaration and Statements of Persons who cannot be called as witnesses (S.26-27 BSA; IEA S.32-33)

Very high yieldRead the full chapter →

Cases in one line

  • Pakala Narayana Swami v. Emperor 1939 'Circumstances of the transaction' covers statements beyond the mere act of injury, e.g. statements of motive and prior events.
  • Khushal Rao v. State of Bombay 1958 A DD is not weaker than any other evidence; it can be the sole basis of conviction.
  • Laxman v. State of Maharashtra 2002 A medical certificate of fitness is not a sine qua non; what matters is the Court's satisfaction that the declarant was in a fit state of mind.
  • Paniben v. State of Gujarat 1992 Summarised principles for evaluating dying declarations.
  • Sharad Birdhichand Sarda v. State of Maharashtra 1984 DD may include statements about the circumstances leading to death, including suicidal statements.
  • Irfan @ Naka v. State of U.P. 2023 Handling of multiple dying declarations; reliance on the one that inspires confidence.

Memory aids

  • Nemo moriturus praesumitur mentiri = no one dies telling a lie.
  • Laxman = Lack of mandatory doctor's certificate.

Exam traps

  • TrapIndian law does not require the declarant to be under expectation of death (unlike English law).
  • TrapA DD recorded by a police officer or doctor is not invalid merely because no Magistrate recorded it.
  • TrapAn unsigned or incomplete DD is not automatically invalid; evidence on the declarant's fitness matters.
  • TrapCorroboration is not mandatory but is a rule of prudence where the DD is suspicious.
5

Documentary Evidence - Primary, Secondary, Proof of Execution and Oral v Documentary (S.56-60, S.64-103 BSA; IEA S.61-100)

High yieldRead the full chapter →

Cases in one line

  • H. Siddiqui v. A. Ramalingam 2011 Secondary evidence cannot be given without laying the foundation under S.65; non-production of the original must be satisfactorily explained.
  • Roman Catholic Mission v. State of Madras 1966 Where the original is not produced, secondary evidence can be given only on satisfying the conditions of S.65.
  • Roop Kumar v. Mohan Thedani 2003 Interpretation of S.91-92: when a document is the sole repository of the terms of a contract, oral evidence is excluded.
  • H. Venkatachala Iyengar v. B.N. Thimmajamma 1959 Principles on proof of Wills and removal of suspicious circumstances.
  • Janki Narayan Bhoir v. Narayan Namdeo Kadam 2003 Calling an attesting witness is a mandatory requirement to prove a Will; mere proof of testator's signature is insufficient.
  • Meena Pradhan v. Kamla Pradhan 2023 Reiterated rules on proof of Will and burden on the propounder to dispel suspicious circumstances.
  • R.V.E. Venkatachala Gounder v. Arulmigu Viswesaraswami 2003 Objection to the MODE of proof (e.g. unstamped/secondary copy) must be taken when the document is tendered, not later; objection to admissibility on law can be taken later.

Memory aids

  • S.65: Always Bring Close Duplicates Probably Fully (a-b-c-d-e-f-g cases).
  • Provisos to S.92: F-Mistake-Silence-Condition-Subsequent-Usage-Language.

Exam traps

  • TrapPrimary evidence: photocopies of a common original are NOT primary.
  • TrapSection 91 excludes oral evidence about the terms; S.92 excludes oral agreements contradicting them. Proviso (1) - fraud etc. - is the favourite exception.
  • TrapSecondary evidence needs a foundation (notice, non-availability) and cannot be admitted as of right.
  • TrapRegistered documents need no attesting witness, but a will always needs one.
  • TrapUnattested document does not require attesting witness proof.
  • TrapPatent ambiguity cannot be explained; latent ambiguity can.

Witnesses: Competence, Privileges, Accomplice, Number of Witnesses (S.124-139 BSA approx.; IEA S.118-134)

High yieldRead the full chapter →

Cases in one line

  • Vadivelu Thevar v. State of Madras 1957 Classification of witnesses; quality over quantity; a single reliable witness may suffice.
  • Rameshwar v. State of Rajasthan 1952 Corroboration of a sexual-offence victim is a rule of prudence, not of law.
  • State of Punjab v. Gurmit Singh 1996 Courts should treat rape victims with sensitivity; sole testimony can convict.
  • Dattu Ramrao Sakhare v. State of Maharashtra 1997 A child witness is competent if he understands the questions; evidence needs careful scrutiny and corroboration as a rule of prudence.
  • M.C. Verghese v. T.J. Ponnan 1969 Spousal communication privilege under S.122 applies to communications made during marriage; third parties can testify.
  • State of U.P. v. Raj Narain 1975 Claim of privilege under S.123 is not conclusive; Court can examine.
  • Bhiva Doulu Patil v. State of Maharashtra 1963 Reconciles S.133 with S.114(b): accomplice evidence needs corroboration as a rule of practice.

Memory aids

  • WIT: Witness Is Competent unless prevented (118).

Exam traps

  • TrapChild witnesses are competent; there is no minimum age.
  • TrapS.133: conviction on uncorroborated accomplice testimony is legally valid but prudence requires corroboration.
  • TrapMarital privilege does not apply in suits between spouses or when a spouse is prosecuted for crime against the other.
  • TrapA witness cannot refuse to answer on the ground of self-incrimination under this Act - but the answer is protected.
  • TrapAdvocate-client privilege is the client's privilege - only he can waive it.
7

Examination of Witnesses: Leading Questions, Hostile Witness, Cross-examination, Judge's Powers (S.140-169 BSA approx.; IEA S.135-167)

High yieldRead the full chapter →

Cases in one line

  • Sat Paul v. Delhi Administration 1976 Hostile witness: there is no need to declare a witness 'hostile' - the Court may permit cross-examination by the calling party.
  • Bhagwan Singh v. State of Haryana 1976 The evidence of a witness who is declared hostile is not wholly rejected.
  • Khujji @ Surendra Tiwari v. State of M.P. 1991 Hostile witness's testimony can be relied on to the extent it is corroborated or trustworthy.
  • Zahira Habibulla H. Sheikh v. State of Gujarat 2006 Court cannot be a mere spectator; duty to ensure a fair trial and to examine witnesses under S.165.
  • Ram Chander v. State of Haryana 1981 Judge's role under S.165 - to elicit truth, not to merely record.
  • State of Rajasthan v. Ani @ Hanif 1997 Sole eye-witness testimony and the importance of minor discrepancies being disregarded.

Memory aids

  • C-C-R: Chief - Cross - Re-exam; Leading allowed only in the middle one.

Exam traps

  • TrapLeading questions are permitted in cross, restricted in chief and re-exam.
  • TrapA hostile witness's entire testimony is not automatically discarded.
  • TrapA Judge cannot be accused of partiality merely for putting questions under S.165/168.
  • TrapFailure to cross-examine on a material point may amount to acceptance of that part of the testimony.
8

Admissions (S.15-21 BSA approx.; IEA S.17-31)

High yieldRead the full chapter →

Cases in one line

  • Bharat Singh v. Bhagirathi 1966 Admissions in pleadings and in other documents are substantive evidence; but the maker is entitled to prove the circumstances.
  • Nagubai Ammal v. B. Shama Rao 1956 Admission in an earlier document, if not explained, is relevant evidence against the maker.
  • Basant Singh v. Janki Singh 1967 Admissions in pleadings are binding; admissions of a co-defendant are not binding on others.

Memory aids

  • All confessions are admissions; not all admissions are confessions.

Exam traps

  • TrapAn admission is not conclusive proof - it can be rebutted or explained.
  • TrapRepresentative character: admissions bind only while the character subsists.
  • TrapAdmission by agent binds principal only if made during the continuance of the agency and within authority.
9

Relevancy of Facts: Res Gestae, Motive, Conspiracy, Similar Facts, Identification (S.3-14 BSA approx.; IEA S.5-16, 9)

Medium yieldRead the full chapter →

Cases in one line

  • Gentela Vijayavardhan Rao v. State of A.P. 1996 Res gestae requires spontaneity and immediate connection with the transaction.
  • Kehar Singh v. State (Delhi Administration) 1988 Conspiracy is rarely proved by direct evidence; Section 10 applies once prima facie conspiracy is shown.
  • State (NCT of Delhi) v. Navjot Sandhu 2005 Conspiracy principles under S.10 applied to the Parliament attack.
  • Matru v. State of U.P. 1971 TIP is not substantive evidence; it only corroborates court identification.

Memory aids

  • Res gestae = same transaction; Section 10 = 'Co-conspirator's words bind co-conspirators'.

Exam traps

  • TrapS.10 (conspiracy) applies only after reasonable ground to believe in the conspiracy is established.
  • TrapTIP is corroborative; substantive evidence is the identification in court.
  • TrapRes gestae must be contemporaneous - an afterthought narrative is excluded.
10

Opinion of Experts, Character, Judgments, Judicial Notice and Facts Admitted (S.34-55 BSA approx.; IEA S.40-58)

Medium yieldRead the full chapter →

Cases in one line

  • Murari Lal v. State of Madhya Pradesh 1980 Court is not bound by expert opinion on handwriting; it may itself compare; but caution is advised.
  • State of Himachal Pradesh v. Jai Lal 1999 Expert must give reasons; the opinion is not conclusive.
  • Ramesh Chandra Agrawal v. Regency Hospital Ltd. 2009 Expert must be a person with special skill; the report must give data and reasoning.
  • Karam Chand Ganga Prasad v. Union of India 1970 Criminal court's findings are not binding in civil proceedings.

Memory aids

  • Judgments: 40-41-42-43-44 = Bar - Conclusive - In rem - Irrelevant - Challenge (fraud/collusion).

Exam traps

  • TrapExpert evidence is not binding - the Court may reject it for reasons.
  • TrapS.41 judgments are conclusive (judgment in rem); other judgments are only relevant, not conclusive.
  • TrapJudicial notice vs proof: no proof required for facts judicially noticed.
  • TrapHearsay is inadmissible except where an express exception applies (dying declaration, business entries, etc.).
