Dying Declaration and Statements of Persons who cannot be called as witnesses (S.26-27 BSA; IEA S.32-33)

iOverview

A dying declaration is a relevant exception to the hearsay rule resting on the maxim nemo moriturus praesumitur mentiri. It can be the sole basis of conviction if it is voluntary, truthful and inspires confidence.

★Must know

  1. S.26(1)(a) BSA (IEA 32(1)): statement made by a person as to the cause of his death or as to any of the circumstances of the transaction which resulted in his death is relevant in proceedings in which the cause of his death comes into question - whether or not he was expecting death at the time of making it.
  2. Other categories (S.26(1)(b)-(h)): statements made in the course of business, against pecuniary or proprietary interest, giving opinion as to public right or custom, relating to the existence of relationship by blood/marriage, in a will or deed relating to family affairs, in a document relating to a transaction, and made by several persons expressing feelings.
  3. Magistrate's presence and doctor's certificate are not mandatory (Laxman v. State of Maharashtra, 2002 - Constitution Bench); if there is doubt about the declarant's fitness, medical evidence assumes importance.
  4. Conviction can rest solely on a DD if it is trustworthy; corroboration is a rule of prudence, not of law (Khushal Rao, 1958; Kundula Bala Subrahmanyam, 1993; Paniben, 1992).
  5. Multiple dying declarations: if consistent, the Court can rely on any; if inconsistent, the Court examines which one is voluntary, truthful and corroborated (Irfan @ Naka v. State of U.P., 2023 and the line before it).
  6. A DD need not be in question-answer form; it may be oral, gestures (nod) or written. A DD of an accused is also covered.
  7. S.27 BSA (IEA 33): evidence given by a witness in a previous judicial proceeding is relevant in a later stage to prove the truth of the facts it states when the witness is dead, cannot be found, is incapable of giving evidence, kept out of the way by the adverse party, or his attendance cannot be procured without unreasonable delay/expense - provided the proceeding was between the same parties, the adverse party had a right and opportunity to cross-examine, and the questions in issue were substantially the same.

⚖Landmark cases

Pakala Narayana Swami v. Emperor

1939

'Circumstances of the transaction' covers statements beyond the mere act of injury, e.g. statements of motive and prior events.

Khushal Rao v. State of Bombay

1958

A DD is not weaker than any other evidence; it can be the sole basis of conviction.

Laxman v. State of Maharashtra

2002

A medical certificate of fitness is not a sine qua non; what matters is the Court's satisfaction that the declarant was in a fit state of mind.

Paniben v. State of Gujarat

1992

Summarised principles for evaluating dying declarations.

Sharad Birdhichand Sarda v. State of Maharashtra

1984

DD may include statements about the circumstances leading to death, including suicidal statements.

Irfan @ Naka v. State of U.P.

2023

Handling of multiple dying declarations; reliance on the one that inspires confidence.

✎Memory aids

  • Nemo moriturus praesumitur mentiri = no one dies telling a lie.
  • Laxman = Lack of mandatory doctor's certificate.

!Exam traps

  • TrapIndian law does not require the declarant to be under expectation of death (unlike English law).
  • TrapA DD recorded by a police officer or doctor is not invalid merely because no Magistrate recorded it.
  • TrapAn unsigned or incomplete DD is not automatically invalid; evidence on the declarant's fitness matters.
  • TrapCorroboration is not mandatory but is a rule of prudence where the DD is suspicious.

?Likely questions

  1. Is the Magistrate's presence mandatory for a DD? (No)
  2. Can a DD be the sole basis of conviction?
  3. Does the BSA/IEA require the declarant to expect death?
  4. Which case: certification by doctor not essential (Laxman)?
