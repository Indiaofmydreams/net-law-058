Witnesses: Competence, Privileges, Accomplice, Number of Witnesses (S.124-139 BSA approx.; IEA S.118-134)

iOverview

Competence is the rule; privilege is the exception. Learn who is competent, who cannot be compelled, advocate-client privilege (S.132 BSA) and the rule that no particular number of witnesses is needed.

★Must know

  1. Competence (IEA S.118): all persons are competent to testify unless the Court considers that they are prevented from understanding the questions or giving rational answers by tender years, extreme old age, disease (body or mind) or any similar cause. A child has no fixed minimum age; the Court tests competence by preliminary questions (voir dire) - Dattu Ramrao Sakhare (1997); Rameshwar v. State of Rajasthan (1952). A dumb witness may give evidence by signs or writing in open court (IEA S.119).
  2. Spouses (IEA S.120): in civil proceedings, parties and their spouses are competent; in criminal proceedings against any person, the spouse is a competent witness.
  3. Judges and Magistrates (IEA S.121): not compelled to answer questions about their own conduct in court, except on special order of a superior Court.
  4. Marital communications (IEA S.122): no person who is or has been married shall be compelled to disclose communications made during marriage, except in suits between married persons or proceedings where one is prosecuted for a crime against the other.
  5. Affairs of State (IEA S.123): unpublished official records relating to affairs of State cannot be given in evidence without the permission of the head of department - the Court decides on privilege claims (State of U.P. v. Raj Narain, 1975; S.P. Gupta, 1981).
  6. Professional communications (IEA S.126 / S.132 BSA): no advocate shall be permitted, without the client's express consent, to disclose communications made in the course and for the purpose of employment, or contents of documents with which he became acquainted, or advice given. Applies to interpreters, clerks and servants of the advocate. Exceptions: communications in furtherance of an illegal purpose; facts observed by the advocate during employment showing a crime or fraud since its commencement. Privilege survives the end of employment. In-house counsel are not within the privilege as 'advocates' (Supreme Court, October 2025 suo motu case on summoning of advocates - verify the holding on summons procedure).
  7. Confidential communications with legal advisers (IEA S.129): no one can be compelled to disclose confidential communications with his legal adviser unless he offers himself as a witness.
  8. Witness not excused on ground that the answer will incriminate him (IEA S.132), but such answer cannot be used against him in criminal proceedings (other than for giving false evidence) - an aspect harmonised with Art 20(3).
  9. Accomplice (IEA S.133): an accomplice is a competent witness and a conviction is not illegal merely because it proceeds on his uncorroborated testimony; the rule of prudence in S.114 illustration (b) requires corroboration in material particulars as to the identity of the accused (Bhiva Doulu Patil, 1963; Sarwan Singh, 1957).
  10. Number of witnesses (IEA S.134): no particular number is required for the proof of any fact. Vadivelu Thevar: witnesses are classified as wholly reliable, wholly unreliable, or neither - the Court needs corroboration only for the third class. The sole testimony of a sexual-offence victim may be sufficient (State of Punjab v. Gurmit Singh, 1996).

⚖Landmark cases

Vadivelu Thevar v. State of Madras

1957

Classification of witnesses; quality over quantity; a single reliable witness may suffice.

Rameshwar v. State of Rajasthan

1952

Corroboration of a sexual-offence victim is a rule of prudence, not of law.

State of Punjab v. Gurmit Singh

1996

Courts should treat rape victims with sensitivity; sole testimony can convict.

Dattu Ramrao Sakhare v. State of Maharashtra

1997

A child witness is competent if he understands the questions; evidence needs careful scrutiny and corroboration as a rule of prudence.

M.C. Verghese v. T.J. Ponnan

1969

Spousal communication privilege under S.122 applies to communications made during marriage; third parties can testify.

State of U.P. v. Raj Narain

1975

Claim of privilege under S.123 is not conclusive; Court can examine.

Bhiva Doulu Patil v. State of Maharashtra

1963

Reconciles S.133 with S.114(b): accomplice evidence needs corroboration as a rule of practice.

!Exam traps

  • TrapChild witnesses are competent; there is no minimum age.
  • TrapS.133: conviction on uncorroborated accomplice testimony is legally valid but prudence requires corroboration.
  • TrapMarital privilege does not apply in suits between spouses or when a spouse is prosecuted for crime against the other.
  • TrapA witness cannot refuse to answer on the ground of self-incrimination under this Act - but the answer is protected.
  • TrapAdvocate-client privilege is the client's privilege - only he can waive it.
