Burden of Proof, Presumptions and Estoppel (S.104-123 BSA; IEA S.101-117)

iOverview

The most formula-driven chapter. Know who bears the burden, what 'may presume / shall presume / conclusive proof' mean, and the four statutory presumptions (legitimacy, abetment of suicide, dowry death, absence of consent in rape) that the examiners repeatedly test.

★Must know

  1. S.104 (IEA 101): whoever desires the Court to give judgment on a legal right or liability dependent on facts that he asserts must prove that those facts exist.
  2. S.105 (IEA 102): the burden of proof in a suit lies on the person who would fail if no evidence at all were given on either side.
  3. S.106 (IEA 103): the burden of proving any particular fact lies on the person who wishes the Court to believe in its existence, unless the law provides otherwise.
  4. S.107 (IEA 104): burden of proving a fact necessary to make evidence admissible lies on the person who wants to give that evidence.
  5. S.108 (IEA 105): burden of proving that the case falls within a General Exception (or any special exception/proviso) lies on the accused; the Court shall presume absence of such circumstances. The accused need only meet the standard of preponderance of probabilities, not beyond reasonable doubt (Dahyabhai Chhaganbhai Thakker, 1964).
  6. S.109 (IEA 106): when a fact is especially within the knowledge of a person, the burden of proving it is on him. It does not relieve the prosecution of its initial burden (Shambhu Nath Mehra, 1956); applied to homicides inside a house (Trimukh Maroti Kirkan, 2006) and to the 'last seen together' theory.
  7. S.110 (IEA 107): person alive within 30 years - burden on the one who says he is dead.
  8. S.111 (IEA 108): person not heard of for seven years by those who would naturally have heard - burden shifts to the one who affirms he is alive. There is no presumption about the date of death.
  9. S.112-S.114 (IEA 109-111): burden in partnership/landlord-tenant/principal-agent relationships; ownership; transactions where one party is in a position of active confidence.
  10. S.116 (IEA 112): birth during the continuance of a valid marriage, or within 280 days after its dissolution, the mother remaining unmarried, is CONCLUSIVE PROOF of legitimacy - rebuttable only by proof that the parties had no access at the relevant time.
  11. S.117 (IEA 113A): where a married woman commits suicide within seven years of marriage and her husband or his relative subjected her to cruelty, the Court MAY presume abetment (read with BNS S.108 / cruelty S.85-86).
  12. S.118 (IEA 113B): in a dowry death prosecution, if it is shown that soon before her death the woman was subjected to cruelty or harassment for or in connection with dowry, the Court SHALL presume the accused caused the dowry death (BNS S.80 / IPC 304B).
  13. S.119 (IEA 114): the Court MAY presume the existence of any fact it thinks likely to have happened. Key illustrations: (a) a person in possession of stolen goods soon after the theft is presumed to be the thief or to have received them knowing them stolen unless he accounts for possession; (b) an accomplice is unworthy of credit unless corroborated; (e) judicial and official acts are presumed regularly performed; (g) evidence which could be produced but is withheld would be unfavourable to the withholder (adverse inference).
  14. S.120 (IEA 114A): in rape prosecutions where sexual intercourse is proved and the victim states in evidence that she did not consent, the Court SHALL presume absence of consent.
  15. Definitions: 'may presume' = Court may or may not treat the fact as proved unless disproved; 'shall presume' = Court must treat as proved unless disproved; 'conclusive proof' = Court must treat as proved and no evidence to disprove is allowed.
  16. Estoppel (IEA 115-117; BSA Chapter on estoppel): S.115 - estoppel by representation (intentional causing of belief; neither the representor nor his representative can later deny); S.116 - tenant cannot deny landlord's title during the tenancy; licensee cannot deny licensor's authority; S.117 - acceptor of a bill cannot deny drawer's authority; bailee/licensee cannot deny bailor's title. Estoppel is a rule of evidence, not a cause of action, and does not operate against a statute.

⚖Landmark cases

Woolmington v. DPP

1935

Golden thread: the prosecution must prove guilt beyond reasonable doubt; the accused's burden is only to raise a reasonable doubt.

Shambhu Nath Mehra v. State of Ajmer

1956

S.106 does not shift the burden of the prosecution; it applies only to facts specially within the accused's knowledge.

Sharad Birdhichand Sarda v. State of Maharashtra

1984

Five golden principles for conviction on circumstantial evidence (panchsheel); S.106 cannot fill gaps in the prosecution case; also on dying declaration.

Trimukh Maroti Kirkan v. State of Maharashtra

2006

In offences in the privacy of a home, the accused who is the only other person present must offer an explanation (S.106).

Dahyabhai Chhaganbhai Thakker v. State of Gujarat

1964

Burden on the accused under S.105 is by preponderance of probability.

Kans Raj v. State of Punjab

2000

Interpretation of 'soon before her death' in S.304B/113B - a proximate and live link is needed.

Satbir Singh v. State of Haryana

2021

Three-judge bench: the presumption under S.113B and S.113A; 'soon before' is flexible and not a fixed period.

Dukhtar Jahan v. Mohd. Farooq

1987

S.112 presumption is conclusive; rebuttal only by proof of non-access.

Goutam Kundu v. State of West Bengal

1993

Blood test cannot be ordered as a routine; strong prima facie case of non-access is needed.

Nandlal Wasudeo Badwaik v. Lata Nandlal Badwaik

2014

A reliable DNA test result prevails over the conclusive presumption of legitimacy (S.112) when the husband succeeds in proving non-access/paternity facts.

Aparna Ajinkya Firodia v. Ajinkya Arun Firodia

2023

DNA tests of a child cannot be ordered casually; Court must weigh the child's best interests and privacy.

Motilal Padampat Sugar Mills v. State of U.P.

1979

Promissory estoppel is an equitable doctrine distinct from S.115 estoppel; it is enforceable against the Government.

✎Memory aids

  • 'Soon before' = proximity, not a stopwatch.
  • 7-7-7: seven years (suicide presumption), seven years (not heard of), seven years (marriage period for dowry death).

!Exam traps

  • TrapS.113A = 'may presume'; S.113B = 'shall presume'; S.114A = 'shall presume'. Examiners swap these words.
  • TrapPresumption of legitimacy is conclusive, but access/non-access is the only rebuttal.
  • TrapS.106 (special knowledge) never relieves the prosecution of proving the foundation facts.
  • TrapSeven-year presumption of death does not tell the date of death.
  • TrapBurden under general exceptions is on the accused but the standard is only a balance of probabilities.
  • TrapAccomplice: S.133 IEA says conviction on accomplice testimony is not illegal, S.114(b) says the Court may presume he is unworthy of credit - so corroboration is a rule of prudence (Bhiva Doulu Patil).

?Likely questions

  3. How long after dissolution is a child still presumed legitimate? (280 days)
  4. Special-knowledge rule - section and effect.
  5. Difference between 'may presume', 'shall presume', 'conclusive proof'.
