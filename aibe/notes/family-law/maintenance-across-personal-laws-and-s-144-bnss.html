Maintenance Across Laws - HAMA, S.144 BNSS (S.125 CrPC), Muslim Women Acts, Senior Citizens Act

iOverview

Maintenance is a statutory, welfare-oriented right with several parallel routes. Learn who can claim under each, the disqualifications, and the landmark cases (Shah Bano, Danial Latifi, Abdul Samad, Rajnesh v Neha).

★Must know

  1. S.144 BNSS (S.125 CrPC; secular): a Magistrate of the first class may order monthly allowance to (a) wife (including a divorced wife who has not remarried), (b) legitimate or illegitimate minor child, (c) adult child unable to maintain self due to physical or mental abnormality or injury, (d) father or mother, where the person with sufficient means neglects or refuses. There is no upper monetary cap (the Rs 500 cap was removed in 2001). Interim maintenance and costs are allowed; allowance runs from the order or, if so ordered, the date of application.
  2. Disqualification of wife (S.144(4) BNSS; S.125(4) CrPC): living in adultery, refusing without sufficient reason to live with husband, or living separately by mutual consent. The wife's own income does not bar the claim unless sufficient (Chaturbhuj v Sita Bai, 2008).
  3. S.144 BNSS is personal-law neutral: Mohd Ahmed Khan v Shah Bano Begum (1985) held that a divorced Muslim wife can claim under it; Parliament responded with the Muslim Women (Protection of Rights on Divorce) Act 1986; Danial Latifi v Union of India (2001) upheld the 1986 Act but read 'reasonable and fair provision' to include future maintenance beyond the iddat; Shabana Bano v Imran Khan (2010) held S.125 CrPC available after iddat; Mohd Abdul Samad v State of Telangana (10 July 2024) held that a divorced Muslim woman can claim under S.125 CrPC (S.144 BNSS) irrespective of the 1986 Act.
  4. HAMA 1956: S.18 - Hindu wife is entitled to maintenance during her lifetime and to live separately without losing it where the husband is guilty of desertion, cruelty, has another wife living, keeps a concubine in the same house or habitually resides with one elsewhere, has converted, or other justifying cause. No claim if she is unchaste or ceases to be a Hindu. S.19: widowed daughter-in-law may claim from the father-in-law if she cannot maintain herself and has no means (obligation is not enforceable if the father-in-law has no ancestral property out of which to maintain). S.20: a Hindu must maintain his legitimate or illegitimate children (until minor) and aged or infirm parents, and unmarried daughters unable to maintain themselves. S.21 dependants; S.22 dependants of deceased; S.23 amount discretionary.
  5. HMA S.24 (interim) and S.25 (permanent alimony) are matrimonial reliefs available to either spouse, even in a void marriage (X v Y, 2025 INSC 197: 'illegitimate wife' and 'faithful mistress' labels are misogynistic; relief under S.25 is discretionary and case specific; interim maintenance is possible when the matrimonial court prima facie finds a marriage void or voidable).
  6. Void marriage and S.125: older view that a woman in a void (bigamous) marriage cannot claim S.125 (Yamunabai Anantrao Adhav, 1988) has been liberalised (Chanmuniya v Virendra Kumar Singh Kushwaha, 2010 - wife includes a woman in a long-standing live-in relationship; Badshah v Urmila Badshah Godse, 2013).
  7. Rajnesh v Neha (2021): maintenance affidavit of disclosure; same-claim overlap across forums to be disclosed and adjusted; payment enforceable like a decree.
  8. Domestic Violence Act S.20 (monetary relief), S.19 (residence) and S.17 (right to reside in shared household) run alongside and in addition to other remedies (S.26).
  9. Maintenance and Welfare of Parents and Senior Citizens Act 2007: parents (including adoptive and step-parents) and senior citizens (60+) may claim from children or relatives who will inherit; claim before the Maintenance Tribunal (Sub-Divisional Officer), appeal to the Appellate Tribunal (District Magistrate); Tribunal should decide within 90 days; S.9(2) caps the monthly allowance at Rs 10,000 (a 2019 amendment Bill to remove the cap lapsed - verify); S.23: a transfer of property by a senior citizen made on condition of care can be declared void if the transferee fails to maintain (Sudesh Chhikara v Ramti Devi, 2022; Urmila Dixit v Sunil Sharan Dixit, 2025); S.24 abandonment is punishable with up to 3 months and/or fine up to Rs 5,000.
  10. Muslim women's rights on divorce (1986 Act): S.3 mahr, reasonable and fair provision and maintenance during iddat, children's maintenance for 2 years; S.4 maintenance from relatives or State Waqf Board where she cannot maintain herself; S.5 option to be governed by S.125-128 CrPC.

⚖Landmark cases

Mohd Ahmed Khan v Shah Bano Begum

1985

S.125 CrPC applies to Muslim wives; mahr is not a substitute for maintenance.

Danial Latifi v Union of India

2001

1986 Act valid; 'reasonable and fair provision' covers the woman's entire future.

Mohd Abdul Samad v State of Telangana

2024

A divorced Muslim woman can invoke S.125 CrPC (S.144 BNSS) irrespective of the 1986 Act; secular remedy.

Rajnesh v Neha

2021

Guidelines on affidavit of disclosure, interim maintenance, quantum.

X v Y (2025 INSC 197)

2025

S.25 HMA maintenance available to a spouse in a void marriage; discretionary.

Chanmuniya v Virendra Kumar Singh Kushwaha

2010

'Wife' in S.125 given a broad meaning; live-in couples can be treated as married for maintenance; larger bench reference.

Chaturbhuj v Sita Bai

2008

Wife with insufficient independent income can claim S.125.

✎Memory aids

  • Shah Bano - Statute response (1986) - Danial saves it - Samad (2024) settles it.

!Exam traps

  • TrapS.125 (S.144 BNSS) has no upper monetary cap.
  • TrapA divorced wife who has remarried loses S.125 rights; one who has not remarried retains them.
  • TrapA wife living in adultery or living separately by mutual consent cannot claim.
  • TrapShah Bano (1985) was legislatively overridden by the 1986 Act, but Danial Latifi read it down and Abdul Samad (2024) confirmed S.125 for all.
  • TrapHAMA S.19 (daughter-in-law) is against the father-in-law, not the husband's relatives generally.
