iOverview

The mechanics of filing a PIL and the categories the Supreme Court listed for letter-petitions: both the included and the excluded categories are frequently asked.

★Must know

  1. Filing: a PIL may be filed by any person or organisation (including a petitioner-in-person) before the Supreme Court under Art 32 or before a High Court under Art 226; a letter or postcard addressed to the Chief Justice or a judge may be treated as a writ petition (epistolary). The petitioner discloses his identity, credentials and the public interest involved, and (under many High Court PIL Rules) files an affidavit stating that he has no personal interest and has made adequate inquiry; the court may require the petitioner to deposit costs or security. The petition names the State/authority and 'proper parties'; notice is issued; the court may seek reports from commissions or committees; relief may be interim.
  2. Supreme Court's guidelines for PIL (1988, as revised): the Court will ordinarily entertain letter-petitions/PIL in respect of: (1) bonded labour matters; (2) neglected children; (3) non-payment of minimum wages to workers and exploitation of casual workers, and complaints of violation of labour laws (except in individual cases); (4) petitions from jails complaining of harassment, for premature release, release after 14 years, death in jail, transfer, release on personal bond, and personal liberty issues of prisoners; (5) petitions seeking speedy trial as a fundamental right; (6) petitions complaining of police inaction in regard to criminal offences; (7) petitions against police for refusing to register a case, harassment by police and death in police custody; (8) petitions against atrocities on women, in particular harassment of bride, bride burning, rape, murder, kidnapping; (9) petitions complaining of harassment or torture of villagers by co-villagers or police from persons belonging to Scheduled Castes and Scheduled Tribes and economically backward classes; (10) petitions pertaining to environmental pollution, disturbance of ecological balance, drugs, food adulteration, maintenance of heritage and culture, antiques, forests and wildlife and other matters of public importance; (11) petitions from riot-victims; (12) family pension. Verify · exact list in the Supreme Court Handbook.
  3. Categories NOT ordinarily entertained as PIL (letter-petitions): (1) landlord-tenant matters; (2) service matters and those pertaining to pension and gratuity; (3) complaints against Central/State Government departments and local bodies, except those relating to matters listed above; (4) admission to medical and engineering colleges; (5) petitions for early hearing of matters pending in High Courts and subordinate courts. Dr. Duryodhan Sahu v. Jitendra Kumar Mishra (1998): PIL cannot be entertained in service matters, especially by strangers; the remedy is before the Administrative Tribunal; Rajiv Ranjan Singh 'Lalan' v. Union of India (2006) reaffirmed that service matters are excluded; Ashok Kumar Pandey v. State of West Bengal (2004).
  4. Judicial reluctance in specific areas: criminal prosecutions - a stranger cannot normally intervene (Janata Dal v. H.S. Chowdhary, 1992; Simranjit Singh Mann v. Union of India, 1992 - mercy petitions); tax and contract disputes; policy matters (court will not substitute its opinion; e.g., Narmada Bachao Andolan v. Union of India, 2000 - Sardar Sarovar dam); tender and commercial decisions (Raunaq International Ltd. v. I.V.R. Construction Ltd., 1999; Jagdish Mandal v. State of Orissa, 2007 - judicial review is of decision-making process; Tata Cellular v. Union of India, 1994); religious and cultural matters - locus standi examined stricter (2026 observations in the Sabarimala reference); scientific and technical issues - experts to be consulted (We are not scientists, 2026).
  5. Costs: the court may dismiss a frivolous or vexatious PIL with exemplary costs; costs may be imposed on the petitioner or the advocate; courts have jailed or fined petitioners for false PIL (e.g., Holicow Pictures Pvt. Ltd. v. Prem Chandra Mishra, 2007 - costs for PIL used as a blackmailing device; Dattaraj Nathuji Thaware v. State of Maharashtra, 2005 - 'PIL is not a pill or a panacea for all wrongs'; Kushum Lata v. Union of India, 2006). In 2026 the Supreme Court dismissed 25 PILs filed by a single advocate-petitioner in one stroke (10 April 2026) telling him not to rush to court (Sachin Gupta v. Union of India, WP(C) 444/2026) Verify.
  6. Interim and final relief: interim injunctions; interim compensation; directions to the State to implement laws; appointing committees; monitoring; closing the case when the grievance is redressed; 'Public law compensation' under Arts 32/226 for violation of Art 21 is distinct from tort damages (Nilabati Behera, 1993).
  7. PIL and other proceedings: PIL cannot be used for private disputes or to enforce contractual rights; a person who has a personal remedy (e.g., consumer forum, civil suit) is generally relegated; a pending PIL on the same issue ordinarily bars repetitive PILs; res judicata applies in PIL in a limited manner (Forward Construction Co. v. Prabhat Mandal, 1986 - constructive res judicata applies in public interest matters; also State of Karnataka v. All India Manufacturers Organisation, 2006 - Bangalore-Mysore Infrastructure; PIL of public-spirited persons not barred for want of specific injury).
  8. Public interest litigation in High Courts: Art 226 jurisdiction; many High Courts (Allahabad, Delhi, Bombay, Calcutta, Madras, Gujarat, Patna, Orissa, Punjab and Haryana etc.) have PIL Rules; Division Bench hears PIL; reporting by amicus.

§Key provisions

ProvisionWhat it says
Supreme Court PIL guidelines (1988)List of 12 categories ordinarily entertained (bonded labour, neglected children, prisoners, police, women, SC/ST atrocities, environment...).
Excluded categoriesLandlord-tenant; service/pension/gratuity; complaints against Government/local bodies (except listed); admissions to colleges; early-hearing requests.
Art 226High Court PIL under Rules; affidavits and costs.

⚖Landmark cases

Dr. Duryodhan Sahu v. Jitendra Kumar Mishra

1998

PIL not maintainable in service matters; Administrative Tribunals have jurisdiction.

Janata Dal v. H.S. Chowdhary

1992

Strangers cannot interfere in criminal prosecutions; locus standi limited to bona fide PIL.

Raunaq International Ltd. v. I.V.R. Construction Ltd.

1999

Courts must be cautious in tender-related PIL; costs may be imposed.

Narmada Bachao Andolan v. Union of India

2000

Court reluctant to interfere in policy decisions in PIL; rehabilitation to be monitored.

Holicow Pictures Pvt. Ltd. v. Prem Chandra Mishra

2007

PIL must be genuine; exemplary costs for misuse.

Dattaraj Nathuji Thaware v. State of Maharashtra

2005

PIL is not a panacea; courts must filter abuse.

✎Memory aids

  • Not PIL: 'L-S-G-A-E' - Landlord-tenant, Service/pension, Government complaints, Admissions, Early hearing.

!Exam traps

  • TrapBonded labour, neglected children, jail-harassment, police inaction, women atrocities and environmental pollution are entertained; service matters and landlord-tenant are not.
  • TrapPIL by a stranger in a criminal case is not ordinarily allowed.
  • TrapCourts impose costs for abuse; PIL is not a 'matter of right'.

?Likely questions

  1. Which of the following would NOT be entertained as PIL? (A service matter / landlord-tenant)
  2. Under which Article can a PIL be filed before a High Court? (Art 226)
  3. Which case held that PIL is not maintainable in service matters? (Dr. Duryodhan Sahu v. Jitendra Kumar Mishra)
