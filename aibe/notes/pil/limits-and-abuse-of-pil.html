iOverview

Courts have tightened PIL: guidelines for filtering petitions, costs for abuse and, in 2026, a push to restructure decades-old continuing mandamus cases. Learn the guidelines and the latest rulings.

★Must know

  1. State of Uttaranchal v. Balwant Singh Chaufal (2010, Supreme Court): comprehensive guidelines to check abuse of PIL: (1) the courts must encourage genuine and bona fide PIL and effectively discourage and curb the PIL filed for extraneous considerations; (2) each High Court should prepare its own Rules for encouraging genuine PIL and discouraging PIL filed with oblique motives; (3) the court should be prima facie satisfied regarding the correctness of the contents of the petition; (4) the court must be satisfied about the credentials of the petitioner; (5) the court should be satisfied that the petition involves a larger public interest, gravity and urgency of the matter; (6) the court should ensure that the petition does not involve personal gain, private motive or oblique motive; (7) the court should not entertain PIL that has publicity interest or political interest; (8) petitioners filing frivolous PIL should be burdened with exemplary costs; (9) the courts should take care of the public interest in matters such as service, family and contractual disputes. The Court also directed that certain matters (such as those under the 1988 guidelines) be examined carefully; Dr. B. Singh v. Union of India (2004) - 'PIL is a weapon which has to be used with great care and circumspection'; Ashok Kumar Pandey v. State of W.B. (2004) - judges must be careful to see that the public interest is not a cloak for private gain; Guruvayur Devaswom Managing Committee v. C.K. Rajan (2003) - guidelines on PIL (a PIL involving public interest and not mere busybody); Kushum Lata v. Union of India (2006) - costs; State of Rajasthan v. Union of India (1977) - 'busybodies'.
  2. Doctrinal limits: (a) separation of powers - the court should not legislate or perform executive functions but can fill gaps in the law and enforce fundamental rights (Supreme Court Bar Association v. Union of India, 1998; Divisional Manager, Aravali Golf Club v. Chander Hass, 2008 - judges must not overstep; Asif Hameed v. State of J&;K, 1989); (b) technical and scientific issues - rely on experts; (c) political questions - courts do not decide purely political questions; (d) policy matters - review limited; (e) alternative remedies; (f) personal-liberty PIL (habeas corpus) is relaxed; (g) criminal matters - strangers are not allowed to interfere with prosecution (Janata Dal v. H.S. Chowdhary, 1992), but a PIL to compel investigation of a serious crime is allowed in rare cases (Vineet Narain; Sakiri Vasu).
  3. Abuse categories (the 'four P's' - 2026 remarks): in May 2026 a Constitution Bench (nine-judge Sabarimala reference) comprising CJI Surya Kant and Justices B.V. Nagarathna, M.M. Sundresh, Ahsanuddin Amanullah, Aravind Kumar, Augustine George Masih, Prasanna B. Varale, R. Mahadevan and Joymalya Bagchi remarked that PIL has become 'Private, Publicity, Paisa and Political Interest Litigation' and that only real and genuine PILs would be entertained; the Court also questioned how a newspaper article gives a cause of action [reported 5 May 2026; VERIFY]. In April 2026 the Court in the same reference emphasised stricter locus standi norms in faith cases, noting that the 2006 Sabarimala PIL by a stranger would have been dismissed under today's standard Verify. On 10 April 2026 the CJI Surya Kant Bench dismissed 25 PILs filed by the same advocate in one go (Sachin Gupta v. Union of India, WP(C) 444/2026).
  4. Docket restructuring of continuing-mandamus PILs: on 13 April 2026 the Bench of CJI Surya Kant and Justice Bagchi restructured the forest conservation litigation In re: T.N. Godavarman Thirumulpad (instituted 1995); in March 2026 (M.C. Mehta v. Union of India, WP(C) 13381/1984, decided 11 March 2026) and in the Taj Trapezium case (2026 INSC 381) the Court closed decades-old M.C. Mehta petitions and split the pending issues into fresh suo motu proceedings and High Court proceedings under Art 226; the Court said it was rationalising its docket and regulating the use of continuing mandamus Verify · citations.
  5. Environmental and animal-welfare PIL (2025-26): Aravalli Hills - the Supreme Court in November 2025 accepted a Ministry committee's definition (a landform of 100 metres or more local relief) in the matter; after criticism it took suo motu cognisance (In re: Definition of Aravalli Hills and Ranges and Ancillary Issues), stayed its own ruling on 29 December 2025 and constituted an independent expert committee; in 2026 it said it would not allow anybody to touch the Aravalli range for now and that the new committee must have broader public participation (25 May 2026); stray dogs - suo motu 'In re: City Hounded by Strays, Kids Pay the Price' (July-August 2025 orders; final judgment on 19 May 2026 held that the Animal Birth Control Rules 2023 confer no unqualified right on stray dogs to inhabit particular premises while the State must manage stray dogs scientifically through sterilisation, vaccination and sheltering) Verify; the Court also said 'We are not scientists' in declining a PIL on urban growth around airports after the Air India AI171 crash Verify.
  6. Role of the High Court and amicus: High Courts increasingly use committees; some have special PIL benches; amici ensure assistance; costs for frivolous cases; and notice to the Advocate-General.
  7. Reform proposals: statutory regulation; Law Commission 177th/ 192nd reports; specialised PIL benches; mandatory pre-filing disclosure of previous petitions; deposits; limit on 'professional petitioners'; use of legal aid authorities to vet PIL; digital filing with credentials verification.

✎Memory aids

  • Balwant Singh Chaufal: 'B-C-C-C' - Bona fide, Credentials, Costs, Concrete public interest.

!Exam traps

  • TrapCosts are the standard penalty for frivolous PIL.
  • TrapCourts will not substitute their opinion in policy matters but will enforce fundamental rights.
  • TrapA PIL cannot be used to seek political mileage or publicity.
