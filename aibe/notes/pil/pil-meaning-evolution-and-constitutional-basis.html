iOverview

Where PIL came from, why courts allowed strangers to sue, and the innovative remedies (epistolary jurisdiction, commissions, continuing mandamus) that define Indian public law.

★Must know

  1. Meaning: litigation for the protection of the public interest or the general interest of a community, as distinct from a private dispute; initiated by a public-spirited person or by the court itself on behalf of persons who cannot approach the court because of poverty, ignorance, disability or social disadvantage. It is not defined in any statute; it is a judicial innovation under Art 32 (Supreme Court) and Art 226 (High Courts). Also called 'social action litigation' (Upendra Baxi) and 'epistolary jurisdiction' when begun by a letter.
  2. Evolution: until the late 1970s Indian courts followed the traditional rule that only a 'person aggrieved' (one whose own legal right had been infringed) could invoke the court. After the Emergency (1975-77), judges such as Justice V.R. Krishna Iyer and Justice P.N. Bhagwati (and later Justice Kuldip Singh, Justice J.S. Verma and Justice Chinnappa Reddy) relaxed the rule of locus standi to make justice accessible to the poor. Early signals: Mumbai Kamgar Sabha v. Abdulbhai (1976); Municipal Council, Ratlam v. Vardhichand (1980) (a community of residents compelled the municipality to provide drains; s.133 CrPC); the first reported PIL: Hussainara Khatoon v. Home Secretary, State of Bihar (1979) - brought on the basis of a newspaper report by advocate Kapila Hingorani about undertrial prisoners in Bihar jails, leading to recognition of the right to speedy trial and free legal aid under Art 21.
  3. Constitutional basis: Art 32 (enforcement of Fundamental Rights; the Supreme Court can issue 'directions, orders or writs'), Art 226 (High Courts, for enforcement of Fundamental Rights and 'for any other purpose'), Art 142 (complete justice), Art 21 (expanded to include dignity, livelihood, health, environment), Art 39A (legal aid), Art 51A(g) (environment). Art 12 'State' is widely read so that PIL can reach authorities and sometimes private bodies performing public functions. Court uses Art 14 to scrutinise arbitrary State action. DPSPs (Arts 38, 39, 41, 42, 43, 47, 48A) are read into Art 21.
  4. Locus standi - traditional v. liberalised: traditional - the petitioner must show the infringement of his own legal right; liberalised - 'any member of the public having sufficient interest' can maintain an action for judicial redress for public injury arising from breach of a public duty or violation of a constitutional or legal provision, provided he acts bona fide and not for personal gain or private profit or political motive (S.P. Gupta v. Union of India, 1981, the Judges' Transfer case - Justice Bhagwati). Representative standing: for the poor and disabled (Bandhua Mukti Morcha v. Union of India, 1984; Peoples' Union for Democratic Rights v. Union of India, 1982); citizen standing: for matters of public concern (e.g., environment; public property); organisational standing: NGOs, bar associations and trade unions (Fertilizer Corporation Kamgar Union v. Union of India, 1981 - a union may challenge sale of plant; Akhil Bharatiya Soshit Karamchari Sangh v. Union of India, 1981).
  5. Types of PIL: (1) representative PIL on behalf of the disadvantaged; (2) citizen/public-spirited PIL (environment, corruption, governance); (3) epistolary jurisdiction - letters or postcards treated as writ petitions (Sunil Batra v. Delhi Administration, 1978/1980; Bandhua Mukti Morcha, 1984); (4) suo motu cognizance - on the basis of newspaper reports, letters or events (e.g., the Aravalli Hills and stray dogs matters, 2025-26); (5) PIL against the State, public authorities, and even private persons where public duty is involved; (6) interest-group PILs (consumer, labour, women, children); (7) amicus curiae-assisted PILs. PIL in the Supreme Court is dealt with by the PIL Cell (letters to the Chief Justice) and the Registry's circulars.
  6. Judicial techniques developed in PIL: (a) relaxation of procedure (letter as petition; no formal pleadings; no costs); (b) appointment of commissions of inquiry/ socio-legal commissions to collect facts (Bandhua Mukti Morcha; Sheela Barse); (c) amicus curiae (e.g., M.C. Mehta cases; Parmanand Katara); (d) expert and monitoring committees (Central Empowered Committee - forests; Monitoring Committee - Delhi sealing; EPCA - Delhi air); (e) continuing mandamus - the court retains jurisdiction and issues periodic directions (Vineet Narain v. Union of India, 1998; Vishaka v. State of Rajasthan, 1997; T.N. Godavarman Thirumulpad v. Union of India, 1996-; M.C. Mehta); (f) interim directions and 'public law compensation' (Rudul Sah v. State of Bihar, 1983; Nilabati Behera, 1993; M.C. Mehta v. Union of India - Oleum Gas, 1987); (g) filling legislative vacuum with guidelines under Art 141 until Parliament acts (Vishaka; Lakshmi v. Union of India (acid sale); Prakash Singh v. Union of India (police reforms, 2006)); (h) reading international conventions into Art 21 (Vishaka); (i) cost orders and exemplary costs on frivolous PIL; (j) liberal interpretation of 'State' (Ajay Hasia, 1981).
  7. Arguments for and against: PIL promotes access to justice, accountability, rule of law and protection of the vulnerable; critics point to judicial overreach, separation of powers, lack of expertise (the Supreme Court itself in 2026 said 'We are not scientists' while rejecting a PIL on urban growth around airports), docket burden, and misuse for publicity, private or political motives. Courts have laid down self-restraint principles (see 9.P4).
  8. Role of Bar and legal aid: advocates and law students may file PIL as petitioners-in-person; legal aid authorities assist; Advocates may be appointed as amici; PIL petitions need a verified petition and, in many High Courts, an affidavit disclosing credentials (Balwant Singh Chaufal, 2010).

§Key provisions

ProvisionWhat it says
Art 32Right to move the Supreme Court for enforcement of Fundamental Rights - 'heart and soul' (Ambedkar).
Art 226High Court writ jurisdiction for Fundamental Rights and 'any other purpose' - broader than Art 32.
Art 39AEqual justice and free legal aid.
Art 142Supreme Court's power to do complete justice - used in PIL directions.

⚖Landmark cases

Hussainara Khatoon v. Home Secretary, State of Bihar

1979

First reported PIL; speedy trial and free legal aid are part of Art 21; release of undertrials.

S.P. Gupta v. Union of India (Judges' Transfer case)

1981

Liberalised locus standi: any public-spirited person can seek redress for public injury.

People's Union for Democratic Rights v. Union of India (Asiad Workers)

1982

PIL maintainable; non-payment of minimum wages is forced labour (Art 23).

Bandhua Mukti Morcha v. Union of India

1984

Epistolary jurisdiction; bonded labour; commissions; Art 21.

Fertilizer Corporation Kamgar Union v. Union of India

1981

Standing of a union/workers to challenge State action; expanded locus.

Municipal Council, Ratlam v. Vardhichand

1980

Community can compel the municipality to discharge its statutory duty; public nuisance and Art 21.

Sunil Batra v. Delhi Administration

1980

Letter from a prisoner treated as a writ petition; prison reforms.

Vineet Narain v. Union of India

1998

Continuing mandamus; CBI independence; guidelines for investigation of high officials.

Vishaka v. State of Rajasthan

1997

Guidelines against sexual harassment at workplace; international conventions read into Art 21.

✎Memory aids

  • PIL tools: 'L-C-A-C-M' - Letter petition, Commission, Amicus, Continuing mandamus, Monitoring committee.

!Exam traps

  • TrapPIL is a judicial creation; there is no statute on PIL.
  • TrapHussainara Khatoon (1979) is the first reported PIL; S.P. Gupta (1981) is the case on locus standi.
  • TrapPIL can be filed under Art 32 and Art 226 but not under Art 136 as such.
  • TrapA High Court can issue directions in PIL 'for any other purpose' (Art 226), unlike Art 32 (enforcement of FRs).
