Amendment of the Constitution (Art 368) and the Basic Structure Doctrine

iOverview

A perennial AIBE favourite. Know: the three methods of amendment, the evolution Shankari Prasad -> Golak Nath -> Kesavananda -> Minerva Mills -> Waman Rao -> I.R. Coelho, and what counts as basic structure. Also know the important Amendment Acts and their numbers.

★Must know

  1. Art 368 (as amended by the 24th Amendment, 1971): Parliament's constituent power; amendment by introducing a Bill in either House; no prior sanction; President MUST give assent (24th Amendment); no joint sitting for deadlock.
  2. Method 1 - SIMPLE MAJORITY (outside Art 368): admission/establishment of new States (Arts 2-4), Legislative Councils (Art 169), citizenship, Fifth and Sixth Schedules, Art 239A/240, languages, quorum, salaries of MPs, etc.
  3. Method 2 - SPECIAL MAJORITY: majority of total membership of each House + two-thirds of members present and voting. Applies to all fundamental rights, DPSPs and all provisions not under Methods 1 and 3.
  4. Method 3 - SPECIAL MAJORITY + RATIFICATION BY AT LEAST HALF OF THE STATE LEGISLATURES: election of President (Arts 54, 55), extent of executive power of Union/States (Arts 73, 162), Supreme Court and High Courts (Chapters IV of Part V and V of Part VI), Union Territories' High Courts (Art 241), legislative relations (Ch I Part XI), Seventh Schedule, representation of States in Parliament, Art 368 itself, GST Council (Art 279A) provisions of the 101st Amendment.
  5. Basic structure doctrine: Parliament can amend any part of the Constitution but cannot destroy or alter its basic structure (Kesavananda Bharati, 1973, 13 judges, 7:6).
  6. Features recognised as basic: supremacy of the Constitution; rule of law; separation of powers; judicial review (Arts 32, 226, 227); independence of judiciary; federalism; secularism; sovereign democratic republic; parliamentary democracy; free and fair elections; unity and integrity of the nation; equality (Art 14 core); harmony and balance between FR and DPSP; limited amending power of Parliament; Art 32 access to Supreme Court; welfare state; essence of FR.
  7. Not held to be basic structure (illustrative): right to property; Art 356 power is subject to review, not itself 'basic'; ordinary legislation cannot be struck merely for violating basic structure (Indira Nehru Gandhi v. Raj Narain, 1975; State of Karnataka v. Union of India, 1977 - basic structure tests constitutional amendments, not ordinary laws).
  8. Ninth Schedule (inserted by the 1st Amendment, 1951 via Art 31B): laws placed here were immune from judicial review. After I.R. Coelho (2007), laws placed after 24 April 1973 are open to review on the touchstone of basic structure and Part III.
  9. Art 31C (25th Amendment, 1971): laws giving effect to Art 39(b)/(c) immune from challenge under Arts 14, 19, 31. Kesavananda upheld the first part and struck the 'declaration is final' clause. 42nd Amendment extended it to ALL DPSPs - struck down in Minerva Mills (1980). Property Owners Association v. State of Maharashtra (Nov 2024, 9 judges) confirmed that Art 31C as upheld in Kesavananda survives.

§Key provisions

ProvisionWhat it says
Art 368(1)Constituent power of Parliament to amend by way of addition, variation or repeal (inserted by 24th Amendment).
Art 368(2)Special majority + ratification where required; President shall give assent.
Art 368(3)Nothing in Art 13 shall apply to any amendment under this Article (24th Amendment).
Art 368(4) and (5)Inserted by the 42nd Amendment (no amendment can be questioned in court; unlimited amending power); struck down in Minerva Mills (1980).
Art 13(2) and 13(4)13(2): no law shall take away or abridge FR; 13(4) added by the 24th Amendment: Art 13 does not apply to Art 368 amendments.
Important amendments (quick list)1st (1951) Ninth Schedule, Art 15(4), 19(2) additions; 7th (1956) reorganisation of States; 24th (1971) amending power; 25th (1971) Art 31C; 42nd (1976) 'mini-Constitution': Preamble, FDs (Art 51A), DPSP changes, Art 368(4)-(5); 44th (1978) property removed from Part III, Art 300A, Emergency safeguards; 52nd (1985) Tenth Schedule; 61st (1988) voting age 18; 69th (1991) Delhi NCT; 73rd and 74th (1992) local governments; 86th (2002) Art 21A; 91st (2003) 15% ministers cap, anti-defection tightening; 97th (2011) cooperatives; 99th (2014) NJAC (struck down 2015); 101st (2016) GST; 102nd (2018) NCBC constitutional status; 103rd (2019) EWS reservation; 104th (2020) SC/ST reservation in legislatures extended, Anglo-Indian seats ended; 105th (2021) States' power to identify SEBCs restored; 106th (2023) women's reservation (Nari Shakti Vandan Adhiniyam).

⚖Landmark cases

Shankari Prasad Singh Deo v. Union of India

1951

Parliament can amend Fundamental Rights; 'law' in Art 13 does not include constitutional amendments.

Sajjan Singh v. State of Rajasthan

1965

Reaffirmed Shankari Prasad (3:2); Hidayatullah and Mudholkar JJ. doubted if basic features could be amended.

I.C. Golak Nath v. State of Punjab

1967

11 judges, 6:5. Parliament cannot amend Part III; constitutional amendment is 'law' under Art 13(2). Applied prospective overruling.

Kesavananda Bharati v. State of Kerala

1973

13 judges, 7:6. Overruled Golak Nath. Parliament can amend any provision but not the 'basic structure'. Longest hearing (68 days).

Indira Nehru Gandhi v. Raj Narain

1975

Art 329A(4) (inserted by the 39th Amendment) struck down - free and fair elections, rule of law and judicial review are basic features.

Minerva Mills Ltd. v. Union of India

1980

Struck down Sec 4 and 55 of the 42nd Amendment (Art 31C extension and Art 368(4)-(5)). Harmony between FR and DPSP is basic structure; 'limited amending power is itself a basic feature'.

Waman Rao v. Union of India

1981

Cut-off 24 April 1973: amendments to Ninth Schedule before that date are valid; after that open to review.

S.P. Sampath Kumar v. Union of India

1987

Tribunal as substitute for HC upheld (later modified by L. Chandra Kumar).

L. Chandra Kumar v. Union of India

1997

Power of judicial review of HC and SC (Arts 226/227/32) is basic structure; tribunals' decisions subject to HC scrutiny.

I.R. Coelho v. State of Tamil Nadu

2007

9 judges. Ninth Schedule laws are not beyond judicial review if they damage basic structure or Arts 14, 19, 21 core.

Supreme Court Advocates-on-Record Association v. Union of India (Fourth Judges case)

2015

99th Amendment and NJAC Act struck down (4:1); collegium system restored; independence of judiciary is basic structure.

Janhit Abhiyan v. Union of India

2022

103rd Amendment (10% EWS reservation, Arts 15(6), 16(6)) upheld 3:2. Economic criterion alone can be a ground; 50% ceiling applies to existing reservations only.

Property Owners Association v. State of Maharashtra

2024

9 judges: 'material resources of the community' in Art 39(b) does not automatically include all privately owned resources; Art 31C as upheld in Kesavananda remains good law.

✎Memory aids

  • Chronology: 'S-S-G-K-M' = Shankari (1951), Sajjan (1965), Golak Nath (1967), Kesavananda (1973), Minerva (1980).
  • Special majority = 'Majority of the total + 2/3 of present and voting'. Remember the word TOTAL.

!Exam traps

  • TrapNumber of judges in Kesavananda = 13; majority 7:6 (a razor-thin margin).
  • TrapGolak Nath = 11 judges (6:5).
  • TrapBasic structure is not found in the text - judge-made. Art 368 has no express mention.
  • TrapThe Preamble can be amended (done once, by the 42nd Amendment) but not so as to alter the basic structure.

?Likely questions

  1. In which case did the Supreme Court first lay down the doctrine of basic structure?
  2. Which amendment inserted Art 368(4) and (5), and what happened to it in Minerva Mills?
  3. Ninth Schedule laws placed after which date are open to judicial review?
  4. Which of the following requires ratification by half of the States?
