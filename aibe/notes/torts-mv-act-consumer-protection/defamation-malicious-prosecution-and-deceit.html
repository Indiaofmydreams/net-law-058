Defamation, Malicious Prosecution, Deceit and Other Torts

iOverview

Libel vs slander, defences (truth, fair comment, privilege), malicious prosecution essentials, conspiracy, passing off, privacy.

★Must know

  1. DEFAMATION: publication of a false statement which tends to lower the plaintiff in the estimation of right-thinking members of society, or cause him to be shunned or avoided, or exposes him to hatred, contempt or ridicule. ESSENTIALS: (1) the statement must be defamatory; (2) it must refer to the plaintiff; (3) it must be PUBLISHED to a third person (publication to the plaintiff alone is not publication - Mahendra Ram v Harnandan Prasad, 1958: letter in Urdu). LIBEL: defamation in permanent form (writing, print, pictures, effigies) - actionable per se and also a crime. SLANDER: defamation in transient form (spoken words, gestures) - actionable only on proof of special damage, except four classes: imputation of a crime punishable with imprisonment, loathsome disease, unchastity of a woman (in England by statute), and imputation affecting the plaintiff's office, profession, trade or business. In India, the distinction between libel and slander is not maintained for the criminal law (Sec 499 IPC - now BNS Sec 356 - covers both); in civil law the English distinction is applied by many courts, though several High Courts have treated slander as actionable per se (Indian courts have not definitely settled the issue).
  2. CRIMINAL DEFAMATION: Sec 499/500 IPC (now BNS Sec 356); upheld as constitutional in Subramanian Swamy v Union of India (2016) (reasonable restriction on Art 19(1)(a) to protect reputation under Art 21). Sec 499 exceptions: truth for the public good, public servant's conduct, conduct of any person touching public question, publication of reports of courts, merits of a case decided, merits of public performance, censure by person having authority, accusation in good faith to authorised person, imputation made in good faith for protection of one's own interests, caution conveyed in good faith.
  3. DEFENCES IN DEFAMATION: (1) JUSTIFICATION/ TRUTH (civil: truth is a complete defence; criminal: truth must also be for the public good); (2) FAIR COMMENT on a matter of public interest - based on facts truly stated, honest opinion without malice; (3) PRIVILEGE: absolute privilege (statements in Parliament/ legislature, judicial proceedings (judges, witnesses, counsel), official communications, communications between spouses (Evidence Act Sec 122)) and qualified privilege (statements made in discharge of a duty or protecting an interest, reports of proceedings (newspapers), statements to police; defeated by malice); (4) CONSENT; (5) APOLOGY (mitigation). R. Rajagopal v State of Tamil Nadu (1994): public officials' actions; no prior restraint; publication of matters of public record is permitted; privacy and Art 21.
  4. MALICIOUS PROSECUTION: ESSENTIALS (Mohamed Amin v Jogendra Kumar Bannerjee, 1947 PC): (1) the defendant prosecuted the plaintiff (set the law in motion) - criminal proceedings; (2) the prosecution ended in the plaintiff's FAVOUR (termination); (3) it was without REASONABLE AND PROBABLE CAUSE; (4) it was actuated by MALICE (an improper motive); (5) damage to reputation, person/ liberty, or property (costs). Contrast: abuse of process (misuse of legal process for a collateral object); malicious civil proceedings (needs special damage); FALSE IMPRISONMENT vs MALICIOUS PROSECUTION: in false imprisonment the detention is without lawful authority; in malicious prosecution the process is regular but maliciously invoked.
  5. OTHER TORTS: DECEIT (fraudulent misrepresentation causing damage - Derry v Peek, 1889); CONSPIRACY (agreement to injure; Quinn v Leathem); INDUCING BREACH OF CONTRACT (Lumley v Gye, 1853); INTIMIDATION; INJURIOUS FALSEHOOD/ trade libel; PASSING OFF (misrepresentation causing damage to goodwill - see the Intellectual Property notes); BREACH OF STATUTORY DUTY; INTERFERENCE with privacy - K.S. Puttaswamy v Union of India (2017): right to privacy is a fundamental right under Art 21; common law invasion of privacy tort developing (R. Rajagopal); NEGLIGENT MISSTATEMENT; OCCUPIERS' LIABILITY; nervous shock; wrongful birth. Interference with employment: the 'tort of interference with contractual relations' - Lumley v Gye; Indian recognition by High Courts (e.g., in competition/ trade contexts).
  6. INTERNET AND AI-ERA TORTS (hot): personality rights and deepfakes - the Delhi and Bombay High Courts have repeatedly granted injunctions to celebrities against AI-generated deepfakes and misuse of voice/ likeness (2026 orders protecting e.g. Swami Ramdev and Sonakshi Sinha); defamation on social media - intermediaries' safe-harbour under Sec 79 IT Act subject to the IT Rules; the tort of passing off for personality (Titan Industries v Ramkumar Jewellers, 2012; Anil Kapoor v Simply Life, 2023). These topics are expected to feature in any 'recent developments' question.

!Exam traps

  • TrapPublication to the plaintiff alone is NOT publication.
  • TrapTruth is a complete defence in civil defamation but in criminal defamation must be for the public good.
  • TrapLibel actionable per se; slander needs special damage (except special categories).
  • TrapMalicious prosecution needs termination in plaintiff's favour.

?Likely questions

  1. Essentials of malicious prosecution: favourable termination, absence of reasonable cause, malice.
  2. Criminal defamation upheld in: Subramanian Swamy.
  3. Which is a defence to defamation? Truth, fair comment, privilege.
