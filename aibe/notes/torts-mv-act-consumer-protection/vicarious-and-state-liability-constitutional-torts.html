Vicarious Liability and Liability of the State; Constitutional Torts

iOverview

Master-servant, course of employment, sovereign immunity vs State liability and compensation under Arts 32/226.

★Must know

  1. VICARIOUS LIABILITY: liability of a person for the torts of another. Basis: respondeat superior; qui facit per alium facit per se. Categories: master and servant (employer liable for torts of an employee committed in the COURSE OF EMPLOYMENT); principal and agent; partners (Partnership Act Sec 18, 25-26); company for acts of its officers; owner for acts of independent contractors in limited cases (non-delegable duties, hazardous operations); parent/ guardian - not automatically.
  2. Tests of employment: control test (master controls the manner of doing the work), integration/ organisation test, multiple factor test; independent contractors - employer generally not liable (exceptions: hazardous works; Rylands; statutory duty; negligent selection; non-delegable duty).
  3. COURSE OF EMPLOYMENT: an act is within the course if it is (a) a wrongful act authorised by the master, or (b) a wrongful and unauthorised mode of doing some act authorised by the master (Limpus v London General Omnibus Co, 1862: bus driver racing against another bus in violation of instructions; Century Insurance v Northern Ireland Road Transport Board, 1942: driver smoking while unloading petrol). The master is NOT liable if the act is wholly outside employment (a 'frolic of his own' - Joel v Morison). Lord Phillips: 'close connection' test in Lister v Hesley Hall (2001) for intentional wrongs. Indian courts apply the same principles to motor accidents: an owner is vicariously liable for a driver's negligence in the course of employment.
  4. LIABILITY OF THE STATE: Art 300(1): the Government of India may sue or be sued by the name of the Union of India, subject to any law enacted; the liability is the same as that of the Dominion/ Province before the Constitution. Historical: P. & O. Steam Navigation Co v Secretary of State for India (1861, Calcutta SC): State not liable for tortious acts done in exercise of sovereign powers but liable for acts of commercial/ non-sovereign nature (maintenance of a dockyard). Kasturi Lal Ralia Ram Jain v State of UP (1965): police officers seized gold and a constable misappropriated it; the State was held NOT liable as the power to seize was a sovereign power; the Court urged legislation. State of Rajasthan v Vidyawati (1962): State liable for the negligence of a driver of a Government jeep. The distinction between sovereign and non-sovereign functions has been progressively diluted: State of Gujarat v Memon Mahomed Haji Hasam (1967); Smt. Basava Kom Dyamogouda Patil v State of Mysore (1977); N. Nagendra Rao & Co v State of Andhra Pradesh (1994): State liable for loss caused by negligence of officials in storing goods seized - the sovereign immunity doctrine is outdated and applies only to inalienable sovereign functions such as defence, war, and legislative functions; Saheli v Commissioner of Police, Delhi (1990): State liable to pay compensation for death caused by police; custodial death. Immunity under the Constitution: the President and Governors (Art 361) personally.
  5. CONSTITUTIONAL TORT / PUBLIC LAW COMPENSATION: the Supreme Court and High Courts award monetary compensation under Arts 32 and 226 for violation of fundamental rights, particularly Art 21, as public law remedy (distinct from private tort damages): Rudul Sah v State of Bihar (1983): compensation for illegal detention for 14 years after acquittal; Bhim Singh v State of J&K (1985): MLA unlawfully detained; Sebastian M. Hongray v Union of India (1984): exemplary costs for disappearance of persons; Nilabati Behera v State of Orissa (1993): custodial death - compensation as public law remedy, sovereign immunity not a defence; D.K. Basu v State of West Bengal (1997): guidelines on arrest and detention; custodial violence; the State is liable to pay compensation. The remedy is without prejudice to civil/ criminal action. Also: compensation for wrongful acts by police in encounters (PUCL v State of Maharashtra, 2014 guidelines); and for medical negligence in government hospitals (Paschim Banga Khet Mazdoor Samity v State of West Bengal, 1996 - right to emergency medical treatment, Art 21).
  6. Government contracts and torts: Sec 80 CPC: notice of 2 months before suing the Government (exception: urgent relief with leave, Sec 80(2)); Limitation: as in the general law. Public authorities' liability for statutory duties: ordinary law of tort (negligence, nuisance, breach of statutory duty). Tort of misfeasance in public office.

!Exam traps

  • TrapKasturi Lal accepted sovereign immunity; Nilabati Behera/ Rudul Sah: public law compensation not barred by sovereign immunity.
  • TrapA master is not liable for an independent contractor's torts (subject to exceptions) or a servant's 'frolic'.
  • TrapPublic law compensation under Art 32/226 is distinct from private law damages.

?Likely questions

  1. Kasturi Lal Ralia Ram Jain v State of UP deals with: sovereign immunity.
  2. Compensation for violation of Art 21 under Art 32: Rudul Sah.
  3. State liability is provided in: Art 300.
