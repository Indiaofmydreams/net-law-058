General Defences in Tort

iOverview

Volenti, necessity, inevitable accident, act of God, private defence, statutory authority and contributory negligence.

★Must know

  1. VOLENTI NON FIT INJURIA: a person who voluntarily consents to run a risk of harm cannot complain of it. Requirements: consent must be free and with full knowledge of the nature and extent of the risk (Smith v Baker, 1891: mere knowledge is not consent); the act must be one the consent covers; consent cannot be to an illegal act. Limits: rescue cases - a rescuer is not volenti (Haynes v Harwood, 1935; Wagner v International Railway); sports - participants consent to normal risks (Hall v Brooklands Auto Racing Club, 1933: spectators accept risk of cars leaving the track; Woolridge v Sumner, 1963); passenger with a drunk driver (Dann v Hamilton; later cases - contributory negligence rather than volenti); statutory duty and employer's liability: workman does not consent to employer's breach (Smith v Baker). Consent to surgery: Sec 88, 92 IPC (now BNS Secs 26, 30 - acts done in good faith for benefit).
  2. INEVITABLE ACCIDENT: an event which could not have been avoided by the exercise of ordinary care, caution and skill (Stanley v Powell, 1891: shooting accident). ACT OF GOD: operation of natural forces without human intervention which no reasonable foresight could provide against (Nichols v Marsland, 1876: extraordinary rainfall; Rylands exception). In India courts require the event to be extraordinary: cyclones and floods may or may not qualify depending on foreseeability. PRIVATE DEFENCE: reasonable force to defend person or property (Secs 96-106 IPC; now BNS Secs 34-44); force must be proportionate. NECESSITY: act done to prevent greater harm to the community or to the actor - Cope v Sharpe (1912: burning heather to stop fire); Leigh v Gladstone (forcible feeding of a hunger-striker); public necessity in Sec 81 IPC (BNS Sec 22).
  3. STATUTORY AUTHORITY: an act authorised by statute is not actionable if done without negligence and within the statute (Vaughan v Taff Vale Railway, 1860: sparks from locomotive - no liability as statute authorised the engine and due care was used; Hammersmith Railway v Brand). Absolute statutory authority (when the nuisance is the inevitable result) is a complete defence; conditional authority requires reasonable care. Judicial acts: judges, magistrates have immunity under the Judicial Officers' Protection Act 1850; executive officers' acts; parental and quasi-parental authority (reasonable chastisement); Sec 79 IPC mistake of fact.
  4. PLAINTIFF'S DEFAULT/ CONTRIBUTORY NEGLIGENCE: at common law contributory negligence was a complete defence (Butterfield v Forrester, 1809: plaintiff riding fast into an obstruction). The rule was softened by the last opportunity rule (Davies v Mann, 1842: donkey fettered on the highway; defendant had the last opportunity to avoid). In India courts apportion damages (analogous to the English Law Reform (Contributory Negligence) Act 1945): damages are reduced in proportion to the plaintiff's share of responsibility; apportionment is routine in motor accident claims (the Motor Accident Claims Tribunals reduce compensation by the percentage of contributory negligence; for example 'non-use of helmet or seat belt' - SC cases). Children: lower standard of care; a young child cannot be contributorily negligent. Composite negligence: when two or more wrongdoers cause the same damage, each is liable for the whole; the plaintiff can sue any or all (joint and several liability); contribution among tortfeasors; Khenyei v New India Assurance (2015) clarifies the distinction between contributory and composite negligence in motor claims.
  5. Other defences: LIMITATION (Limitation Act 1963; one year for defamation, false imprisonment and malicious prosecution (Arts 73-76 - verify), three years residuary Art 113); PLAINTIFF'S ILLEGALITY (ex turpi causa non oritur actio); MISTAKE (not generally a defence in tort except in defamation and trespass in limited cases); LEAVE AND LICENCE.

!Exam traps

  • TrapVolenti is complete defence; contributory negligence reduces damages (apportionment).
  • TrapRescuers are not volenti.
  • TrapKnowledge of the risk alone does not equal consent (scienti non fit injuria).

?Likely questions

  1. Haynes v Harwood relates to: rescue and volenti.
  2. Which defence reduces damages rather than barring the claim? Contributory negligence.
  3. Act of God: Nichols v Marsland.
