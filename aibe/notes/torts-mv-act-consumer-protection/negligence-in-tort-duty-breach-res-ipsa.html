iOverview

The most heavily tested tort. Duty of care, standard of care, breach, causation, remoteness, res ipsa loquitur, medical negligence.

★Must know

  1. Negligence = breach of a legal duty to take care, resulting in damage to the plaintiff (Alderson B in Blyth v Birmingham Waterworks: omission to do something which a reasonable man would do, or doing something which a prudent and reasonable man would not do). As a TORT, negligence needs: (1) a LEGAL DUTY of care owed by the defendant to the plaintiff; (2) BREACH of that duty; (3) DAMAGE caused by the breach (causation) and not too remote.
  2. DUTY OF CARE: Donoghue v Stevenson (1932, HL): the 'neighbour principle' (Lord Atkin) - you must take reasonable care to avoid acts or omissions which you can reasonably foresee would be likely to injure your neighbour, i.e., persons so closely and directly affected by your act that you ought reasonably to have them in contemplation. A snail in a ginger beer bottle; manufacturer's duty to ultimate consumer. Caparo Industries v Dickman (1990): three-stage test - foreseeability of damage, proximity of relationship, and that it is fair, just and reasonable to impose a duty. Indian courts follow both; Municipal Corporation of Delhi v Subhagwanti (1966): liability of the corporation for the collapse of a clock tower; res ipsa.
  3. STANDARD OF CARE: the reasonable man (Vaughan v Menlove, 1837 - the objective standard; 'reasonable prudent man'); skilled professionals: standard of the ordinary skilled person exercising that special skill - BOLAM TEST (Bolam v Friern Hospital Management Committee, 1957): a doctor is not negligent if he acted in accordance with a practice accepted as proper by a responsible body of medical opinion. Indian Supreme Court adopts Bolam in Jacob Mathew v State of Punjab (2005) (criminal negligence needs gross negligence/ recklessness - 'rash or negligent act' under Sec 304A IPC, now BNS Sec 106) and Poonam Verma v Ashwin Patel (1996). Informed consent - Samira Kohli v Prabha Manchanda (2008) (consent must be informed; patient must be told of material risks; consent for diagnostic procedure does not cover therapeutic). Children - lower standard; old/ infirm - no lower objective standard.
  4. BREACH: considered with the magnitude of risk, probability of harm, gravity of harm, utility of the act and cost of precautions (Bolton v Stone, 1951: cricket ball hitting a passer-by - risk too remote; Paris v Stepney Borough Council, 1951: greater care to a one-eyed workman; Latimer v AEC, 1953: cost of precautions).
  5. RES IPSA LOQUITUR ('the thing speaks for itself'): where the thing causing the damage is under the management of the defendant, and the accident is such as in the ordinary course of things does not happen if those who have the management use proper care, the accident itself affords reasonable evidence of negligence in the absence of explanation by the defendant. Byrne v Boadle (1863: flour barrel falling from a warehouse window); Scott v London & St Katharine Docks (1865: sugar bags). The maxim shifts the evidentiary burden (not the legal burden) to the defendant. In India it applies in surgery (instrument left inside the body), in aviation, rail and road accidents (Shyam Sunder v State of Rajasthan, 1974: the plaintiff need not prove how the negligence occurred). In medical negligence - V. Kishan Rao v Nikhil Super Speciality Hospital (2010): res ipsa applies in appropriate cases; no need for an expert evidence in every case.
  6. CAUSATION: 'but for' test (Barnett v Chelsea & Kensington Hospital, 1969: arsenic poisoning - hospital's negligence did not cause death as the patient would have died anyway). Novus actus interveniens (new intervening cause) breaks the chain - but a rescuer's or victim's reasonable response doesn't (Scott v Shepherd, 1773 - the squib case). REMOTENESS: Re Polemis (1921) - directness test (all direct consequences even if unforeseeable), now displaced by The Wagon Mound (No 1) (1961, PC): the defendant is liable only for damage of a kind that is reasonably foreseeable (oil spilt on water caught fire due to welding - fire damage not foreseeable). EGGSHELL SKULL RULE: take your victim as you find him (Smith v Leech Brain, 1962; Dulieu v White) - the extent of damage need not be foreseeable if the kind is.
  7. SPECIAL AREAS: (1) Nervous shock/ psychiatric harm (Hambrook v Stokes; Alcock v Chief Constable of South Yorkshire - close tie of love and affection, proximity in time and space, direct perception); (2) Negligent misstatement (Hedley Byrne v Heller, 1964 - duty where a special relationship exists and reliance); (3) Pure economic loss; (4) Occupier's liability (invitees, licensees, trespassers; Indian cases apply the common duty of care); (5) Employer's duty - safe system, competent staff, safe premises, adequate plant; (6) Product liability (Donoghue; now CPA 2019 Chapter VI); (7) Professional negligence - doctors, lawyers (Bar of Indian Lawyers v D.K. Gandhi, 2024 - the Court held that advocates' services are outside the CPA 2019 but the decision has been stayed and referred to a larger Bench; verify current status), engineers, architects, banks.
  8. MEDICAL NEGLIGENCE IN INDIA: civil action in tort or under the Consumer Protection Act (Indian Medical Association v V.P. Shantha, 1995 - medical services are 'service' (free services excluded; paid services and contracts of service excluded under personal service)); criminal liability requires gross negligence (Jacob Mathew); doctors are not liable merely because a procedure fails or a more skilful approach existed; liability if (a) lacks the skill professed, or (b) fails to exercise reasonable care. Hospitals vicariously liable for nurses and doctors; Spring Meadows Hospital v Harjol Ahluwalia (1998): the child as patient and parents as consumers; Nizam's Institute of Medical Sciences v Prasanth S. Dhananka (2009); Martin F. D'Souza v Mohd. Ishfaq (2009): directions that complaints against doctors be examined by a competent doctor's opinion before notice - later refined in V. Kishan Rao (2010) which held that the direction was not mandatory in all cases.
  9. CONTRIBUTORY NEGLIGENCE AND COMPOSITE NEGLIGENCE: see 1.2. Negligence in public authorities: State vicarious liability (1.5).

✎Memory aids

  • Negligence = D-B-D: Duty, Breach, Damage.

!Exam traps

  • TrapRes ipsa loquitur shifts the evidential burden; it does not reverse the legal burden.
  • TrapWagon Mound (No 1) replaced Re Polemis: foreseeability, not directness.
  • TrapBolam: accepted practice of a responsible body of professionals.
  • TrapCriminal negligence of doctors requires GROSS negligence (Jacob Mathew).

?Likely questions

  1. Neighbour principle: Donoghue v Stevenson.
  2. Remoteness of damage - The Wagon Mound.
  3. Res ipsa loquitur: meaning and effect.
  4. Test of professional negligence: Bolam.
