Strict and Absolute Liability; Environmental and Hazardous Torts

iOverview

Rylands v Fletcher, M.C. Mehta (Oleum gas) and the Indian doctrine of absolute liability are near-certain topics.

★Must know

  1. RYLANDS v FLETCHER (1868, HL): a person who for his own purposes brings on his land and collects and keeps there anything likely to do mischief if it escapes must keep it at his peril, and, if he does not do so, is prima facie answerable for all the damage which is the natural consequence of its escape (Blackburn J). The House of Lords added 'non-natural use' of land. Facts: water from a reservoir flooded the plaintiff's mine through disused shafts.
  2. ESSENTIALS: (1) dangerous thing likely to do mischief if it escapes; (2) ESCAPE from the defendant's land to a place outside his occupation or control (Read v Lyons, 1947: no escape - no liability); (3) NON-NATURAL USE of land (Rickards v Lothian, 1913: ordinary domestic water supply is natural); (4) damage. Mens rea or negligence is unnecessary.
  3. EXCEPTIONS (defences): (1) plaintiff's consent or common benefit; (2) act of God (Nichols v Marsland); (3) act of a stranger (Box v Jubb); (4) statutory authority; (5) default of the plaintiff; (6) in India some courts treat natural use differently, but the main change comes with absolute liability.
  4. ABSOLUTE LIABILITY - M.C. MEHTA v UNION OF INDIA (1987) 1 SCC 395 (Oleum Gas Leak case): after oleum gas leaked from Shriram Foods and Fertiliser Industries in Delhi, the Supreme Court (Bhagwati CJ) held that the Rylands rule, born in the 19th century England, cannot be applied to modern industrial India as is. It laid down the rule of ABSOLUTE LIABILITY: an enterprise engaged in a hazardous or inherently dangerous activity which poses a potential threat to the health and safety of persons working in the factory and residing in the surrounding area owes an absolute and non-delegable duty to the community to ensure that no harm results to anyone; if harm results, the enterprise is strictly and absolutely liable, WITHOUT the exceptions of Rylands v Fletcher (no defence of act of God or act of a stranger) and without the requirement of 'escape' or 'non-natural use'. The measure of compensation must be correlated to the magnitude and capacity of the enterprise (the 'deep pocket' principle) so that it has a deterrent effect.
  5. Other leading cases: Union Carbide Corporation v Union of India (1989): the Bhopal Gas disaster (1984) was settled for USD 470 million (about Rs 715 crore); the Supreme Court upheld the settlement (1991 review); curative petition seeking additional compensation of about Rs 7,844 crore was dismissed in March 2023. Bhopal Gas Leak Disaster (Processing of Claims) Act 1985 appointed the Central Government as parens patriae. Indian Council for Enviro-Legal Action v Union of India (1996): 'polluter pays' and absolute liability for hazardous industry; the burden to prove no harm lies on the industry. Vellore Citizens' Welfare Forum v Union of India (1996): precautionary principle and polluter pays are part of Indian environmental law (Art 21 and Art 48A, 51A(g)). Charan Lal Sahu v Union of India (1990): the state as parens patriae. State of Punjab v Modern Cultivators (1964): Rylands applied to the bursting of a canal.
  6. STATUTORY BACKDROP: Public Liability Insurance Act 1991 - no-fault liability for hazardous substances accidents; owner must take insurance and contribute to the Environmental Relief Fund; National Green Tribunal Act 2010, Sec 17 - NGT can award compensation on the principle of strict liability/ 'no fault' for environmental damage (Sec 17(3) - no fault liability for death/ injury/ damage in accidents involving hazardous substances); Environment (Protection) Act 1986. The NGT Act replaced the National Environment Tribunal Act 1995.
  7. Contrast: Strict liability (Rylands): exceptions available; escape required; non-natural use required. Absolute liability (M.C. Mehta): no exceptions; hazardous industry; no escape requirement; compensation proportional to enterprise capacity. Both are civil liabilities not requiring proof of negligence or intention.

⚖Landmark cases

Rylands v Fletcher

1868

Strict liability for escape of dangerous things; non-natural use.

M.C. Mehta v Union of India

1987

Absolute liability for hazardous industry; no exceptions; deep-pocket principle.

Indian Council for Enviro-Legal Action v UoI

1996

Polluter pays; absolute liability; burden on industry.

Vellore Citizens' Welfare Forum v UoI

1996

Precautionary principle and polluter pays form part of Indian law.

Union Carbide Corporation v Union of India

1989

Bhopal Gas settlement.

Read v Lyons

1947

No escape, no liability under Rylands.

✎Memory aids

  • Rylands = E-N-D: Escape, Non-natural use, Danger.

!Exam traps

  • TrapAbsolute liability has NO exceptions (act of God and stranger not available); strict liability has exceptions.
  • TrapAbsolute liability does not require 'escape'; the Mehta rule is limited to HAZARDOUS or inherently dangerous industries.
  • TrapRylands requires NON-NATURAL USE of land.
