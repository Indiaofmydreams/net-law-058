Nature, Definition and Essentials of Tort; Tort vs Contract and Crime

High yieldRead the full chapter →

Cases in one line

  • Ashby v White 1703 Injuria sine damno: violation of a legal right is actionable even without loss.
  • Gloucester Grammar School Case 1410 Damnum sine injuria: loss through lawful competition not actionable.
  • Bradford Corporation v Pickles 1895 A lawful act is not rendered unlawful by a bad motive.
  • Donoghue v Stevenson 1932 Tort duty independent of contract; neighbour principle.

Exam traps

  • TrapInjuria sine damno = right violated, no loss, ACTIONABLE; damnum sine injuria = loss, no right violated, NOT actionable.
  • TrapTort remedy is UNLIQUIDATED damages; contract remedy may be liquidated.
  • TrapActio personalis moritur cum persona has statutory exceptions in India (Sec 306 Succession Act).
2

General Defences in Tort

High yieldRead the full chapter →

Cases in one line

  • Smith v Baker 1891 Knowledge of risk is not consent; volenti needs voluntary agreement.
  • Haynes v Harwood 1935 Rescuer not volenti.
  • Hall v Brooklands Auto-Racing Club 1933 Spectators accept the inherent risks of a motor race.
  • Nichols v Marsland 1876 Act of God as a defence to strict liability.
  • Vaughan v Taff Vale Railway 1860 Statutory authority defence.
  • Butterfield v Forrester (1809); Davies v Mann 1842 Contributory negligence and the last opportunity rule.
  • Khenyei v New India Assurance 2015 Composite vs contributory negligence.

Exam traps

  • TrapVolenti is complete defence; contributory negligence reduces damages (apportionment).
  • TrapRescuers are not volenti.
  • TrapKnowledge of the risk alone does not equal consent (scienti non fit injuria).
3

Negligence

Very high yieldRead the full chapter →

Cases in one line

  • Donoghue v Stevenson 1932 Neighbour principle; manufacturer's duty to ultimate consumer.
  • Caparo Industries v Dickman 1990 Three-stage test: foreseeability, proximity, fair-just-reasonable.
  • Bolam v Friern Hospital Management Committee 1957 Professional standard of care.
  • Jacob Mathew v State of Punjab 2005 Criminal negligence of doctors requires gross negligence.
  • Samira Kohli v Prabha Manchanda 2008 Informed consent in medical treatment.
  • The Wagon Mound (No 1) 1961 Remoteness: reasonable foreseeability.
  • Byrne v Boadle 1863 Res ipsa loquitur.
  • Barnett v Chelsea & Kensington Hospital 1969 But-for causation.
  • Hedley Byrne v Heller 1964 Negligent misstatement and special relationship.
  • Indian Medical Association v V.P. Shantha 1995 Medical services under consumer law.

Memory aids

  • Negligence = D-B-D: Duty, Breach, Damage.

Exam traps

  • TrapRes ipsa loquitur shifts the evidential burden; it does not reverse the legal burden.
  • TrapWagon Mound (No 1) replaced Re Polemis: foreseeability, not directness.
  • TrapBolam: accepted practice of a responsible body of professionals.
  • TrapCriminal negligence of doctors requires GROSS negligence (Jacob Mathew).
4

Strict and Absolute Liability; Environmental and Hazardous Torts

Very high yieldRead the full chapter →

Cases in one line

  • Rylands v Fletcher 1868 Strict liability for escape of dangerous things; non-natural use.
  • M.C. Mehta v Union of India 1987 Absolute liability for hazardous industry; no exceptions; deep-pocket principle.
  • Indian Council for Enviro-Legal Action v UoI 1996 Polluter pays; absolute liability; burden on industry.
  • Vellore Citizens' Welfare Forum v UoI 1996 Precautionary principle and polluter pays form part of Indian law.
  • Union Carbide Corporation v Union of India 1989 Bhopal Gas settlement.
  • Read v Lyons 1947 No escape, no liability under Rylands.

Memory aids

  • Rylands = E-N-D: Escape, Non-natural use, Danger.

Exam traps

  • TrapAbsolute liability has NO exceptions (act of God and stranger not available); strict liability has exceptions.
  • TrapAbsolute liability does not require 'escape'; the Mehta rule is limited to HAZARDOUS or inherently dangerous industries.
  • TrapRylands requires NON-NATURAL USE of land.
5

Vicarious Liability and Liability of the State; Constitutional Torts

High yieldRead the full chapter →

Cases in one line

  • Limpus v London General Omnibus 1862 Prohibited mode of doing authorised act is within the course of employment.
  • State of Rajasthan v Vidyawati 1962 State liable for negligence of its servant in non-sovereign function (driver of jeep).
  • Kasturi Lal v State of UP 1965 Sovereign immunity - gold seized by police; State not liable.
  • Nilabati Behera v State of Orissa 1993 Public law compensation for custodial death; sovereign immunity no defence.
  • Rudul Sah v State of Bihar 1983 First case of compensation for illegal detention under Art 32.
  • N. Nagendra Rao v State of AP 1994 Sovereign immunity doctrine restricted to true sovereign functions.
  • D.K. Basu v State of West Bengal 1997 Custodial violence; guidelines; compensation.

Exam traps

  • TrapKasturi Lal accepted sovereign immunity; Nilabati Behera/ Rudul Sah: public law compensation not barred by sovereign immunity.
  • TrapA master is not liable for an independent contractor's torts (subject to exceptions) or a servant's 'frolic'.
  • TrapPublic law compensation under Art 32/226 is distinct from private law damages.
6

Nuisance and Trespass

High yieldRead the full chapter →

Cases in one line

  • St Helen's Smelting Co v Tipping 1865 Physical damage nuisance - locality no defence.
  • Sturges v Bridgman 1879 Locality test; coming to the nuisance is no defence.
  • Christie v Davey 1893 Malicious use may turn a lawful act into a nuisance.
  • Municipal Council, Ratlam v Vardichan 1980 Public nuisance; duty of local authority.
  • Bird v Jones 1845 False imprisonment needs total restraint.
  • Entick v Carrington 1765 Government agents' trespass without legal authority.

Exam traps

  • TrapTrespass is actionable per se; private nuisance needs proof of damage.
  • TrapComing to the nuisance is no defence; but prescription (20 years) is.
  • TrapTrespass needs DIRECT interference; if consequential, it is nuisance or action on the case.
7

Defamation, Malicious Prosecution, Deceit and Other Torts

High yieldRead the full chapter →

Cases in one line

  • Subramanian Swamy v Union of India 2016 Criminal defamation constitutionally valid.
  • R. Rajagopal v State of Tamil Nadu 1994 Press freedom and defamation of public officials; privacy.
  • Mohamed Amin v Jogendra Kumar Bannerjee 1947 PC Ingredients of malicious prosecution.
  • Derry v Peek 1889 Deceit - fraud requires knowledge/ recklessness.
  • Lumley v Gye 1853 Inducing breach of contract.
  • K.S. Puttaswamy v Union of India 2017 Right to privacy as a fundamental right.

Exam traps

  • TrapPublication to the plaintiff alone is NOT publication.
  • TrapTruth is a complete defence in civil defamation but in criminal defamation must be for the public good.
  • TrapLibel actionable per se; slander needs special damage (except special categories).
  • TrapMalicious prosecution needs termination in plaintiff's favour.

Remedies, Damages, Joint Tortfeasors and Fatal Accidents

Medium yieldRead the full chapter →

Cases in one line

  • Rookes v Devlin 1964 Three categories of exemplary damages.
  • Rudul Sah v State of Bihar 1983 Exemplary compensation for constitutional tort.

Exam traps

  • TrapExemplary damages: only the Rookes v Devlin categories at common law; Indian courts are more liberal in public law claims.
  • TrapFatal Accidents Act claims are for the dependants' loss; the estate's survival claim is different.

Motor Vehicles Act: Overview, Definitions, Licensing and Registration

Medium-high yieldRead the full chapter →

Exam traps

  • TrapAge: 18 years for driving; 16 for a gearless motor cycle up to 50 cc; commercial/ transport vehicle: 20 years (verify the 2019 text).
  • TrapHelmet: Sec 129; Good Samaritan: Sec 134A; driver's duty after accident: Sec 134 (report within 24 hours).
  • TrapRegistration is compulsory (Sec 39); transfer reporting - 14 days.
10

Motor Vehicles Act: Insurance against Third-Party Risks (Chapter XI, Secs 145-164)

Very high yieldRead the full chapter →

Cases in one line

  • National Insurance Co v Swaran Singh 2004 Pay and recover; insurer must prove willful breach by the owner.
  • Mukund Dewangan v Oriental Insurance 2017 LMV licence covers light transport vehicles up to 7,500 kg GVW.
  • Bajaj Allianz General Insurance v Rambha Devi (Constitution Bench, Nov 2024) - Constitution Bench reaffirmed Mukund Dewangan: LMV licence covers light transport vehicles up to 7,500 kg GVW.
  • United India Insurance v Lehru 2003 Insurer's liability for owner's breach where owner takes reasonable care.

Exam traps

  • TrapThe insurer's defences are limited to Sec 149(2) grounds; mere breach is not enough - the breach must be fundamental and causative.
  • TrapInsurer remains liable to the THIRD PARTY even when entitled to avoid the policy as against the insured (pay and recover).
  • TrapSec 146: insurance is compulsory for all vehicles in a public place except Government vehicles used for non-commercial purposes.
11

Motor Vehicles Act: Liability without Fault, Hit-and-Run, Structured Formula and Interim Relief (Secs 140-144, 161-164B)

Very high yieldRead the full chapter →

Cases in one line

  • Deepal Girishbhai Soni v United India Insurance 2004 Sec 163A is a final no-fault compensation scheme; election between Sec 163A and Sec 166.
  • S. Rajaseekaran v Union of India 2025 orders Directions to implement golden-hour cashless treatment under Sec 162.
  • SaveLife Foundation v Union of India (26 May 2026) - Directions to operationalise PM-RAHAT and emergency care.

Exam traps

  • TrapNo-fault liability (Sec 140): Rs 50,000 (death), Rs 25,000 (permanent disablement) - NOT for simple injuries.
  • TrapHit-and-run: Rs 2 lakh (death), Rs 50,000 (grievous hurt) from 1 April 2022.
  • TrapSec 163A claimants cannot also claim under Sec 166.
  • TrapCashless treatment: Rs 1.5 lakh for seven days (PM-RAHAT).
12

Motor Vehicles Act: Motor Accident Claims Tribunals and Computation of Compensation (Secs 165-176)

Very high yieldRead the full chapter →

Cases in one line

  • Sarla Verma v Delhi Transport Corporation 2009 Standard multiplier table; deduction for personal expenses.
  • National Insurance Co v Pranay Sethi 2017 Future prospects 40/25/10; conventional heads; five-judge Bench.
  • Magma General Insurance v Nanu Ram 2018 Loss of consortium for parents and children.
  • Raj Kumar v Ajay Kumar 2011 Injury claims: functional disability.
  • Bimla Devi v Himachal Road Transport Corporation 2009 Standard of proof in claims: preponderance of probabilities.
  • Khenyei v New India Assurance 2015 Composite negligence in motor accident cases.

Exam traps

  • TrapAppeal against Tribunal's award: High Court within 90 days; insurer/ owner must deposit Rs 25,000 or 50% of the award, whichever is less (Sec 173).
  • TrapCivil Court's jurisdiction is barred by Sec 175.
  • TrapStandard of proof: preponderance of probability.
  • TrapClaims Tribunal can award interest from the date of the claim (Sec 171).
  • TrapPranay Sethi: 40% / 25% / 10% for self-employed; 50% / 30% / 15% for permanent-job holders (reading carefully!).

Consumer Protection Act 2019: Scheme, Definitions and Consumer Rights

Very high yieldRead the full chapter →

Cases in one line

  • Lucknow Development Authority v M.K. Gupta 1994 Statutory authorities providing housing are 'service' providers; compensation for harassment.
  • Laxmi Engineering Works v P.S.G. Industrial Institute 1995 Commercial purpose and self-employment.
  • Indian Medical Association v V.P. Shantha 1995 Medical services are 'service' (not free ones).
  • Spring Meadows Hospital v Harjol Ahluwalia 1998 Parents are consumers where child is patient.

Exam traps

  • TrapFree service and contract of personal service are excluded from 'service'.
  • TrapGoods bought for resale or commercial purpose: not a consumer; self-employment livelihood is not commercial purpose.
  • TrapSix rights are in Sec 2(9) (not eight).
  • TrapUnfair contract (Sec 2(46)) is relevant to State and National Commissions' powers (Secs 49(2), 59(2)).

Central Consumer Protection Authority (CCPA), Misleading Advertisements and Penalties

Very high yieldRead the full chapter →

Exam traps

  • TrapSec 21 penalty: Rs 10 lakh (first), Rs 50 lakh (subsequent); endorser ban 1 year (first) / 3 years (subsequent).
  • TrapSec 89 imprisonment: 2 years (first), 5 years (subsequent).
  • TrapCCPA can recall goods and order refunds (Sec 20) but doesn't adjudicate individual compensation claims (that is for Commissions).
16

Consumer Disputes Redressal Commissions: Composition, Jurisdiction, Procedure and Appeals

Very high yieldRead the full chapter →

Cases in one line

  • Rutu Mihir Panchal v Union of India 2025 INSC 593 Pecuniary jurisdiction based on consideration paid is constitutionally valid.
  • New India Assurance Co v Hilli Multipurpose Cold Storage 2020 45-day outer limit for written version is mandatory.
  • Pioneer Urban Land & Infrastructure v Govindan Raghavan 2019 One-sided terms in builder-buyer agreements are unfair trade practice; consumer not bound by them.
  • Imperia Structures v Anil Patni 2020 Consumer remedies and RERA remedies are concurrent.
  • Bar of Indian Lawyers v D.K. Gandhi PS 2024 Advocates' services held outside the Act; judgment was stayed/ referred to a larger Bench - check status.

Exam traps

  • TrapPecuniary jurisdiction: Rs 50 lakh / Rs 2 crore - NOT the original Rs 1 crore / Rs 10 crore.
  • TrapBasis: value of CONSIDERATION PAID, not the claim.
  • TrapAppeal to the State Commission needs a 50% deposit.
  • TrapComplaint: 2 years; version of the opposite party: 30 + 15 days (strictly 45).
  • TrapSec 72 imprisonment 1 month to 3 years; fine Rs 25,000 to Rs 1 lakh.

Consumer Protection Act: Product Liability, Unfair Contracts and Key Case Law

High yieldRead the full chapter →
