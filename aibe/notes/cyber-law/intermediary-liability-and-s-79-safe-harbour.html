Intermediary Liability, Safe Harbour (S.79), IT Rules 2021 and the 2026 Synthetic Content Amendment

iOverview

S.79 protects intermediaries conditionally; Shreya Singhal read down 'actual knowledge'; the 2021 Rules impose due diligence and grievance duties, and the February 2026 amendment adds labelling and fast takedown for AI-generated (synthetic) content.

★Must know

  1. S.79(1)-(2): an intermediary is not liable for third-party information, data or communication link hosted or made available by it if (a) its function is limited to providing access to a communication system, or (b) it does not initiate the transmission, select the receiver or select or modify the information, and (c) it observes due diligence and other guidelines prescribed by the Central Government.
  2. S.79(3): safe harbour is lost if (a) the intermediary has conspired, abetted, aided or induced the commission of the unlawful act, or (b) upon receiving actual knowledge, or on being notified by the appropriate Government or its agency, that information is being used to commit an unlawful act, it fails to expeditiously remove or disable access without vitiating the evidence. Shreya Singhal (2015) read down S.79(3)(b): 'actual knowledge' means knowledge through a court order or a notification by the appropriate Government (and the unlawful act must relate to Art 19(2)); intermediaries need not judge the legality of each request.
  3. Case law on intermediaries: Avnish Bajaj v State (NCT of Delhi) (2008, Delhi HC) - Bazee.com CEO prosecution for obscene listing led to the 2008 amendments and the reformed S.79; Digumarti (2016) - special law; MySpace Inc v Super Cassettes Industries (Delhi HC DB, 2016) and Christian Louboutin SAS v Nakul Bajaj (Delhi HC, 2018) - an intermediary playing an active role (curating, promoting) can lose the safe harbour; Google India v Visakha Industries (2019) - intermediary liability in defamation context.
  4. Information Technology (Intermediary Guidelines and Digital Media Ethics Code) Rules 2021: due diligence by intermediaries (user agreements; remove unlawful content on court order or government notice within 36 hours (now shortened - see below); retain data 180 days; assist agencies within 72 hours); Significant Social Media Intermediaries (SSMIs: 5 million or more registered users) must appoint a Chief Compliance Officer, a Nodal Contact Person and a Resident Grievance Officer (all residents in India), publish monthly compliance reports, and messaging services must enable identification of the first originator of a message for serious offences (Rule 4(2); under challenge in WhatsApp v Union of India, Delhi HC). Grievance mechanism: acknowledge within 24 hours and resolve within 15 days (now 7 days); Part III covers digital news and OTT content - three-tier self-regulation. Online gaming intermediaries were added by IT Amendment Rules 2023 (self-regulatory bodies).
  5. IT Amendment Rules 2026 on 'synthetically generated information' (SGI) (notified 10 February 2026, in force 20 February 2026): SGI is audio, visual or audio-visual content created or altered by a computer resource so that it appears authentic (deepfakes, voice cloning, AI-altered images; plain AI text is excluded). Lawful SGI must be clearly and prominently labelled; where feasible, intermediaries embed permanent metadata or unique identifiers; labels cannot be removed; the draft's 10% watermark was dropped. Timelines: unlawful content after a court order or government notice - 3 hours (earlier 36); content about an individual - 36 hours (earlier 72); high-risk content such as non-consensual intimate imagery, impersonation or morphed images - 2 hours (earlier 24); general grievances - 7 days (earlier 15). Failure of due diligence can cost safe harbour (reported; verify the final text).
  6. Blocking: S.69A and the Blocking Rules 2009 (written reasons, committee review, hearing to the originator/intermediary where identifiable); emergency blocking; Sahyog portal for notices - X Corp v Union of India (Karnataka HC, September 2025) rejected X's challenge to the Sahyog portal; appeal reported - verify.
  7. Ministry of Electronics and IT (MeitY) is the nodal ministry; the proposed Digital India Act to replace the IT Act has not been enacted (verify). Telecommunications Act 2023 replaces the Indian Telegraph Act 1885 (interception, suspension of services, SIM-fraud offences) - being brought into force in phases (verify).
  8. Internet shutdowns: Anuradha Bhasin v Union of India (2020) - freedom of speech and trade over the internet is protected by Art 19(1)(a) and 19(1)(g); indefinite shutdown impermissible; orders must be published, proportionate and reviewable; the Temporary Suspension of Telecom Services Rules 2017 provide a Review Committee. Faheema Shirin v State of Kerala (Kerala HC, 2019): access to the internet is part of Art 21.

⚖Landmark cases

Shreya Singhal v Union of India

2015

S.79(3)(b) read down - actual knowledge means a court order or government notification.

MySpace Inc v Super Cassettes Industries

2016

Intermediary with actual knowledge of specific infringing content must take it down; S.79 and Copyright Act harmonised.

Christian Louboutin SAS v Nakul Bajaj

2018

Active role (not mere conduit) can forfeit safe harbour.

Anuradha Bhasin v Union of India

2020

Internet access and use for speech and trade protected; indefinite shutdown impermissible.

✎Memory aids

  • 79: Mere conduit + Due diligence = Safe; Aid or Ignore a notice = Sunk.
  • SSMI: 50 lakh users; 3 officers (CCO, Nodal, Grievance).

!Exam traps

  • TrapS.79 safe harbour is conditional, not absolute.
  • TrapAfter Shreya Singhal, 'actual knowledge' = court order or government notification, not a private complaint.
  • TrapSSMI threshold is 5 million registered users.
  • TrapUnder the 2026 amendment, takedown on a court order or government notice is 3 hours.
  • TrapPlain AI-generated text is outside the SGI definition.
