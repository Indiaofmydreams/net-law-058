Information to Police, FIR, Investigation, Remand, Default Bail and Police Report

iOverview

The single most important block. Zero FIR, e-FIR, preliminary enquiry, power to investigate, Magistrate's order for investigation (s.175(3)/(4)), examination of witnesses, confessions, remand (s.187), default bail and the final report (s.193).

★Must know

  1. FIR (s.173(1) BNSS; s.154 CrPC): information relating to the commission of a cognizable offence, given orally or by electronic communication to the officer in charge of a police station, irrespective of the area where the offence was committed ('Zero FIR'); if oral, it is reduced to writing, read over and signed; if by electronic communication, it must be signed within three days to be taken on record; recorded in the prescribed book; informant gets a copy free of cost (s.173(2)). Rape and sexual offences (BNS ss.64-71): information recorded by a woman police officer; if the victim is temporarily or permanently mentally or physically disabled, it is recorded at the victim's residence or a convenient place, in the presence of an interpreter or special educator, with videography.
  2. Preliminary enquiry (s.173(3) BNSS; new): for offences punishable with three years or more but less than seven years, the officer in charge may, with the prior permission of an officer not below the rank of Deputy Superintendent of Police, conduct a preliminary enquiry within fourteen days to ascertain whether a prima facie case exists, or proceed with investigation. For offences punishable with seven years or more, an FIR must be registered.
  3. Lalita Kumari v. Government of U.P. (2014, Constitution Bench): registration of an FIR is mandatory if the information discloses a cognizable offence; no preliminary inquiry in such cases; preliminary inquiry is permitted only to find whether a cognizable offence is disclosed and only in categories such as matrimonial/family disputes, commercial offences, medical negligence, corruption cases, and cases with abnormal delay (more than three months without satisfactory explanation); inquiry to be concluded within a week (now 14 days under BNSS for the 3-7 years category). Failure to register: erring officer is liable to action (BNS s.199 (IPC s.166A)). Zero FIR: Satvinder Kaur v. State (Govt of NCT of Delhi), 1999 - jurisdiction is no ground for refusing registration, but investigation must be by the competent police station.
  4. Refusal to register FIR (s.173(4) BNSS; s.154(3) CrPC): the aggrieved person may send the substance of the information in writing or by post (or electronically) to the Superintendent of Police, who must either investigate or direct investigation; if SP does not act, the informant may apply to the Magistrate under s.175(3) BNSS (s.156(3) CrPC); remedy under Art 226 also available. Delay in lodging FIR: explainable delay is not fatal (State of H.P. v. Gian Chand, 2001); FIR is not substantive evidence; used to corroborate/contradict the maker (Hasib v. State of Bihar, 1972). Second FIR for the same incident is barred (T.T. Antony v. State of Kerala, 2001) but a counter-version FIR is permitted (Upkar Singh v. Ved Prakash, 2004); 'test of sameness' - Amish Devgan v. Union of India (2020).
  5. Information as to non-cognizable cases (s.174 BNSS; s.155 CrPC): enter in the book and refer the informant to the Magistrate; no investigation without the Magistrate's order; if a case involves both, it is treated as cognizable.
  6. Police power to investigate (s.175 BNSS; s.156 CrPC): an officer in charge may investigate any cognizable case which a court having jurisdiction would have power to try; no proceeding of a police officer is invalid on the ground that he lacked power. s.175(3): any Magistrate empowered under s.210 may order investigation, after considering the application supported by an affidavit under s.173(4), after making such inquiry as he thinks necessary and after considering the submission of the police officer (new). s.175(4) (new): where the complaint is against a public servant arising in the course of official duty, the Magistrate may order investigation only after receiving a report containing facts and circumstances from the superior officer and after considering the assertions of the public servant about the situation that led to the incident. The Supreme Court (Jan 2026 and Oct 2026) held that s.175(4) is a procedural adjunct to s.175(3), not a standalone provision, and that it expands safeguards for public servants beyond the old s.156(3) Verify.
  7. Magistrate's s.156(3)/175(3) power: Sakiri Vasu v. State of U.P. (2008) - Magistrate can direct registration and monitor investigation; Priyanka Srivastava v. State of U.P. (2015) - affidavit and prior approach to police/SP are mandatory; Anil Kumar v. M.K. Aiyappa (2013) - sanction under s.197 CrPC is required before the Magistrate orders investigation against a public servant; an April 2026 Supreme Court ruling reportedly held that no prior sanction is needed merely to direct FIR registration under s.156(3)/s.175(3) Verify; State of Bihar v. J.A.C. Saldanha (1980) - courts should not interfere with the statutory investigation; Vinubhai Haribhai Malaviya v. State of Gujarat (2019) - Magistrate can order further investigation up to the stage trial begins/ concludes.
  8. Procedure for investigation (s.176 BNSS; s.157 CrPC): where there is reason to suspect a cognizable offence, the officer proceeds in person to the spot, investigates facts, and takes measures to discover and arrest the offender; may depute a subordinate for trivial matters. s.176(3) BNSS (new): for offences punishable with imprisonment of seven years or more, a forensic expert shall visit the crime scene to collect forensic evidence and the process shall be videographed on a mobile phone or other electronic device; if a State lacks forensic facility, it shall notify a facility in another State.
  9. Power to require attendance of witnesses (s.179 BNSS; s.160 CrPC): a police officer making an investigation may, by written order, require any person acquainted with the facts to attend; a male person under the age of 15 years or above 60 years (CrPC: 65), a woman, or a mentally or physically disabled person shall not be required to attend any place other than the place of residence. Examination of witnesses by police (s.180; s.161 CrPC): oral examination; the person is bound to answer truly all questions except those which tend to expose him to a criminal charge, penalty or forfeiture; statements may be recorded by audio-video means; statements of women victims of offences under BNS ss.64-71 etc. to be recorded by a woman police officer.
  10. Statements to police (s.181 BNSS; s.162 CrPC): not to be signed; not to be used for any purpose at any inquiry or trial except to contradict the witness (Tahsildar Singh v. State of U.P., 1959) - exceptions: dying declarations, statements leading to discovery (BSA s.23(2)), and statements in FIR.
  11. Recording of confessions and statements (s.183 BNSS; s.164 CrPC): a Judicial Magistrate (not a police officer) may record a confession or statement made during investigation, whether or not the Magistrate has jurisdiction; the Magistrate must explain that the person is not bound to make a confession and that it may be used as evidence; he must be satisfied that it is voluntary; no police officer to be present; confessions can be recorded by audio-video means; the person must be given time for reflection if he is produced from police custody; a statement of a person alleging offences under BNS ss.64-71 etc. must be recorded by a Judicial Magistrate, if possible a woman, as soon as the commission of the offence is brought to notice; a disabled victim's statement is to be recorded with an interpreter or special educator and videographed, which then has the value of examination-in-chief and need not be repeated at trial.
  12. Medical examination of rape victim (s.184 BNSS; s.164A CrPC): by a registered medical practitioner employed in a Government hospital (or any registered medical practitioner with the consent of the woman/guardian), within twenty-four hours of receiving information; report forwarded to the investigating officer within seven days, who forwards it to the Magistrate.
  13. Search by police officer (s.185-186). Remand and detention (s.187 BNSS; s.167 CrPC): when investigation cannot be completed within 24 hours (Art 22(2)), the officer forwards the accused to the nearest Magistrate with a copy of the case-diary entries; the Magistrate (even without jurisdiction) may authorise detention for a term not exceeding fifteen days in the whole (police custody or otherwise); beyond fifteen days, only judicial custody; maximum detention: ninety days where the investigation relates to an offence punishable with death, life imprisonment or imprisonment of ten years or more; sixty days for any other offence; on the expiry of that period the accused shall be released on bail if he is prepared to and does furnish bail ('default bail', 'statutory bail', 'indefeasible right'). BNSS allows police custody to be taken in whole or in parts within the first forty days (for 60-day offences) or the first sixty days (for 90-day offences) Verify · the exact formulation and judicial treatment. An Executive Magistrate may authorise detention for seven days at a time where no Judicial Magistrate is available. The accused must be produced physically or via audio-video means.
  14. Default bail jurisprudence: Chaganti Satyanarayana v. State of A.P. (1986) - Art 21; Central Bureau of Investigation v. Anupam J. Kulkarni (1992) - police custody only within first fifteen days; Sanjay Dutt v. State (1994) - indefeasible right; Uday Mohanlal Acharya v. State of Maharashtra (2001) - right must be exercised before the chargesheet is filed; Rakesh Kumar Paul v. State of Assam (2017) - 'not less than ten years' means a minimum of ten years; so the 90-day limit applies only to offences with minimum 10 years or death/life, and 60 days to others; Bikramjit Singh v. State of Punjab (2020) - UAPA extension must be by a Court; M. Ravindran v. Intelligence Officer, DRI (2021) - the right accrues on expiry, even if application is made later; filing of an incomplete chargesheet to defeat default bail is impermissible; Ritu Chhabaria v. Union of India (2023) - investigation not complete merely because the chargesheet is filed incompletely.
  15. Release of accused when evidence deficient (s.189 BNSS; s.169 CrPC) and cases sent to Magistrate when evidence is sufficient (s.190; s.170); diary of proceedings in investigation (s.192; s.172 CrPC) - to be maintained day-to-day; courts may use it to aid in inquiry or trial but not as evidence; accused has no right to call for it (Mukund Lal v. Union of India, 1989; Khatri v. State of Bihar, 1981 - subject to s.172(3) and s.145 Evidence Act).
  16. Police report (s.193 BNSS; s.173 CrPC): on completion of investigation, a report is forwarded to the Magistrate (by electronic means also); investigation of rape (BNS ss.64-71 etc.) to be completed within two months from the date of recording the information (s.193(2)); contents (s.193(3)): names of parties, nature of information, names of persons acquainted with the circumstances, whether any offence appears to have been committed and by whom, whether the accused has been arrested, released on bond or bail, forwarded in custody, and whether the medical examination report of the woman is attached; s.193(3)(ii) (new): the police must inform the victim or informant, within ninety days, of the progress of the investigation by any means including electronic communication; the Magistrate has to take cognizance or may direct further investigation. s.193(9) (new in text, per Vinubhai principle): further investigation after the report is permitted, with the court's permission if the trial has commenced, and should be completed within ninety days (extendable with permission).
  17. Closure report: the Magistrate may accept, reject (and take cognizance or issue process), or direct further investigation; the informant must be given notice and an opportunity to file a protest petition (Bhagwant Singh v. Commissioner of Police, 1985); the Magistrate cannot compel the police to file a charge-sheet but can take cognizance on the material (Abhinandan Jha v. Dinesh Mishra, 1967).
  18. Inquests: s.194 BNSS (s.174 CrPC): police inquest on unnatural deaths - report to the nearest Magistrate; s.196 (s.176 CrPC): Magistrate's inquiry into the cause of death in police custody, disappearance, or rape in custody is mandatory; Magistrate may hold inquest in other cases (exhumation allowed).
  19. Quashing of FIR: State of Haryana v. Bhajan Lal (1992) - seven categories; R.P. Kapur v. State of Punjab (1960); Neeharika Infrastructure Pvt. Ltd. v. State of Maharashtra (2021) - courts to exercise the power sparingly and not to stay investigations in routine manner.
  20. Investigation - miscellany: Custodial interrogation permitted but torture banned (Art 21); statements under pressure are inadmissible (BSA s.22-23); investigation officer's duty to maintain case diary; special investigative agencies (CBI/ NIA) may need State consent under DSPE Act s.6 (State of W.B. v. Committee for Protection of Democratic Rights, 2010 - Art 226 can order CBI inquiry in exceptional cases).

§Key provisions

ProvisionWhat it says
BNSS s.173 (CrPC s.154)FIR - zero FIR, e-FIR (3 days), copy to informant, preliminary enquiry (14 days).
BNSS s.175(3) and (4) (CrPC s.156(3))Magistrate's order for investigation; new safeguards for public servants.
BNSS s.176(3)Mandatory forensic visit for offences punishable with 7 years or more; videography.
BNSS s.183 (CrPC s.164)Confessions and statements before Magistrate.
BNSS s.187 (CrPC s.167)Remand: 15 days police custody max; 60/90 days detention; default bail.
BNSS s.193 (CrPC s.173)Police report; 90-day victim update; 2 months in rape cases.
BNSS s.196 (CrPC s.176)Magistrate's inquiry into death in custody.

⚖Landmark cases

Lalita Kumari v. Government of U.P.

2014

FIR registration mandatory when cognizable offence disclosed; limited preliminary inquiry.

State of Haryana v. Bhajan Lal

1992

Seven categories for quashing FIR/complaint.

T.T. Antony v. State of Kerala

2001

No second FIR for the same cognizable offence; fresh FIR for a different version is permitted subject to sameness.

Sakiri Vasu v. State of U.P.

2008

Magistrate's s.156(3) power includes monitoring the investigation.

Priyanka Srivastava v. State of U.P.

2015

Affidavit and prior recourse to s.154(3) are required before s.156(3).

Tahsildar Singh v. State of U.P.

1959

Meaning of s.162: statements to police cannot be used except to contradict.

Bhagwant Singh v. Commissioner of Police

1985

Informant must be given notice before Magistrate accepts a closure report.

Vinubhai Haribhai Malaviya v. State of Gujarat

2019

Magistrate can order further investigation up to the stage before trial concludes.

Rakesh Kumar Paul v. State of Assam

2017

Default bail - 90 days only for offences with a minimum of 10 years.

Sanjay Dutt v. State

1994

Default bail is an indefeasible right.

Uday Mohanlal Acharya v. State of Maharashtra

2001

Right to default bail is lost if the chargesheet is filed before the right is availed.

CBI v. Anupam J. Kulkarni

1992

Police custody permitted only in the first 15 days of detention.

Hasib v. State of Bihar

1972

FIR is not substantive evidence.

Satvinder Kaur v. State (Delhi)

1999

Police cannot refuse an FIR on territorial grounds; zero FIR.

Mukund Lal v. Union of India

1989

Case diary cannot be used by the accused; court may use it.

✎Memory aids

  • FIR 'E-Z-P': e-FIR signed in 3 days; Zero FIR anywhere; Preliminary enquiry within 14 days (3-7 years).
  • Remand: '15-60-90': 15 days police custody; 60/90 days overall; default bail after.

!Exam traps

  • TrapPreliminary enquiry is available only in the 3-to-7-year category and with DSP-level permission; not for 7+ years.
  • TrapFIR can be lodged at any police station (zero FIR); investigation is done by the territorially competent station.
  • TrapDefault bail right is lost once the chargesheet is filed before the application is made.
  • TrapConfession before Magistrate: no police officer to be present; 24 hours reflection if from police custody.

?Likely questions

  1. Which BNSS section provides for FIR? (s.173)
  2. e-FIR must be signed by the informant within how many days? (3)
  3. Which section requires forensic expert visit for crimes punishable with 7+ years? (s.176(3))
  4. What is the maximum period of police custody? (15 days)
  5. BNSS s.187: the maximum period of detention for offences punishable with 10+ years? (90 days)
