Foreign Courts, Foreign Judgments and Foreign States (Secs 13, 14, 44A, 83-87B)

iOverview

Section 13 grounds and Section 44A (reciprocating territories) are the usual targets.

★Must know

  1. Sec 2(5) FOREIGN COURT: a court situated outside India and not established or continued by the authority of the Central Government; Sec 2(6) FOREIGN JUDGMENT: the judgment of a foreign court. Foreign judgment is enforceable in India either by (a) a suit on the judgment (as a debt) or (b) execution under Sec 44A if it is a decree of a superior court of a reciprocating territory.
  2. Sec 13 - WHEN FOREIGN JUDGMENT NOT CONCLUSIVE: a foreign judgment shall be conclusive as to any matter thereby directly adjudicated upon between the same parties or between parties under whom they or any of them claim litigating under the same title except: (a) where it has not been pronounced by a court of competent jurisdiction; (b) where it has not been given on the merits of the case; (c) where it appears on the face of the proceedings to be founded on an incorrect view of international law or a refusal to recognise the law of India in cases in which such law is applicable; (d) where the proceedings in which the judgment was obtained are opposed to natural justice; (e) where it has been obtained by fraud; (f) where it sustains a claim founded on a breach of any law in force in India. Mnemonic: C-M-I-N-F-B (Competent court, Merits, International law/ Indian law, Natural justice, Fraud, Breach of Indian law). Sec 13 is a rule of res judicata for foreign judgments; and it applies to judgments, not to the underlying foreign law. A foreign judgment on the merits means that the court applied its mind to the evidence; a decree by default or ex parte without considering evidence is not on merits (International Woollen Mills v Standard Wool (UK) Ltd, 2001: a foreign judgment is 'on the merits' only if the foreign court applied its mind to the merits and not merely on a technical default or without considering the defence).
  3. Competence (Sec 13(a)): the foreign court must be competent both under its own law and in the international sense (the defendant must have submitted to jurisdiction, or been resident/ present in that country, or the cause of action must have arisen there) - R. Viswanathan v Rukn-ul-Mulk Syed Abdul Wajid (1963): a foreign court's jurisdiction in personam requires that the defendant be a subject of, resident in, or have submitted to the jurisdiction of that court; Moloji Nar Singh Rao v Shankar Saran (1962): a decree of a foreign court passed against a defendant who appeared under protest to contest jurisdiction is not a submission. Matrimonial judgments: Y. Narasimha Rao v Y. Venkata Lakshmi (1991): a foreign matrimonial judgment is recognised in India only if the court had jurisdiction under the Act governing the parties, is on a ground available under that Act, and the respondent had submitted to the jurisdiction or consented; residence of a respondent for the purposes of matrimonial jurisdiction.
  4. Sec 14 - PRESUMPTION AS TO FOREIGN JUDGMENTS: the court shall presume, upon the production of any document purporting to be a certified copy of a foreign judgment, that such judgment was pronounced by a court of competent jurisdiction, unless the contrary appears on the record; but such presumption may be displaced by proving want of jurisdiction. Sec 44A - EXECUTION OF DECREES PASSED BY COURTS IN RECIPROCATING TERRITORY: (1) where a certified copy of a decree of any of the superior courts of any reciprocating territory has been filed in a District Court, the decree may be executed in India as if it had been passed by the District Court. (2) a certificate from the superior court stating the extent, if any, to which the decree has been satisfied or adjusted shall be filed along with the decree, and such certificate shall be conclusive proof of the extent of such satisfaction or adjustment. (3) the provisions of Sec 47 shall as from the filing of the certified copy of the decree apply to the proceedings of a District Court executing a decree under this section, and the District Court shall refuse execution of any such decree, if it is shown to the satisfaction of the court that the decree falls within any of the exceptions specified in clauses (a) to (f) of Sec 13. Explanation 1: 'reciprocating territory' means any country or territory outside India which the Central Government may, by notification in the Official Gazette, declare to be a reciprocating territory (notified: the United Kingdom, Singapore, Bangladesh, UAE, Malaysia, Trinidad and Tobago, New Zealand, the Cook Islands (including Niue) and the Trust Territories of Western Samoa, Hong Kong, Papua New Guinea, Fiji and Aden); 'superior courts' in relation to any such territory means such courts as may be specified in the said notification. Explanation 2: 'decree' with reference to a superior court means any decree or judgment of such court under which a sum of money is payable, not being a sum payable in respect of taxes or other charges of a like nature or in respect of a fine or other penalty, but shall in no case include an arbitration award, even if such an award is enforceable as a decree or judgment. Decrees of courts of non-reciprocating territories (e.g., USA) cannot be executed directly: a fresh suit must be filed on the foreign judgment (within the limitation period of 3 years from the date of the foreign judgment - Art 101 Limitation Act), and Sec 13 exceptions apply; Alcon Electronics v Celem SA (2017): a foreign decree of a non-reciprocating country can be enforced only by a fresh suit; and in respect of a reciprocating territory, the decree must come under Sec 44A. India has recently been considering the notification of other jurisdictions. The Arbitration Act Part II governs foreign awards (separate regime).
  5. SUITS BY ALIENS AND FOREIGN STATES (Secs 83-87B): Sec 83 - alien enemies residing in India with the permission of the Central Government, and alien friends, may sue in any court otherwise competent to try the suit, as if they were citizens of India; but alien enemies residing in India without such permission, or residing in a foreign country, shall not sue in any such court. Sec 84 - a foreign State may sue in a competent court, provided that the object of the suit is to enforce a private right vested in the Ruler of such State or in any officer of such State in his public capacity. Sec 85 - the Central Government may, at the request of the Ruler of a foreign State, or at the request of any person competent in the opinion of the Central Government to act on behalf of such Ruler, by order, appoint any person to prosecute or defend any suit on behalf of such Ruler. Sec 86 - SUITS AGAINST FOREIGN RULERS, AMBASSADORS AND ENVOYS: (1) no foreign State may be sued in any court otherwise competent to try the suit except with the consent of the Central Government certified in writing by a Secretary to that Government; (2) such consent may be given with respect to a specified suit or to several specified suits or with respect to all suits of any specified class or classes, and may specify, in the case of any suit or class of suits, the court in which the foreign State may be sued, but it shall not be given unless the foreign State has instituted a suit in the court against the person desiring to sue it, or by itself or another, trades within the local limits of the jurisdiction of the court, or is in possession of immovable property situated within those limits and is to be sued with reference to such property or for money charged thereon, or has expressly or impliedly waived the privilege accorded to it by this section; (3) Rulers, Ambassadors and Envoys of foreign States are similarly protected: they may not be arrested or have their property proceeded against in execution except with the consent of the Central Government certified by a Secretary (verify the text of Sec 86(1)-(4)). Sec 87 - style of foreign Rulers as parties to suits. Sec 87A - definitions of 'foreign State' and 'Ruler'. Sec 87B - Secs 85 and 86 apply to the Ruler of a former Indian State (former Indian Rulers). Case: Ethiopian Airlines v Ganesh Narain Saboo (2011): foreign State immunity and commercial transactions; Sec 86 and the restrictive immunity doctrine.

⚖Landmark cases

R. Viswanathan v Rukn-ul-Mulk Syed Abdul Wajid

Competence of a foreign court; jurisdiction in personam; Sec 13.

Y. Narasimha Rao v Y. Venkata Lakshmi

Recognition of foreign matrimonial judgments.

✎Memory aids

  • Sec 13 exceptions = C-M-I-N-F-B.

!Exam traps

  • TrapSec 44A: only decrees of SUPERIOR courts of RECIPROCATING territories can be executed directly; USA decrees need a fresh suit.
  • TrapForeign arbitral awards do not come under Sec 44A (Arbitration Act Part II).
  • TrapSec 13 applies to 'conclusive' foreign judgments - it is a rule of res judicata with six exceptions.
  • TrapSec 86 requires Central Government consent for suing a foreign State.

?Likely questions

  1. Sec 13 foreign judgment not conclusive: lack of competent jurisdiction, not on merits, natural justice, fraud, breach of Indian law.
  2. Execution of decrees of reciprocating territories: Sec 44A.
  3. Which section requires Central Government consent to sue a foreign State? Sec 86.
