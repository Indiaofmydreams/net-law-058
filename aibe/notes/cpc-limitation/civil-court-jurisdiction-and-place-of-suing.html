Jurisdiction of Civil Courts and Place of Suing (Secs 9, 15-25)

iOverview

Section 9 (all suits of civil nature), exclusion of jurisdiction, pecuniary/ territorial limits (Secs 15-20), timely objection (Sec 21) and transfer (Secs 22-25). At least one question every year.

★Must know

  1. Sec 9: the Courts shall (subject to the provisions herein contained) have jurisdiction to try all suits of a civil nature excepting suits of which their cognizance is either expressly or impliedly barred. Explanation I: a suit in which the right to property or to an office is contested is a suit of a civil nature, notwithstanding that such right may depend entirely on the decision of questions as to religious rites or ceremonies. Explanation II: for the purposes of Sec 9, it is immaterial whether or not any fees are attached to the office referred to in Explanation I or whether or not such office is attached to a particular place. A suit of civil nature = one where the principal question relates to determination of civil rights (right to property, contract, tort, status, office); not political or purely religious questions (caste disputes, rights of worship without civil right).
  2. EXPRESS BAR: when a statute expressly ousts the civil court (e.g., Sec 175 MV Act; Sec 34 SARFAESI Act). IMPLIED BAR: when a statute creates a special right/ liability and provides a complete machinery for adjudication. DHULABHAI v STATE OF MADHYA PRADESH (1968): principles - (1) where the statute gives finality to the orders of special tribunals the civil courts' jurisdiction must be held to be excluded if there is adequate remedy to do what the civil courts would normally do in a suit (such a provision, however, does not exclude those cases where the provisions of the particular Act have not been complied with or the statutory tribunal has not acted in conformity with the fundamental principles of judicial procedure); (2) where there is an express bar of the jurisdiction of the court, an examination of the scheme of the particular Act to find the adequacy or the sufficiency of the remedies provided may be relevant but is not decisive to sustain the jurisdiction of the civil court (where there is no express exclusion the examination of the remedies and the scheme of the particular Act to find out the intendment becomes necessary and the result of the inquiry may be decisive); (3) challenge to the provisions of the particular Act as ultra vires cannot be brought before tribunals constituted under that Act (even the High Court cannot go into that question on a revision or reference from the decision of tribunals); (4) when a provision is already declared unconstitutional or the constitutionality of any provision is to be challenged, a suit is open; (5) questions of the correctness of an assessment apart from its constitutionality are for the decision of the authorities, and a civil suit does not lie if the orders of the authorities are declared to be final or there is an express prohibition in the particular Act; (6) where the particular Act contains no machinery for refund of tax collected in excess of constitutional limits or illegally collected, a suit lies; (7) questions of the correctness of assessment apart from its constitutionality are for the decision of the authorities. Additional: a civil court can look into a case where the tribunal acts without jurisdiction/ in violation of fundamental principles of judicial procedure (Firm of Illuri Subbayya Chetty v State of Andhra Pradesh, 1964).
  3. SUBJECT-MATTER JURISDICTION cannot be conferred by consent, waiver or acquiescence; a decree passed by a court without inherent jurisdiction is a nullity and can be challenged at any stage, even in execution or collateral proceedings (Kiran Singh v Chaman Paswan, 1954; Hasham Abbas Sayyad). But objection to territorial or pecuniary jurisdiction may be waived and must be taken at the earliest opportunity (Sec 21). Jurisdiction over parties: Sec 20. Sec 11 CPC read with Sec 9: res judicata. Jurisdiction under Art 226 is not ousted by statutory bars (Whirlpool).
  4. Sec 15: every suit shall be instituted in the court of the lowest grade competent to try it. Sec 16 - SUITS RELATING TO IMMOVABLE PROPERTY: subject to the pecuniary or other limitations prescribed by any law, suits (a) for the recovery of immovable property with or without rent or profits, (b) for the partition of immovable property, (c) for foreclosure, sale or redemption in the case of a mortgage of or charge upon immovable property, (d) for the determination of any other right to or interest in immovable property, (e) for compensation for wrong to immovable property, (f) for the recovery of movable property actually under distraint or attachment, shall be instituted in the court within the local limits of whose jurisdiction the property is situate. PROVISO: a suit to obtain relief respecting, or compensation for wrong to, immovable property held by or on behalf of the defendant may, where the relief sought can be entirely obtained through his personal obedience, be instituted either in the court within the local limits of whose jurisdiction the property is situate, or in the court within the local limits of whose jurisdiction the defendant actually and voluntarily resides, or carries on business, or personally works for gain (Latin: 'actio in personam' exception based on equity acts in personam - e.g., suit for specific performance of a contract for sale of immovable property; suit for injunction restraining a defendant). Sec 17: where a suit is to obtain relief respecting, or compensation for wrong to, immovable property situate within the jurisdiction of different courts, the suit may be instituted in any court within the local limits of whose jurisdiction any portion of the property is situate, provided the entire claim is cognizable by such court as regards the value of the subject-matter. Sec 18: where local limits of jurisdiction of courts are uncertain. Sec 19: SUITS FOR COMPENSATION FOR WRONGS TO PERSON OR MOVABLES: where a suit is for compensation for wrong done to the person or to movable property, if the wrong was done within the local limits of the jurisdiction of one court and the defendant resides, or carries on business, or personally works for gain, within the local limits of the jurisdiction of another court, the suit may be instituted at the option of the plaintiff in either of the said courts.
  5. Sec 20 - OTHER SUITS: subject to the limitations aforesaid, every suit shall be instituted in a court within the local limits of whose jurisdiction (a) the defendant, or each of the defendants where there are more than one, at the time of the commencement of the suit, actually and voluntarily resides, or carries on business, or personally works for gain; or (b) any of the defendants, where there are more than one, at the time of the commencement of the suit actually and voluntarily resides, or carries on business, or personally works for gain, provided that in such case either the leave of the court is given, or the defendants who do not reside, or carry on business, or personally work for gain, as aforesaid, acquiesce in such institution; or (c) the cause of action, wholly or in part, arises. Explanation: a corporation shall be deemed to carry on business at its sole or principal office in India or, in respect of any cause of action arising at any place where it has also a subordinate office, at such place. Principles: 'cause of action' - Sec 20(c) requires a material part of the bundle of facts to have arisen within the territory; mere residence of plaintiff is irrelevant (Dhodha House v S.K. Maingi, 2006; Navinchandra N. Majithia v State of Maharashtra, 2000 - Art 226 context). AGREEMENT AS TO JURISDICTION: parties cannot by agreement confer jurisdiction on a court which has none, but where two or more courts have jurisdiction, parties may by agreement choose one and exclude the other (Hakam Singh v Gammon (India) Ltd, 1971; A.B.C. Laminart v A.P. Agencies, 1989 - clauses such as 'subject to Delhi jurisdiction' only ousts other courts if words like 'only', 'exclusive', 'alone' are used or the intention is clear; Swastik Gases v Indian Oil Corporation, 2013 - 'subject to Kolkata jurisdiction' read as exclusive; Patel Roadways v Prasad Trading, 1991 - corporation's cause of action at subordinate office). Choice of forum by agreement is not hit by Sec 28 of the Contract Act.
  6. Sec 21 OBJECTION TO JURISDICTION: (1) no objection as to the place of suing shall be allowed by any appellate or revisional court unless such objection was taken in the court of first instance at the earliest possible opportunity and, in all cases where issues are settled, at or before such settlement, and unless there has been a consequent failure of justice. (2) similar for pecuniary jurisdiction. (3) objection as to competence of the executing court with reference to the local limits of its jurisdiction shall not be allowed by any appellate or revisional court unless taken in the executing court at the earliest possible opportunity and unless there has been a consequent failure of justice. Sec 21A (2002): no suit shall lie challenging the validity of a decree passed in a former suit between the same parties on any ground based on an objection as to the place of suing. Sec 21 does NOT cure inherent lack of jurisdiction (subject matter). Sec 6, Sec 15: pecuniary - courts' limits fixed by State civil courts Acts (Bengal, Agra and Assam Civil Courts Act 1887; Madras Civil Courts Act, etc.) - valuation of suits: Suits Valuation Act 1887; Court Fees Act 1870.
  7. TRANSFER: Sec 22 - where a suit may be instituted in any one of two or more courts and is instituted in one of such courts, any defendant, after notice to the other parties, may, at the earliest possible opportunity and in all cases where issues are settled at or before such settlement, apply to have the suit transferred to another court, and the court to which the application is made, after considering the objections of the other parties (if any), shall determine in which of the several courts having jurisdiction the suit shall proceed. Sec 23: application to which court - to the appellate court (if the courts are subordinate to the same appellate court) or the High Court (if subordinate to different appellate courts of the same High Court, or to different High Courts). Sec 24: GENERAL POWER OF TRANSFER AND WITHDRAWAL: on the application of any of the parties and after notice to the parties and after hearing such of them as desire to be heard, or of its own motion without such notice, the High Court or the District Court may at any stage (a) transfer any suit, appeal or other proceeding pending before it for trial or disposal to any court subordinate to it and competent to try or dispose of the same; or (b) withdraw any suit, appeal or other proceeding pending in any court subordinate to it, and (i) try or dispose of the same; or (ii) transfer the same for trial or disposal to any court subordinate to it and competent to try or dispose of the same; or (iii) re-transfer the same for trial or disposal to the court from which it was withdrawn. A court to which a suit is transferred shall continue the same from the stage at which it was transferred or re-try (Sec 24(3)). Sec 24(5): a suit or proceeding may be transferred under this section although the court from which it is transferred has no jurisdiction to try it. Sec 25: POWER OF THE SUPREME COURT TO TRANSFER SUITS ETC.: on the application of a party and after notice to the parties, the Supreme Court may, at any stage, if satisfied that an order under this section is expedient for the ends of justice, direct that any suit, appeal or other proceeding be transferred from a High Court or other civil court in one State to a High Court or other civil court in any other State (2002 amendment extended Sec 25 to transfer from one High Court to another or from civil court in one State to another; earlier restricted to State-to-State transfer). Grounds for transfer: convenience of parties, apprehension of bias, balance of convenience, multiplicity, public interest; the discretion is exercised judiciously (Maneka Sanjay Gandhi v Rani Jethmalani, 1979: the plaintiff's convenience is not the sole consideration; a reasonable apprehension of not getting justice).

⚖Landmark cases

Dhulabhai v State of MP

1968

Principles on exclusion of civil courts' jurisdiction by statute.

Kiran Singh v Chaman Paswan

1954

Decree passed without jurisdiction is a nullity; Sec 11 of the Suits Valuation Act.

Hakam Singh v Gammon (India) Ltd

1971

Parties may choose one among several competent courts; ouster of all courts is void.

A.B.C. Laminart v A.P. Agencies

1989

Exclusive jurisdiction clause; cause of action principles.

Swastik Gases v Indian Oil Corporation

2013

'Subject to ... jurisdiction' clause read as exclusive.

Maneka Sanjay Gandhi v Rani Jethmalani

1979

Transfer petitions under Sec 25 - ends of justice; apprehension of unfair trial.

!Exam traps

  • TrapObjection to territorial and pecuniary jurisdiction: waived unless taken at the earliest possible opportunity, before settlement of issues, and failure of justice shown (Sec 21). Subject-matter jurisdiction: cannot be waived.
  • TrapSec 16 proviso: suits for relief obtainable by personal obedience may also be filed where the defendant resides.
  • TrapSec 19 gives the plaintiff an option; Sec 20 is the residuary provision (defendant's residence or cause of action).
  • TrapA corporation carries on business at its principal office and, for causes arising there, also at a subordinate office (Explanation to Sec 20).
  • TrapSec 24: High Court or District Court; Sec 25: only the Supreme Court.

?Likely questions

  1. Suits relating to immovable property are to be filed where the property is situated: Sec 16.
  2. Who can transfer a suit from one State to another? Supreme Court under Sec 25.
  3. Sec 21: objection to place of suing must be taken at the earliest possible opportunity.
  4. Which section gives civil courts jurisdiction to try all suits of civil nature? Sec 9.
