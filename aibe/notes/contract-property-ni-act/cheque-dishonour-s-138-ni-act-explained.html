Dishonour of Cheque: Section 138 (Chapter XVII, Secs 138-148)

iOverview

The most predictable AIBE area within this subject. Statutory timelines, presumptions, jurisdiction (Sec 142(2)), interim compensation (Sec 143A), appellate deposit (Sec 148), compounding (Sec 147). 2025-26 Supreme Court decisions have been numerous; the principles behind them are fair game.

★Must know

  1. BACKGROUND: Chapter XVII (Secs 138-142) was inserted by the Banking, Public Financial Institutions and Negotiable Instruments Laws (Amendment) Act 1988 to promote the credibility of cheques. Amendments: 2002 (Secs 143-147 added; summary trial; 30-day notice; 15-day payment period), 2015 (Sec 142(2) and 142A - territorial jurisdiction), 2018 (Sec 143A interim compensation; Sec 148 deposit in appeal; maximum punishment raised from 1 year to 2 years). Criminal liability for cheque dishonour is a civil wrong made criminal; but the offence is compoundable (Sec 147).
  2. SEC 138 - ESSENTIALS: (1) a cheque drawn by a person on an account maintained by him with a banker; (2) for payment of any amount of money to another person from out of that account; (3) for the discharge, in whole or in part, of any debt or other liability (Explanation: legally enforceable debt or other liability); (4) the cheque is returned by the bank unpaid either because the amount of money standing to the credit of the account is insufficient to honour the cheque, or that it exceeds the amount arranged to be paid from that account by an agreement made with the bank; (5) the cheque was presented to the bank within a period of six months from the date on which it was drawn or within the period of its validity, whichever is earlier (practically THREE MONTHS since RBI's circular of 1 April 2012); (6) the payee or holder in due course makes a DEMAND for the payment of the said amount of money by giving a NOTICE in writing to the drawer within THIRTY DAYS of the receipt of information by him from the bank regarding the return of the cheque as unpaid; (7) the drawer fails to make payment within FIFTEEN DAYS of the receipt of the said notice. If these ingredients are satisfied, the drawer is deemed to have committed an offence, punishable with imprisonment for a term which may extend to TWO YEARS, or with fine which may extend to TWICE the amount of the cheque, or with both.
  3. Dishonour for reasons other than insufficiency of funds - 'payment stopped by drawer', 'account closed', 'refer to drawer', 'signature differs/ not as per specimen' etc. Courts have held that stop-payment instructions and account closure attract Sec 138 (the Sec 138 purpose of preventing dishonour; Modi Cements v Kuchil Kumar Nandi, 1998; Goa Plast v Chico Ursula D'Souza, 2004). However, a cheque with a patent material alteration cannot found a conviction (SC, 17 July 2026).
  4. NOTICE: the notice must be in writing, must demand the cheque amount (not a larger sum - Suman Sethi v Ajay K. Churiwal, 2000 - a notice demanding a sum with other claims was held vitiated only if the cheque amount isn't separately demanded), and must be given within 30 days of receipt of the bank's information. A notice sent to the correct address by registered post is presumed served (Sec 27 General Clauses Act; C.C. Alavi Haji v Palapetty Muhammed, 2007 - the drawer cannot defeat the complaint by saying he did not receive the notice; he must pay within 15 days of the deemed receipt). Notice can also be given by email/ other electronic modes (Information Technology Act); a notice by the payee's authorised agent/ advocate is valid.
  5. CAUSE OF ACTION AND LIMITATION (Sec 142): a court takes cognizance only on a written complaint made by the payee or holder in due course, within ONE MONTH of the date on which the cause of action arises under clause (c) of the proviso to Sec 138 (i.e., from the expiry of the 15 days). The Court may take cognizance after the prescribed period if the complainant satisfies it that he had sufficient cause for not making a complaint within such period (Sec 142(1)(b)); the SC (2026 quarterly digest) addressed the sequencing of condonation of delay and cognizance. No court inferior to that of a Metropolitan Magistrate or a Judicial Magistrate of the First Class shall try an offence under Sec 138. Complaint can be filed by an authorised representative; a company (as complainant) acts through an authorised person. Cheque may be presented multiple times within its validity period, but only ONE cause of action arises from the notice following the dishonour on a presentation (MSR Leathers v S. Palaniappan, 2013: successive presentations are allowed; failure to file complaint after the first dishonour does not bar a complaint after the later one). In January 2026, the Supreme Court held that dishonour of multiple cheques issued in the same transaction can give rise to separate causes of action and separate complaints.
  6. SEC 142(2) TERRITORIAL JURISDICTION (as inserted in 2015, with Sec 142A): the offence under Sec 138 shall be inquired into and tried only by a court within whose local jurisdiction, (a) if the cheque is delivered for collection through an account, the branch of the bank where the payee or holder in due course, as the case may be, maintains the account, is situated; or (b) if the cheque is presented for payment otherwise than through an account, the branch of the drawee bank where the drawer maintains the account, is situated. Explanation: where a cheque is delivered for collection at any branch of the bank of the payee or holder in due course, then, the cheque shall be deemed to have been delivered to the branch of the bank in which the payee or holder in due course, as the case may be, maintains the account. This overrides Dashrath Rupsingh Rathod v State of Maharashtra (2014), which had held that the place of the drawee bank was the place of jurisdiction. Sec 142A validated the transfer of pending cases. Bridgestone India v Inderpal Singh (2016) clarified the 2015 Amendment's retrospectivity.
  7. SEC 139 PRESUMPTION IN FAVOUR OF THE HOLDER: it shall be presumed, unless the contrary is proved, that the holder of a cheque received the cheque of the nature referred to in Sec 138 for the discharge, in whole or in part, of any debt or other liability. Sec 139 presumption includes the existence of a legally enforceable debt (Rangappa v Sri Mohan, 2010). The presumption is rebuttable; the accused need not step into the witness box and may rely on the material on record; the standard is 'preponderance of probabilities' (not beyond reasonable doubt - Basalingappa v Mudibasappa, 2019; Kumar Exports v Sharma Carpets, 2009). A signed blank cheque voluntarily handed over attracts the presumption (Bir Singh v Mukesh Kumar, 2019). In August 2026 the Supreme Court restated that a mere denial of debt or consideration is not enough to rebut the presumption. In April 2026 (Renuka v State of Maharashtra) the Court held that where the basic ingredients of Sec 138 are made out, the complaint cannot be quashed at the pre-trial stage on the ground that the cheque was not issued for a debt - that is a matter of trial. The Court has also held that a time-barred debt may still be a 'legally enforceable debt' only if the cheque amounts to a fresh promise within Sec 25(3) of the Contract Act (check the cases before relying), and that violation of Sec 269SS of the Income-tax Act (cash loan above the prescribed limit) does not automatically make the debt unenforceable (discussed in Sanjabij Tari v Kishore S. Borcar - verify the exact holding before citing in an examination of case law).
  8. SEC 140: it shall not be a defence in a prosecution for an offence under Sec 138 that the drawer had no reason to believe when he issued the cheque that the cheque may be dishonoured on presentment for the reasons stated in that section. SEC 141 OFFENCES BY COMPANIES: where an offence under Sec 138 has been committed by a company, every person who, at the time the offence was committed, was in charge of, and was responsible to the company for the conduct of the business of the company, as well as the company, shall be deemed to be guilty and shall be liable to be proceeded against. Proviso: not liable if he proves that the offence was committed without his knowledge, or that he had exercised all due diligence to prevent it. Sec 141(2): where the offence is committed with the consent or connivance of, or is attributable to the neglect on the part of, any director, manager, secretary or other officer of the company, such person shall also be deemed guilty. Principles: S.M.S. Pharmaceuticals v Neeta Bhalla (2005) - the complaint must specifically aver that the director was in charge of and responsible for the conduct of business; mere designation as director is not enough; but a signatory to the cheque is liable by virtue of signing; Aneeta Hada v Godfather Travels (2012) - the company must be arraigned as an accused for the directors to be prosecuted (the company is the principal offender); Pooja Ravinder Devidasani v State of Maharashtra (2014) - non-executive directors and those who resigned cannot be roped in without specific allegations. Also: the partnership firm is a 'company' for Sec 141 (Explanation: 'company' means any body corporate and includes a firm or other association of individuals; 'director' in relation to a firm means a partner).
  9. SEC 143 SUMMARY TRIAL: notwithstanding anything in the CrPC (read now with the BNSS 2023), all offences under Sec 138 shall be tried by a Judicial Magistrate of the First Class or by a Metropolitan Magistrate, and the provisions of Secs 262 to 265 (both inclusive) of the CrPC shall, as far as may be, apply to such trials (now the summary trial provisions of Secs 283-288 BNSS, saved/ applied through the General Clauses Act and Sec 531 BNSS). In the case of conviction in a summary trial, it shall be lawful for the Magistrate to pass a sentence of imprisonment for a term not exceeding ONE YEAR and an amount of fine exceeding five thousand rupees. If, at the commencement of or in the course of a summary trial, it appears that a sentence of imprisonment exceeding one year may have to be passed, the Magistrate shall after hearing the parties record an order to that effect and thereafter recall any witness who may have been examined and proceed to hear or rehear the case in the manner provided by the Code (conversion to summons case). Sec 143(3): the trial shall be conducted as expeditiously as possible and an endeavour shall be made to conclude the trial within six months from the date of filing of the complaint. Meters and Instruments v Kanchan Mehta (2018): guidelines on summary trial, compounding, and conversion.
  10. SEC 143A INTERIM COMPENSATION (2018): (1) notwithstanding anything contained in the CrPC, the Court trying an offence under Sec 138 may order the drawer of the cheque to pay interim compensation to the complainant (a) in a summary trial or a summons case, where he pleads not guilty to the accusation made in the complaint; and (b) in any other case, upon framing of charge. (2) The interim compensation shall not exceed TWENTY PER CENT of the amount of the cheque. (3) It shall be paid within SIXTY DAYS from the date of the order, or within such further period not exceeding THIRTY DAYS as may be directed by the Court on sufficient cause being shown. (4) If the drawer is acquitted, the Court shall direct the complainant to repay to the drawer the amount so paid with interest at the bank rate as published by the RBI, prevailing at the beginning of the relevant financial year, within 60 days (extendable by 30 days). The recovery may be made as if it were a fine under Sec 421 CrPC (now under the BNSS). The provision is directory/ 'may' (Rakesh Ranjan Srivastava v State of Jharkhand, 2023) but, per G.J. Raja v Tejraj Surana (2019), Sec 143A applies only to offences committed after 1 September 2018 (it is prospective). The complainant who is a 'victim' can appeal (Sec 372 CrPC proviso/ Sec 413 BNSS).
  11. SEC 144: mode of service of summons - by speed post/ approved courier; Sec 145: evidence on affidavit - the evidence of the complainant may be given by him on affidavit and may, subject to all just exceptions, be read in evidence in any enquiry, trial or other proceeding; the Court may, if it thinks fit, and shall, on the application of the prosecution or the accused, summon and examine any person giving evidence on affidavit as to the facts contained therein. Sec 146: banks' slip or memo having thereon the official mark denoting that the cheque has been dishonoured shall be prima facie evidence of the fact of dishonour (court shall presume the genuineness of such mark unless disproved). Sec 147: OFFENCES TO BE COMPOUNDABLE - notwithstanding anything contained in the CrPC, every offence punishable under this Act shall be compoundable. Damodar S. Prabhu v Sayed Babalal H (2010): guidelines to encourage compounding by costs payable to the Legal Services Authority at the rate of: no costs if compounded at the first or second hearing; 10% of the cheque amount (before Magistrate at later stage); 15% (Sessions Court/ High Court); 20% (Supreme Court). The recent decision in Sanjabij Tari v Kishore S. Borcar consolidated the procedural directions in NI Act cases and modified the compounding framework - read the latest cost schedule in the judgment before relying on figures.
  12. SEC 148 DEPOSIT IN APPEAL (2018): in an appeal by the drawer against conviction under Sec 138, the Appellate Court may order the appellant to deposit such sum which shall be a minimum of TWENTY PER CENT of the fine or compensation awarded by the trial court. This amount is in addition to any interim compensation paid under Sec 143A. It shall be deposited within 60 days (extendable by 30 days). The Appellate Court may direct release of the amount to the complainant at any time during the pendency of the appeal. Surinder Singh Deswal v Virender Gandhi (2019): Sec 148 applies to appeals even in cases for cheque offences committed before the 2018 Amendment (appeals filed after 1 September 2018); the word 'may' is to be read as 'shall' normally - the 20% deposit should ordinarily be directed except for special reasons (Jamboo Bhandari v M.P. State Industrial Development Corp, 2023).
  13. OTHER LEADING DEVELOPMENTS: (a) Indian Bank Association v Union of India (2014) - directions for speedy disposal; (b) In Re: Expeditious Trial of Cases under Section 138 of NI Act (2021) - Suo Motu; special courts and guidelines (including online summons); (c) Sripati Singh v State of Jharkhand (2021) - security cheques: if the debt is crystallised/ due, a cheque given as security may attract Sec 138; (d) P. Mohanraj v Shah Brothers Ispat (2021) - the moratorium under Sec 14 of the IBC bars Sec 138 proceedings against the corporate debtor, but not against its directors/ signatories (natural persons); the individual-insolvency moratorium (Sec 96, Part III IBC) and its effect on Sec 138 was considered by the Supreme Court in a 2026 ruling - read the judgment for the exact outcome; (e) 4 November 2026 - the Supreme Court held that an Asset Reconstruction Company that takes over a debt (assignee of the loan) can step into the shoes of the bank and pursue a Sec 138 prosecution on the dishonoured cheque (the cheque 'travels with the loan'); (f) 16 April 2026 (Canara Bank v Kavita Chowdhary) - a bank's failure to present a cheque within its validity period as agent for its customer can amount to deficiency in service under the Consumer Protection Act; (g) The legislature has repeatedly considered decriminalisation of Sec 138; as of now it remains a criminal offence (the Jan Vishwas (Amendment of Provisions) Act 2023 did not decriminalise Sec 138).
  14. ADMINISTRATIVE POINTS for exam: (i) 'Drawer' liable; payee/ holder in due course complains; (ii) the offence is a 'non-cognizable' offence/ summons case; (iii) A person cannot be convicted under Sec 138 for an unsigned cheque; (iv) The cheque must relate to a legally enforceable debt existing on the date of the cheque; a post-dated cheque is a cheque only on its date - presenting before the date is premature (Sec 138 offence is not committed if the cheque is dishonoured before the date); (v) 'Account payee' cheques have no effect on Sec 138; (vi) The offence is personal to the drawer; legal heirs are not criminally liable, though civil liability may continue.

!Exam traps

  • TrapCheque validity is 3 months (practically), though the statute says 'six months or validity, whichever is earlier'.
  • TrapNotice: 30 days from RECEIPT OF INFORMATION from the bank (not from the date of the cheque). Payment: 15 days from receipt of NOTICE. Complaint: 1 month from the expiry of the 15 days.
  • TrapMaximum punishment: 2 years (since 2018) - not 1 year. Fine: up to twice the cheque amount.
  • Trap143A is for interim compensation by trial court (20% cap); 148 is a deposit in appeal (minimum 20%). Don't confuse 'cap' with 'minimum'.
  • TrapOffence is compoundable (Sec 147) - at any stage with costs as per Damodar Prabhu.
  • TrapJurisdiction post-2015: payee's bank branch (collection through account), not the drawee bank.
  • TrapSec 141: the company must be made an accused; directors need specific averments; signatory automatically liable.

?Likely questions

  1. Time for sending demand notice under Sec 138(b): 30 days.
  2. Drawer's time to pay after notice: 15 days.
  3. Limitation to file complaint: one month from cause of action.
  4. Interim compensation limit: 20% (Sec 143A).
  5. Which section makes offence compoundable? 147.
  6. Which section deals with offences by companies? 141.
  7. Trial under NI Act is: summary trial (Sec 143).
