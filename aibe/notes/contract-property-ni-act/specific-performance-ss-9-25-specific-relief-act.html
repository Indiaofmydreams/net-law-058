Specific Relief Act: Specific Performance of Contracts (Secs 9-25)

iOverview

The heart of the Act. Know what contracts can/cannot be specifically enforced, who can sue, the readiness-and-willingness requirement, and the 2018 reforms.

★Must know

  1. Sec 10 (as amended): specific performance of a contract shall be enforced by the court, subject to the provisions contained in sub-section (2) of Sec 11, Sec 14 and Sec 16.
  2. Sec 11: specific performance of contracts connected with trusts enforced - Sec 11(1): where the trustee has contracted within his powers; Sec 11(2): a contract made by a trustee in excess of his powers or in breach of trust cannot be specifically enforced.
  3. Sec 12: specific performance of part of a contract. General rule: court shall not direct specific performance of a part of contract. Exceptions: Sec 12(2): if a party fails to perform a part of the contract which bears only a small proportion to the whole in value and admits of compensation in money, the court may direct specific performance of so much as can be performed and award compensation; Sec 12(3): where a part (of considerable portion) cannot be performed, the court may direct specific performance of the remainder only if the party in default relinquishes all claims to further performance and compensation (or if the plaintiff pays the whole consideration without abatement); Sec 12(4): where a part is separate and distinct, the court can enforce that part.
  4. Sec 13: rights of purchaser or lessee against a person with no title or imperfect title - the buyer can require the seller to make good title acquired subsequently and for compensation, including enforcement against after-acquired title.
  5. Sec 14 (as substituted in 2018): contracts which cannot be specifically enforced: (a) where the party seeking performance has obtained substituted performance under Sec 20; (b) a contract the performance of which involves a continuous duty which the court cannot supervise; (c) a contract so dependent on the personal qualifications of the parties that the court cannot enforce specific performance of its material terms; (d) a contract which in its nature is determinable. Typical examples: contracts of personal service, building contracts requiring continuous supervision, partnership agreements, agreement to marry. Before 2018, additional grounds included contracts where compensation was an adequate relief.
  6. Sec 15: who may obtain specific performance: any party to the contract; representative-in-interest; principal; beneficiary; remainderman; reversioner; the amalgamated company; etc. (Sec 15(a)-(g)).
  7. Sec 16 PERSONAL BARS: specific performance cannot be enforced in favour of a person (a) who has obtained substituted performance under Sec 20; (b) who has become incapable of performing, or violates any essential term of, the contract that on his part remains to be performed, or acts in fraud of the contract, or wilfully acts at variance with, or in subversion of, the relation intended to be established by the contract; (c) who fails to prove that he has performed or has always been ready and willing to perform the essential terms of the contract which are to be performed by him, other than terms the performance of which has been prevented or waived by the defendant.
  8. READINESS AND WILLINGNESS (Sec 16(c)): 'readiness' = financial capacity to perform (and, where applicable, to produce the money); 'willingness' = conduct/intention. Both must be pleaded and proved, from the date of the agreement until the date of the decree. A plaintiff need not actually tender the price unless the contract so requires (Explanation (ii)), but must show readiness. Delay alone is not fatal if within limitation, but unexplained delay with changing conditions or inconsistent conduct disentitles. Recent Supreme Court judgments of 2026 (Mohammed Khaleel v Jayamma, 23 June 2026; V.N.A.S. Chandran v S. Venila, 31 July 2026) restate this: readiness must be proved by reference to the relevant period, with documentary proof of funds (post-suit FDRs are insufficient), and a decree can be refused after prolonged delay when it would be inequitable. By contrast, the Court has also held that a buyer's delay in issuing a legal notice is not conclusive of lack of willingness where nearly the entire price has been paid (2026 INSC 573).
  9. Sec 17: a contract to sell or let immovable property cannot be specifically enforced in favour of a vendor or lessor who knowingly has no title, or who, though believing he had good title, afterwards becomes aware he has none. Sec 18: where a defendant sets up a variation - the plaintiff can obtain performance only with the variation in cases of fraud/ mistake/ misrepresentation, to produce a legal result, or subsequent variation by parties. Sec 19: relief against parties and persons claiming under them by subsequent title - except a transferee for value who paid money in good faith without notice of the original contract (and a person claiming under a title prior to the contract and known to the plaintiff which might have been displaced by the defendant).
  10. Sec 20 (as substituted in 2018) SUBSTITUTED PERFORMANCE: (1) Without prejudice to the generality of the provisions of the Contract Act, and subject to the provisions of this section, where the contract is broken due to non-performance of promise by any party, the party who suffers by such breach shall have the option of substituted performance through a third party or by his own agency, and, recover expenses and costs actually incurred, spent or suffered by him, from the party committing such breach. (2) No substituted performance of contract under sub-section (1) shall be undertaken unless the party who suffers such breach has given a notice in writing, of not less than thirty days, to the party in breach calling upon him to perform the contract within such time as specified in the notice, and on such refusal or failure by the party in breach, the party who suffers such breach may get the same performed by a third party or by his own agency. (3) After the contract has been so performed, the party who suffers such breach shall not be entitled to claim relief of specific performance against the party in breach. (4) Nothing in this section shall prevent the party who has suffered breach of contract from claiming compensation from the party in breach.
  11. Sec 20A: no injunction shall be granted by a court in a suit under this Act involving a contract relating to an infrastructure project specified in the Schedule, where granting the injunction would cause impediment or delay in the progress or completion of such project. Sec 20B: State Governments, with the concurrence of the Chief Justice of the High Court, to designate special courts (Civil Courts of required grade) to try suits under the Act in respect of infrastructure project contracts. Sec 20C: suits involving infrastructure contracts to be disposed of within twelve months from the date of service of summons to the defendant, extendable by a maximum of six months in total, recorded in writing.
  12. Sec 21: in a suit for specific performance, the plaintiff may also claim compensation for its breach, either in addition to, or in substitution for, such performance; if the court decides that specific performance ought not to be granted, but that there is a contract and the defendant has broken it, and the plaintiff is entitled to compensation, it shall award him compensation; if the court decides that specific performance ought to be granted but that it is insufficient to satisfy the justice of the case, it shall award compensation in addition. If the plaintiff has not claimed compensation, the court shall, at any stage of the proceeding, allow him to amend the plaint. Compensation is assessed under Sec 73 of the Contract Act.
  13. Sec 22: in a suit for specific performance of a contract to transfer immovable property, the plaintiff may also ask for possession, or partition and separate possession, and any other relief, including refund of earnest money/ deposit. Sec 23: a contract that stipulates for payment of liquidated damages in the event of breach does not by itself prevent specific performance, unless the court finds that the intention was that the party in default could pay and be relieved of performance (alternative performance). Sec 24: dismissal of a suit for specific performance bars a suit for compensation for breach, but does not bar other relief to which he is entitled. Sec 25 was omitted by the 2018 Amendment.
  14. Time of essence: time is not of the essence for contracts relating to immovable property unless expressly or impliedly so (Sec 55 Contract Act; Chand Rani v Kamal Rani). Limitation: three years from the date fixed for performance, or if no date, from the date the plaintiff has notice that performance is refused (Article 54, Limitation Act 1963).
  15. Equitable principles that continue to colour the remedy: he who seeks equity must do equity; delay defeats equity; hardship to the defendant; unfair advantage. Post-2018, hardship is only relevant under Sec 20 pre-2018 or as a defence under Sec 9 (e.g. mistake, fraud).

⚖Landmark cases

Mohammed Khaleel v Jayamma (SC, 23 Jun 2026)

-

Continuous readiness and willingness from the date of agreement to decree; readiness is financial capacity, willingness is conduct; post-suit financial documents insufficient.

V.N.A.S. Chandran v S. Venila (SC, 31 Jul 2026)

-

Specific performance refused after prolonged delay and inconsistent conduct; decree not an automatic consequence of a valid agreement.

Lourdu Mari David v Louis Chinnaya Arogiaswamy

1996

Plaintiff must plead and prove readiness and willingness.

K.S. Vidyanadam v Vairavan

1997

Time not of essence for immovable property, but unreasonable delay in rising markets matters.

Saradamani Kandappan v S. Rajalakshmi

2011

Rising property prices: courts require prompt action.

Chand Rani v Kamal Rani

1993

Time is not of the essence in agreements for sale of immovable property.

Satya Jain v Anis Ahmed Rushdie

2013

Equitable considerations in specific performance, burden on plaintiff.

!Exam traps

  • TrapPost-2018 Sec 10: 'shall be enforced'. Pre-2018: 'may be enforced' with discretion (old Sec 20).
  • TrapSec 14 grounds now are only four (a-d). 'Compensation adequate relief' ground was DELETED in 2018.
  • TrapSec 20: substituted performance needs at least 30 days' written notice.
  • TrapSec 20C: 12 months (+6 months) for infrastructure suits.
  • TrapReadiness and willingness is a question of fact, and must be PLEADED and PROVED.

?Likely questions

  1. Contract which cannot be specifically enforced (Sec 14): personal skill / continuous duty / determinable.
  2. Substituted performance notice period: 30 days.
  3. Which section deals with readiness and willingness: Sec 16(c).
  4. No injunction in infrastructure project contracts: Sec 20A.
  5. Limitation for specific performance suit: 3 years.
