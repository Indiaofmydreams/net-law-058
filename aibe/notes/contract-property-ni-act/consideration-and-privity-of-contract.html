Consideration and Privity (Sections 2(d), 25)

iOverview

Indian law of consideration is wider than English law. Examiners love Sec 2(d), Sec 25 and the exceptions to 'no consideration, no contract'.

★Must know

  1. Sec 2(d): when at the desire of the promisor, the promisee or any other person has done or abstained from doing, or does or abstains, or promises to do or abstain, such act or abstinence is called consideration. Key: 'at the desire of the promisor' AND 'promisee or any other person'.
  2. Therefore in India consideration may move from a stranger (Chinnaya v Ramayya), but a stranger to the CONTRACT cannot sue (privity). English law requires consideration to move from the promisee.
  3. Consideration may be past, present or future. Past consideration is valid in India (Sec 2(d) 'has done or abstained'), if done at the desire of the promisor.
  4. Rules: must be real (not illusory), lawful, need not be adequate (Explanation 2 to Sec 25) but must be something of value; must not be physically impossible; must not be a pre-existing public duty (Collins v Godefroy); performance of existing contractual duty is generally not consideration (Indian law is more flexible).
  5. Sec 25: an agreement made without consideration is void, unless: (1) it is expressed in writing AND registered under the law for registration of documents, and is made on account of natural love and affection between parties standing in a near relation to each other; (2) it is a promise to compensate, wholly or in part, a person who has already voluntarily done something for the promisor, or something which the promisor was legally compellable to do (past voluntary service); (3) it is a promise, made in writing and signed by the person to be charged (or his authorised agent), to pay wholly or in part a debt of which the creditor might have enforced payment but for the law of limitation.
  6. Other exceptions to 'no consideration no contract': Sec 185 (no consideration needed to create an agency), Sec 63 (promise to remit performance/ accord), Sec 126-127 (past consideration for guarantee), completed gift (Sec 25 Explanation 1), bailment (gratuitous), charitable subscriptions acted upon (Kedar Nath v Gorie Mohamed).
  7. Privity of contract: only parties can sue/be sued (Tweddle v Atkinson; Dunlop v Selfridge). Exceptions in India: (a) beneficiary under a trust/charge; (b) marriage settlements/family arrangements (Khwaja Muhammad Khan v Husaini Begum); (c) assignment of contract; (d) acknowledgment/estoppel; (e) agency; (f) covenants running with land; (g) contracts through statutory duty. M.C. Chacko v State Bank of Travancore (1969): a person for whose benefit a contract is made may sue to enforce it.
  8. Doctrine of promissory estoppel: Central London Property Trust v High Trees; in India, Motilal Padampat Sugar Mills v State of UP (1979): applies against the Government; need not show detriment, only alteration of position; cannot be used to compel the Government to act contrary to law and public interest.

⚖Landmark cases

Chinnaya v Ramayya

1882

Consideration can move from a third party; promisee need not be the one giving it.

Durga Prasad v Baldeo

1880

Consideration must be at the desire of the promisor; a person who built shops on his own initiative cannot claim.

Kedar Nath v Gorie Mohamed

1886

Subscribers' promise to build a Town Hall: expenses incurred on the faith of the promise constituted consideration.

M.C. Chacko v State Bank of Travancore

1969

A beneficiary can enforce a contract made for his benefit.

Motilal Padampat Sugar Mills v State of UP

1979

Promissory estoppel applicable against Government.

Tweddle v Atkinson (1861); Dunlop v Selfridge

1915

Privity of contract.

!Exam traps

  • TrapSec 25(1) needs BOTH writing and REGISTRATION and 'natural love and affection' and 'near relation'.
  • TrapSec 25(3) (time-barred debt) needs a WRITTEN promise SIGNED by the debtor; oral promises don't qualify. It is a new promise, not an extension of limitation.
  • Trap'Consideration need not be adequate but must be real'. A question may ask which of these is 'not' valid consideration: past consideration is valid in India, but past voluntary act not at promisor's desire is not.

?Likely questions

  1. In Indian law, consideration can move from: promisee or any other person.
  2. Which exception to 'no consideration' requires registration? (Sec 25(1))
  3. A stranger to a contract cannot sue: this is the doctrine of? (privity)
