iOverview

Object and structure: the IBC consolidates laws on insolvency of corporate persons, partnership firms and individuals, in a time-bound manner, to maximise value of assets, promote entrepreneurship, availability of credit and balance the interests of all stakeholders.

★Must know

  1. Object and structure: the IBC consolidates laws on insolvency of corporate persons, partnership firms and individuals, in a time-bound manner, to maximise value of assets, promote entrepreneurship, availability of credit and balance the interests of all stakeholders. Regulator: the Insolvency and Bankruptcy Board of India (IBBI). Adjudicating authority: NCLT for corporate persons; Debt Recovery Tribunal for individuals and partnership firms. Information Utilities (IUs) store financial information to establish default. Insolvency Professionals (IPs) conduct the process. The Code has a non-obstante clause overriding other laws (s.238).
  2. Initiation of CIRP: by a financial creditor (s.7), by an operational creditor after a demand notice (s.8-9), or by the corporate applicant (s.10), on default of at least Rs 1 crore. Financial creditor: a person to whom a financial debt is owed (disbursed against consideration for the time value of money), including allottees of real estate projects (explanation to s.5(8)(f)); the 2020 amendment to s.7 requires a minimum of 100 allottees or 10% of allottees in the same real estate project, whichever is less, to file a joint application; operational creditor: a person to whom an operational debt (claim arising from provision of goods or services, including employment or a debt in respect of the repayment of dues arising under any law, payable to the Central Government, any State Government or any local authority) is owed. Demand notice (s.8) gives 10 days to the debtor to repay or to bring notice of a dispute; s.9 application is filed on failure; the NCLT must admit or reject within 14 days of the application.
  3. Admission and consequences: for a s.7 application, the NCLT must be satisfied that a default has occurred and the application is complete; the NCLT gives the debtor an opportunity to be heard (Innoventive: once default and completeness are shown, the application must be admitted; Vidarbha Industries Power Ltd v Axis Bank Ltd (2022): admission is not automatic, the NCLT has discretion to consider whether the debtor is viable). Upon admission: moratorium (s.14) prohibits institution or continuation of suits or proceedings against the corporate debtor, transferring, encumbering or alienating its assets, any action to foreclose, recover or enforce any security interest, and recovery of property by an owner or lessor; supply of essential goods and services cannot be terminated; Interim Resolution Professional (IRP) takes over the management; the board of directors is suspended; public announcement inviting claims (s.15); the Committee of Creditors (CoC) is constituted of financial creditors (s.21) with voting shares in proportion to the financial debt; operational creditors do not have voting rights but may attend.
  4. Timeline: CIRP must be completed within 180 days, extendable by 90 days by the NCLT (s.12), with a maximum of 330 days including litigation and extension (inserted in 2019); Essar Steel (2019) read the 330-day outer limit as directory in exceptional circumstances; the 2019 amendment again inserted the 330-day limit with a time for exclusion of litigation delays Verify. Resolution plan (s.30): submitted by a resolution applicant; must provide for payment of CIRP costs in priority, payment to operational creditors (not less than the amount receivable in the liquidation), repayment to financial creditors who dissent (not less than the liquidation value); approved by the CoC by a vote of not less than 66% of the voting share (reduced from 75% in 2019) and then approved by the NCLT (s.31); it binds the corporate debtor, its employees, members, creditors, guarantors and other stakeholders (Ghanashyam Mishra and Sons Pvt Ltd v Edelweiss Asset Reconstruction Co (2021): a successful resolution plan extinguishes all claims not part of the plan: the 'clean slate' principle). Withdrawal after admission (s.12A): permitted with the approval of 90% voting share of the CoC (Uttara Foods and Feeds v Mona Pharmacem, 2019; Brilliant Alloys v Mahadeo Prasad Chhawsaria (2018): not the NCLT's discretion alone).
  5. Section 29A (ineligibility of resolution applicants): persons who are undischarged insolvents, wilful defaulters, persons whose account has been classified as an NPA for one year or more (unless they pay off all overdue amounts), persons convicted for offences punishable with imprisonment for two years or more (except for certain offences), persons disqualified as directors, persons prohibited by SEBI, persons who have executed a guarantee for a corporate debtor under CIRP or liquidation where the guarantee has been invoked and remains unpaid, or related parties of any such persons. ArcelorMittal India Pvt Ltd v Satish Kumar Gupta (2019): the 'connected person' and the date for determining NPA; Chitra Sharma v Union of India (2018): Jaypee Infratech homebuyers' rights and s.29A's purposes.
  6. Waterfall under liquidation (s.53): (1) insolvency resolution process costs and liquidation costs; (2) workmen's dues for the 24 months preceding the liquidation commencement date and debts owed to secured creditors who have relinquished their security (pari passu); (3) wages and unpaid dues to employees other than workmen for the 12 months preceding; (4) financial debts owed to unsecured creditors; (5) amount due to the Central and State Governments for the 2 years preceding the liquidation commencement date and debts owed to secured creditors for any amount unpaid following enforcement of security interest; (6) remaining debts and dues; (7) preference shareholders; (8) equity shareholders or partners. The Supreme Court in Essar Steel (2019) held that the CoC has the commercial wisdom to distribute proceeds among creditors in a resolution plan and that the equality principle does not require equal treatment of unequals (financial and operational creditors); the decision protected the secured creditors' priority.
  7. Avoidance transactions (ss.43-51, 66): the resolution professional or liquidator can apply to the NCLT to avoid: preferential transactions (within 2 years if made to related parties, within 1 year otherwise, before the insolvency commencement date), undervalued transactions (same look-back periods), extortionate credit transactions (within 2 years), and fraudulent or wrongful trading (s.66): where the business of the corporate debtor has been carried on with intent to defraud creditors or for any fraudulent purpose, the persons who were knowingly parties may be made liable to contribute to the assets. Anuj Jain, Interim Resolution Professional for Jaypee Infratech Ltd v Axis Bank Ltd (2020): the Court upheld the avoidance of mortgage transactions entered into by Jaypee Infratech in favour of lenders for the benefit of its holding company, as undervalued/preferential transactions.
  8. Landmark decisions: Mobilox Innovations Pvt Ltd v Kirusa Software (2017): the test for pre-existing dispute: whether there is a plausible contention requiring further investigation, which is not patently feeble; Macquarie Bank Ltd v Shilpi Cable Technologies (2017): the operational creditor's demand notice must be served in the prescribed form, and the dispute may be raised in response; Swiss Ribbons Pvt Ltd v UoI (2019): upheld the constitutionality of IBC, including the differential treatment of financial and operational creditors, s.29A and the CoC's role; K. Sashidhar v Indian Overseas Bank (2019): CoC's commercial wisdom is non-justiciable, including a decision on liquidation vs resolution; Committee of Creditors of Essar Steel India Ltd v Satish Kumar Gupta (2019): CoC decision on distribution is final; Jaypee Kensington (2021): protection of the interests of homebuyers; B.K. Educational Services v Parag Gupta (2018): the Limitation Act, 1963 applies to IBC applications (3 years from the date of default; Article 137); Gujarat Urja Vikas Nigam Ltd v Amit Gupta (2021): the NCLT cannot adjudicate on the termination of a contract by the counterparty under the contract on the ground of insolvency unless the contract is of the type central to the corporate debtor's survival (Section 60(5)); Lalit Kumar Jain v UoI (2021): the personal guarantors to corporate debtors can be proceeded against even after approval of the resolution plan; Dena Bank v C. Shivakumar Reddy (2021): the application of Article 137; Kotak Mahindra Bank v A. Balakrishnan (2022): the acknowledgement in the balance sheet extends limitation.
  9. Pre-packaged insolvency (Chapter III-A, inserted in April 2021): available only to MSMEs, with a threshold of default of Rs 10 lakh, initiated by the corporate debtor with approval of 66% of the financial creditors that are not related parties, and with a time limit of 120 days for the process; the existing management stays in control (debtor-in-possession model). Fast-track CIRP: for corporate debtors with assets or income below Rs 1 crore, startups and unlisted companies, with 90 days (extendable by 45 days). Group insolvency and cross-border insolvency are under discussion; the Insolvency and Bankruptcy Code (Amendment) Bill, 2025 (if passed) introduces creditor-initiated insolvency resolution and group insolvency frameworks Verify · status.
  10. Personal insolvency (Part III): fresh start process (for debts up to Rs 35,000, as notified), insolvency resolution process for individuals and firms (Part III, Chapter III) and bankruptcy (Chapter IV); personal guarantors to corporate debtors fall under the DRT jurisdiction from 1 December 2019 notification, upheld in Lalit Kumar Jain. Liquidation of the corporate debtor: the liquidator is appointed (s.34), and the liquidation process must be completed within two years (regulation 44 of the IBBI Liquidation Regulations); sale of the corporate debtor as a going concern is encouraged. Offences under the Code: concealment of property (s.69), transactions defrauding creditors (s.69), misconduct in the course of insolvency (s.70), falsification of books (s.71), false representations to creditors (s.73), contravention of moratorium (s.74), punished with imprisonment from 3 to 5 years and fines Verify.

⚖Landmark cases

Innoventive Industries v ICICI Bank

2017

Default is the sole trigger for CIRP under s.7; the NCLT must admit where default and completeness are shown.

Mobilox Innovations v Kirusa Software

2017

A pre-existing dispute that is not spurious or hypothetical bars an operational creditor's application.

Swiss Ribbons v Union of India

2019

IBC constitutionally valid; financial and operational creditors are different classes; s.29A is valid.

K. Sashidhar v Indian Overseas Bank

2019

CoC's commercial wisdom is paramount; the NCLT cannot examine the merits of the CoC's decision.

Committee of Creditors of Essar Steel v Satish Kumar Gupta

2019

Distribution under the resolution plan is a matter of CoC's commercial wisdom; secured creditors' priority protected.

B.K. Educational Services v Parag Gupta

2018

Limitation Act applies to applications under ss.7 and 9; three years from default.

Ghanashyam Mishra & Sons v Edelweiss ARC

2021

A resolution plan extinguishes all claims not included; clean slate for the successful applicant.

Anuj Jain v Axis Bank

2020

Undervalued and preferential transactions of the corporate debtor can be avoided; ss.43-51.

Lalit Kumar Jain v Union of India

2021

Personal guarantors can be proceeded against under IBC; the resolution plan does not discharge them.

Gujarat Urja Vikas Nigam v Amit Gupta

2021

NCLT cannot go into contractual disputes arising from termination of a contract unrelated to insolvency.

Vidarbha Industries Power v Axis Bank

2022

Admission under s.7 is not mandatory even on default; the Adjudicating Authority has discretion.

!Exam traps

  • TrapThreshold of default: Rs 1 crore. Financial creditors have voting rights in the CoC; operational creditors do not.
  • TrapCoC approval: 66% of voting share for a resolution plan; 90% for withdrawal under s.12A.
  • Trap180 + 90 days, outer limit of 330 days. Fast track: 90 days (+45).
  • TrapIBC overrides other laws through s.238; the Limitation Act applies.
