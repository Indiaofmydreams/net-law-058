iOverview

Lifting the veil, statutory grounds: reducing members below statutory minimum (s.45 repealed

★Must know

  1. Lifting the veil, statutory grounds: reducing members below statutory minimum (s.45 repealed; now in s.3), misdescription of the name, fraudulent trading (s.339), improper use of the name (s.12), liability for ultra vires prospectus misstatement, and holding-subsidiary consolidation. Judicial grounds: fraud or improper conduct (Gilford Motor Co v Horne, 1933; Jones v Lipman, 1962), sham or façade, determination of enemy character in wartime (Daimler Co v Continental Tyre, 1916), tax evasion (Juggilal Kamlapat v CIT, 1969), group enterprises (DHN Food Distributors v Tower Hamlets), and agency. Indian cases: State of UP v Renusagar Power Co (1988); Life Insurance Corporation of India v Escorts Ltd (1986): the veil may be lifted in the interest of justice and the public.
  2. Doctrine of ultra vires: acts beyond the objects clause of the MoA are void and cannot be ratified even by all members. Ashbury Railway Carriage & Iron Co v Riche (1875). Acts within the powers of the company but beyond the powers of directors (intra vires the company) can be ratified. Acts beyond the AoA but within the MoA may be ratified.
  3. Doctrine of constructive notice: every person dealing with a company is deemed to have read the MoA and AoA and the documents filed with the Registrar (Ernest v Nicholls). Doctrine of indoor management (rule in Royal British Bank v Turquand, 1856): outsiders dealing with a company in good faith may assume the internal procedures have been complied with. Exceptions: actual knowledge of irregularity; suspicion should have put one on inquiry (Anand Bihari Lal v Dinshaw & Co); forgery (Ruben v Great Fingall Consolidated, 1906); acts outside the apparent authority.
  4. Doctrine of majority rule and minority protection: Foss v Harbottle (1843): the proper plaintiff for a wrong done to the company is the company itself; courts will not interfere in matters of internal management where the majority can ratify. Exceptions: ultra vires or illegal acts; fraud on the minority; acts requiring a special majority passed by simple majority; personal rights of members; just and equitable grounds. Statutory protection: oppression and mismanagement (ss.241-246), class action suits (s.245), and the right to apply for winding up.
  5. Oppression and mismanagement (ss.241-242): any member may apply to the NCLT if the affairs of the company are being conducted in a manner prejudicial to public interest, oppressive to members, or prejudicial to the interests of the company; the facts must justify winding up on just and equitable grounds but winding up would unfairly prejudice the members. Eligibility to apply (s.244): for a company with share capital, at least 100 members or one-tenth of the total number of members, whichever is less, or members holding at least one-tenth of the issued share capital; the NCLT may waive these requirements. Powers: regulate affairs, buy-out, restrict transfer, set aside transactions, appoint directors.
  6. Cyrus Investments Ltd v Tata Sons Ltd (2021): the Supreme Court set aside the NCLAT order restoring Cyrus Mistry; the removal of a director was valid; the 'oppression' standard was not met; the AoA's Articles 121 and 104 (conversion of public to private) were respected; reiterated that NCLT/NCLAT cannot rewrite company arrangements and that the test for oppression is 'burdensome, harsh and wrongful' conduct with a continuing course of acts. Also Needle Industries v Needle Industries Newey (1981).
  7. Class action suit (s.245): members or depositors may file on behalf of the class against the company or its directors or auditors for acts prejudicial to interests; at least 100 members or 10% of the total members (or depositors) as the case may be.

⚖Landmark cases

Foss v Harbottle

1843

Proper plaintiff rule and majority rule; exceptions protect minorities.

Royal British Bank v Turquand

1856

Indoor management: outsiders can assume compliance with internal procedures.

Ashbury Railway Carriage v Riche

1875

Ultra vires acts are void and incapable of ratification.

Gilford Motor Co v Horne

1933

The company was a sham to evade a covenant; veil lifted.

Cyrus Investments Ltd v Tata Sons Ltd

2021

Supreme Court reversed NCLAT; limits on NCLT/NCLAT interference in oppression and mismanagement cases.

LIC v Escorts Ltd

1986

Corporate veil may be lifted where justice and public interest require; the Court recognised exceptions.

State of UP v Renusagar Power Co

1988

Court may lift the veil to see the economic reality, particularly in tax and welfare contexts.

!Exam traps

  • TrapUltra vires acts cannot be ratified even by unanimous vote; irregular acts of directors that are intra vires the company can.
  • TrapIndoor management does NOT protect an outsider with actual or constructive suspicion or in cases of forgery.
  • TrapFoss v Harbottle: the company is the proper plaintiff; the individual shareholder cannot sue for corporate wrongs, subject to exceptions.
