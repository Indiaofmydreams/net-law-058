iOverview

Directors (ss.149-172): minimum 3 for a public company, 2 for a private company, 1 for an OPC

★Must know

  1. Directors (ss.149-172): minimum 3 for a public company, 2 for a private company, 1 for an OPC; maximum 15, extendable by special resolution; at least one woman director for prescribed classes of companies (listed companies and public companies with paid-up capital of Rs 100 crore or turnover Rs 300 crore); at least one director who stayed in India for not less than 182 days in the previous year (resident director); DIN is mandatory (s.153-154). Independent directors (s.149(6)): one-third of the Board for listed companies; tenure of 5 years renewable once by special resolution, then a cooling period of 3 years; they cannot receive stock options.
  2. Disqualifications (s.164): unsound mind, undischarged insolvent, conviction for an offence involving moral turpitude with imprisonment of at least 6 months (5-year bar after release), disqualification by court order, failure to file financial statements or annual returns for 3 consecutive years, failure to repay deposits or debentures; Vacation of office (s.167). Removal of directors (s.169): by ordinary resolution with special notice; the director may make representations. Additional directors, nominee directors, alternate directors, and directors appointed in casual vacancy (s.161). Retirement by rotation (s.152): two-thirds of directors are subject to rotation and one-third retire every year.
  3. Key Managerial Personnel (s.203): CEO/MD/manager, company secretary, whole-time director and CFO (applies to listed companies and public companies with paid-up capital Rs 10 crore or more).
  4. Board meetings (s.173): at least 4 meetings a year with a gap of not more than 120 days between two consecutive meetings; first meeting within 30 days of incorporation; quorum: one-third of total strength or 2 directors, whichever is higher (s.174); notice of at least 7 days. Annual General Meeting (s.96): within 6 months of the financial year-end and not more than 15 months between two AGMs; first AGM within 9 months of the end of the first financial year; Registrar may extend by up to 3 months. EGM (s.100): requisition by members holding at least one-tenth of paid-up capital with voting rights. Quorum for general meetings (s.103): 5 members for public companies with up to 1,000 members, 15 for 1,000 to 5,000, and 30 for over 5,000; 2 members for private companies. Notice of at least 21 clear days (short notice with consent of 95% of members).
  5. Resolutions: ordinary (simple majority of votes cast) and special (not less than 75% of votes cast; s.114). Matters requiring special resolution: alteration of AoA, change of name, alteration of objects, reduction of share capital, buy-back above 10%, appointment of a director beyond 15, etc. Postal ballot and e-voting are mandatory for listed companies for specified matters.
  6. Related party transactions (s.188) need Board approval and, above prescribed thresholds, prior approval of the shareholders by ordinary resolution (after the 2017 amendment), with the related party abstaining from voting. Loans to directors (s.185): prohibited except in specified exceptions; with special resolution loans to entities in which directors are interested may be made for principal business Verify · post-2017 amendments. Loans and investments by a company (s.186): up to 60% of paid-up capital, free reserves and securities premium, or 100% of free reserves and securities premium, whichever is higher; beyond that, special resolution.
  7. Audit (ss.139-148): auditor appointed for a term of 5 years (renewable once for individuals up to 5 years; firms up to 10 years in two terms of 5 years); rotation after the term; cooling-off of 5 years; Cost audit for prescribed companies; Auditor's liability for misstatement (s.147). Section 143: powers and duties; reporting of fraud to the Central Government if above Rs 1 crore (and to the Audit Committee or Board if below Rs 1 crore). Removal of auditor: an auditor cannot be removed before the expiry of the term except by special resolution and Central Government approval (s.140). National Financial Reporting Authority (NFRA) is the audit regulator for listed and large companies.
  8. CSR (s.135): applicable to companies with net worth of Rs 500 crore or more, or turnover of Rs 1,000 crore or more, or net profit of Rs 5 crore or more in any financial year; the Board must ensure spending of at least 2% of the average net profit of the immediately preceding three financial years; CSR Committee with at least 3 directors, including one independent director (not required for private companies with 2 directors); unspent amount to be transferred to an Unspent CSR Account (ongoing projects) or to a fund under Schedule VII (within 6 months of the end of the financial year) Verify · current wording. The Companies (CSR Policy) Rules, 2014 were amended in 2021 and decriminalised partly (penal consequences are civil under the 2019/2020 amendments).
  9. Fraud (s.447): any act, omission, concealment or abuse of position with intent to deceive, gain undue advantage or injure the interests of the company, shareholders, creditors or any other person, whether or not there is wrongful gain or loss. Punishment: imprisonment for 6 months to 10 years and fine of not less than the amount involved in the fraud, extending to 3 times that amount; if fraud involves public interest, minimum imprisonment is 3 years. The Serious Fraud Investigation Office (SFIO, s.211) investigates. Bail for s.447 offences is subject to the twin conditions of s.212(6); Nikesh Tarachand Shah (2017) struck down the similar PMLA condition and the legislature later amended s.212(6) Verify · current position.
  10. Decriminalisation: Companies (Amendment) Act 2019 and 2020 moved many compoundable offences to in-house adjudication; the Jan Vishwas (Amendment of Provisions) Act 2023 amended certain provisions; the Jan Vishwas (Amendment of Provisions) Bill, 2025 proposes further decriminalisation of minor offences across various Acts Verify · status.
  11. Tribunals: National Company Law Tribunal (NCLT; s.408) and NCLAT (s.410) constituted in 2016; appeals lie from the NCLT to the NCLAT within 45 days (s.421) and from the NCLAT to the Supreme Court within 60 days (s.423). Registrar of Companies (s.609 etc.) maintains the register; striking off defunct companies under s.248; shell company crackdown. Winding up of companies is by the NCLT (s.271): grounds include inability to pay debts, special resolution, acts against the sovereignty, integrity or security of the State, fraudulent conduct, default in filing financial statements for 5 consecutive years, and just and equitable grounds. Voluntary winding up is under the Insolvency and Bankruptcy Code, 2016, s.59.
  12. Insolvency and Bankruptcy Code, 2016 (IBC): Corporate Insolvency Resolution Process (CIRP) can be initiated by a financial creditor (s.7), an operational creditor (s.9) or the corporate debtor itself (s.10) upon a default of at least Rs 1 crore (threshold raised from Rs 1 lakh in March 2020). Moratorium under s.14; interim resolution professional (IRP); Committee of Creditors (CoC) decides with 66% voting share; CIRP time limit 180 days, extendable by 90 days, with an overall outer limit of 330 days including litigation (Essar Steel (2019) read the outer limit as directory in exceptional cases; Ghanashyam Mishra and Sons v Edelweiss ARC (2021): once approved, the resolution plan binds all stakeholders including the Government and statutory dues of which no claim was filed); Section 29A bars ineligible persons (wilful defaulters, non-performing assets holders) from submitting resolution plans. Operational creditor trigger: Mobilox Innovations v Kirusa Software (2017): the existence of a pre-existing dispute defeats the s.9 application; Innoventive Industries v ICICI Bank (2017): existence of default is the key for the financial creditor; Swiss Ribbons v Union of India (2019): IBC held constitutional; Essar Steel India Ltd v Satish Kumar Gupta (2019): the commercial wisdom of the CoC is non-justiciable; the equality principle between creditors; Jaypee Kensington Boulevard Apartments Welfare Association v NBCC (2021): homebuyers are financial creditors; Pioneer Urban Land v UoI (2019). Pre-packaged insolvency (PPIRP) for MSMEs from 2021; Liquidation process under Chapter III; Personal guarantors: Lalit Kumar Jain v UoI (2021) upheld the notification extending IBC to personal guarantors.

⚖Landmark cases

Swiss Ribbons Pvt Ltd v Union of India

2019

IBC upheld; classification between financial and operational creditors is valid.

Mobilox Innovations v Kirusa Software

2017

Pre-existing dispute test for operational creditors.

Innoventive Industries v ICICI Bank

2017

On default, the adjudicating authority must admit the s.7 application.

Essar Steel India v Satish Kumar Gupta

2019

CoC's commercial wisdom is paramount and not subject to judicial review on merits.

Jaypee Kensington Boulevard Apartments Welfare Association v NBCC

2021

Homebuyers are financial creditors; balance of interests.

Ghanashyam Mishra & Sons v Edelweiss ARC

2021

Approved resolution plan extinguishes all claims not part of the plan; clean slate principle.

Lalit Kumar Jain v Union of India

2021

Personal guarantors to corporate debtors are brought within IBC.

!Exam traps

  • TrapIBC minimum default threshold is Rs 1 crore (not Rs 1 lakh, which was the pre-2020 figure).
  • TrapCIRP: 180 days + 90 days extension; 330-day outer limit.
  • TrapAGM: within 6 months of year-end; max gap 15 months between AGMs.
  • TrapBoard meetings: 4 per year, maximum gap 120 days.
  • TrapSpecial resolution requires at least 75% of votes cast in favour.
