Nature, Incorporation and Types of Companies (Companies Act, 2013)

iOverview

A company is an artificial juristic person with perpetual succession, a common seal (optional since 2015), the power to sue and be sued in its own name, and separate legal existence.

★Must know

  1. A company is an artificial juristic person with perpetual succession, a common seal (optional since 2015), the power to sue and be sued in its own name, and separate legal existence. Salomon v Salomon & Co (1897): a duly incorporated company is a separate legal entity distinct from its members, even a one-man company.
  2. Section 2(20) 'company' means a company incorporated under the 2013 Act or any previous company law. Section 3: a company may be formed for any lawful purpose by 7 or more persons (public), 2 or more (private), or 1 person (One Person Company).
  3. Private company (s.2(68)): restricts the right to transfer shares, limits members to 200 (excluding employees and ex-employees), prohibits invitation to the public to subscribe for securities; minimum paid-up capital requirement removed in 2015. Public company (s.2(71)): not a private company; minimum 7 members; no maximum; minimum 3 directors. (The earlier rule deeming a private company that is a subsidiary of a public company to be a public company was removed in 2015.)
  4. One Person Company (s.2(62)): single member; nominee required; OPC can be a private company; threshold for mandatory conversion (paid-up capital Rs 50 lakh or turnover Rs 2 crore) was removed by the 2021 amendment rules, which also allow NRIs to form OPCs and cut the residency requirement to 120 days.
  5. Small company (s.2(85)): paid-up capital up to Rs 4 crore and turnover up to Rs 40 crore originally; thresholds were later raised (Rs 10 crore and Rs 100 crore) Verify; exemption from certain filings. Section 8 company: for promoting charitable objects (commerce, art, science, sports, education, research, social welfare, religion, charity, protection of environment), with licence from the Central Government, income applied only to objects, no dividend. Producer company (Part IXA). Government company (s.2(45)): at least 51% paid-up share capital held by Central or State governments. Holding and subsidiary (s.2(46), 2(87)). Foreign company (s.2(42)). Dormant company (s.455). Listed company (s.2(52)).
  6. Incorporation process: name approval (RUN, SPICe+ forms), Memorandum of Association (MoA; s.4), Articles of Association (AoA; s.5), declaration of compliance, DIN for directors, filing with the Registrar of Companies (ROC), and a Certificate of Incorporation (s.7), which is conclusive evidence of due incorporation. Company comes into existence from the date of incorporation mentioned in the certificate.
  7. MoA clauses (public): name, registered office (state), objects, liability of members, authorised capital, and association clause (subscribers). AoA contains regulations for internal management; Table F is the model for companies limited by shares; the AoA is subordinate to the MoA and the Act; AoA can be altered by a special resolution (s.14), but cannot go beyond the MoA or the Act. Alteration of the name clause needs a special resolution and Central Government approval where applicable; objects clause by special resolution (and postal ballot for certain changes).
  8. Promoters (s.2(69)): a person named in the prospectus or annual return, or who has control over affairs of the company, or on whose advice the Board is accustomed to act (professionals excluded). Promoters stand in a fiduciary position to the company: Erlanger v New Sombrero Phosphate Co (1878); Gluckstein v Barnes (1900): secret profits must be disclosed.
  9. Prospectus (s.2(70), ss.26-40): any document described or issued as a prospectus including a red herring prospectus, shelf prospectus or any notice, circular, advertisement inviting offers from the public. Misstatement in a prospectus attracts civil liability (s.35) and criminal liability (s.34: fraud under s.447). Only public companies may issue prospectuses; private placement (s.42) cannot be offered to more than 200 persons in a financial year (excluding qualified institutional buyers and employees under ESOPs).
  10. Share capital: equity and preference shares (s.43); issue at premium or discount (discount prohibited except sweat equity: s.53); allotment, calls, forfeiture and lien; buy-back (s.68): up to 25% of paid-up capital and free reserves, and not more than 25% of the total paid-up equity in a year; board resolution up to 10%; special resolution above that; debt-equity ratio 2:1 post buy-back; no re-issue of bought-back shares for 6 months Verify. Dematerialisation compulsory for listed companies; transfer of shares of private companies per AoA.
  11. Dividend (s.123): paid out of profits of the year or accumulated profits after depreciation, and transfer to reserves; interim dividend allowed; unpaid dividend to be transferred to the Unpaid Dividend Account within 7 days of the 30-day period, and to the Investor Education and Protection Fund (IEPF) after 7 years (s.124-125).

⚖Landmark cases

Salomon v Salomon & Co Ltd

1897

Separate corporate personality; the company is not an agent or trustee of its members.

Lee v Lee's Air Farming Ltd

1960

A sole shareholder-director can also be an employee of his own company.

Macaura v Northern Assurance Co

1925

A shareholder has no insurable interest in the property of the company.

Tata Engineering and Locomotive Co v State of Bihar

1964

Company is a juristic person separate from its shareholders; veil lifted only in exceptional cases.

Erlanger v New Sombrero Phosphate Co

1878

Promoters owe a fiduciary duty and cannot make secret profits.
