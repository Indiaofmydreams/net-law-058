iOverview

The map of dispute-resolution methods and the statutory institutions around them. Know who decides, whether the outcome is binding, and which statute governs.

★Must know

  1. ADR methods: (1) Negotiation - direct, voluntary; no third party. (2) Mediation - neutral facilitator helps parties reach a voluntary settlement; mediator does not decide. (3) Conciliation - conciliator may propose settlement terms (A&C Act Part III). (4) Arbitration - private adjudication by a tribunal chosen by parties; award binding. (5) Lok Adalat - statutory people's court; settlement by compromise; award is a civil-court decree. (6) Judicial settlement, med-arb, early neutral evaluation, mini-trial, Online Dispute Resolution (ODR). Benefits: speed, cost, confidentiality, preserving relationships, reducing docket; constitutional anchor: Art 39A (equal justice and free legal aid) and Art 21 (speedy justice).
  2. Section 89 CPC and Order X Rules 1A, 1B, 1C: where it appears to the court that there exist elements of a settlement which may be acceptable to the parties, the court shall formulate the terms of settlement and refer the same for (a) arbitration; (b) conciliation; (c) judicial settlement including settlement through Lok Adalat; or (d) mediation. Salem Advocate Bar Association v. Union of India (2005): validity upheld; court to follow Rules; Afcons Infrastructure Ltd. v. Cherian Varkey Construction Co. (2010): the court cannot refer parties to arbitration without the consent of both sides (arbitration needs agreement); mandatory referral to Lok Adalat, conciliation, mediation allowed without consent as steps; categories of cases generally unsuitable for ADR: representative suits (Order I Rule 8), election disputes, grant of probate/letters of administration, cases involving public interest and serious allegations of fraud, cases against minors/persons of unsound mind, prosecution for criminal offences; suited: relating to trade, commerce, contracts, specific performance, motor accident claims (some), partition, matrimonial, family disputes, consumer, banking etc. Order XXIII Rule 3 CPC: compromise decree. Order XXXIIA: family matters - conciliation.
  3. Legal Services Authorities Act 1987 (in force 9 November 1995; 'National Legal Services Day'): Art 39A. NALSA (apex body; Chief Justice of India is Patron-in-Chief; an executive chairman who is a serving/retired Supreme Court judge); State Legal Services Authority (Chief Justice of the High Court is Patron-in-Chief), District Legal Services Authority (District Judge is Chairman), Taluk Legal Services Committee, High Court Legal Services Committee, Supreme Court Legal Services Committee. Persons entitled to free legal services (s.12): members of Scheduled Castes/Tribes; victim of trafficking or begar; women and children; persons with disabilities; persons in circumstances of undeserved want such as victims of mass disaster, ethnic violence, caste atrocity, flood, drought, earthquake or industrial disaster; industrial workmen; persons in custody (including protective home, juvenile home, psychiatric hospital); persons whose annual income is below the limit fixed by the Government (for Supreme Court cases, Rs 5 lakh as notified).
  4. Lok Adalat (Chapter VI, ss.19-22 LSA Act): organised by the Authorities at periodic intervals; consists of serving or retired judicial officers and other persons as prescribed; jurisdiction over (i) any case pending before any court; or (ii) any matter falling within the jurisdiction of any court and not brought before it (pre-litigation) - but no jurisdiction over any offence not compoundable under the law (s.19(5)). Cases are referred if (i) the parties agree, or one party applies and the court is prima facie satisfied that there are chances of settlement (s.20); the Lok Adalat shall proceed in a summary manner guided by principles of justice, equity, fair play and other principles (s.20(4)); where no compromise is reached the record is returned to the court for disposal in accordance with law (s.20(5)). Award (s.21): deemed a decree of a civil court; final and binding on all parties; no appeal lies; court fee paid is refunded under the Court Fees Act. Powers (s.22): same as a civil court for summoning and examining witnesses, discovery and production of documents, receiving evidence on affidavits, requisitioning public records; proceedings are judicial proceedings for ss.193, 219, 228 IPC (BNS equivalents) and a civil court for s.195 CrPC (s.215 BNSS). National Lok Adalats are held on specified dates; special Lok Adalats for specific subjects (e.g., motor accident claims, bank recovery, electricity, matrimonial).
  5. Lok Adalat jurisprudence: State of Punjab v. Jalour Singh (2008) - a Lok Adalat has no adjudicatory role; it can only help parties arrive at a compromise by consent; an award made without consent is a nullity; P.T. Thomas v. Thomas Job (2005) - the Lok Adalat award has the finality of a decree and cannot be challenged except on very limited grounds; Bhargavi Constructions v. Kothakapu Muthyam Reddy (2018) - an award can be challenged only by a writ petition under Arts 226/227 on grounds such as fraud or coercion (no appeal; no civil suit); B.P. Moideen Sevamandir v. A.M. Kutty Hassan (2009) - the Lok Adalat is not a 'court' for deciding merits; K.N. Govindan Kutty Menon v. C.D. Shaji (2012): Lok Adalat award can be challenged only by writ.
  6. Permanent Lok Adalats (ss.22A-22E, inserted by the 2002 Amendment) - for public utility services: transport services for carriage of passengers or goods by air, road or water; postal, telegraph or telephone services; supply of power, light or water to the public by any establishment; public conservancy or sanitation; services in hospitals or dispensaries; insurance services; and (by notification) education or other public utility services. Composition: Chairman who is or has been a District Judge/Additional District Judge or has held higher judicial office, and two members with adequate experience in public utility services. Jurisdiction: pre-litigation only (a party must not have approached a court) over disputes involving public utility services where the value of the property in dispute does not exceed Rs 1 crore (raised from Rs 10 lakh by 2015 notification Verify); excludes non-compoundable offences. Procedure (s.22C): conciliation first; if no settlement the PLA has the power to decide the dispute on merits (unlike ordinary Lok Adalats); the award is final, binding and a decree (s.22E); no appeal. United India Insurance Co. Ltd. v. Ajay Sinha (2008): PLA's adjudicatory role upheld; the pre-litigation settlement process must be strictly followed.
  7. Gram Nyayalayas Act 2008 (in force 2 October 2009): village courts established at the intermediate Panchayat level by the State Government in consultation with the High Court; presided by a Nyayadhikari (a judicial officer with powers of a Judicial Magistrate First Class); mobile court sittings at village level; civil and criminal jurisdiction as per Schedules; emphasis on conciliation; simplified procedure; appeals - criminal: to the Court of Session (within 30 days; to be disposed within six months), civil: to the District Court (within 30 days; to be disposed within six months) Verify; number actually operational is limited Verify.
  8. Family Courts Act 1984: Family Courts established for settlement of matrimonial and family disputes; s.9 - duty of the court to make efforts for settlement and to assist and persuade parties; s.13 - no party has a right to be represented by a legal practitioner (court may appoint amicus curiae); s.10 - procedure; s.19 - appeal to the High Court within 30 days; mediation in matrimonial cases: K. Srinivas Rao v. D.A. Deepa (2013) - directions on mediation in cruelty cases (including s.498A IPC / s.85 BNS) and sending matters to mediation; Hindu Marriage Act s.23(2) - reconciliation. [Cross-ref: Family Law.]
  9. Commercial Courts Act 2015, s.12A (inserted 2018): a suit which does not contemplate any urgent interim relief shall not be instituted unless the plaintiff exhausts the remedy of pre-institution mediation in accordance with the Commercial Courts (Pre-Institution Mediation and Settlement) Rules 2018; commercial disputes of 'specified value' (Rs 3 lakh and above). Patil Automation Pvt. Ltd. v. Rakheja Engineers Pvt. Ltd. (2022): s.12A is mandatory; plaint is liable to be rejected under Order VII Rule 11(d) CPC; applied prospectively from 20 August 2022; Yamini Manohar v. T.K.D. Keerthi (2023): the court must assess whether 'urgent interim relief' is genuine and not a cover to avoid mediation. Mediation under the Rules must be completed within three months (extendable by two months with consent); settlement agreement has the same status as an arbitral award on agreed terms (s.12A(5)).
  10. Other ADR institutions and statutes: Consumer Protection Act 2019 - reference to mediation (s.37) and mediation cells (ss.74-81) Verify; Industrial Disputes Act/IR Code - conciliation officers and voluntary arbitration; Banking Ombudsman Scheme (now the Reserve Bank - Integrated Ombudsman Scheme 2021); Insurance Ombudsman Rules 2017; SEBI Online Dispute Resolution portal (2023); Online Dispute Resolution Policy Plan for India (NITI Aayog, 2021); MCA-21 'Company Law Settlement'; Panchayat Nyaya; Tribal customary dispute mechanisms; Supreme Court Mediation and Conciliation Project Committee (MCPC) - 'Mediation for the Nation' campaign launched in July 2025 for pending matters Verify.

§Key provisions

ProvisionWhat it says

⚖Landmark cases

Salem Advocate Bar Association v. Union of India

2005

Constitutional validity of s.89 CPC upheld; model rules for ADR and mediation.

Afcons Infrastructure Ltd. v. Cherian Varkey Construction Co.

2010

Reference to arbitration under s.89 requires consent of both parties; categories of cases suited and unsuited for ADR.

State of Punjab v. Jalour Singh

2008

Lok Adalat can only facilitate consent-based settlements; it cannot decide on merits.

Bhargavi Constructions v. Kothakapu Muthyam Reddy

2018

Lok Adalat award is final; challenge only by writ (Arts 226/227).

Patil Automation Pvt. Ltd. v. Rakheja Engineers Pvt. Ltd.

2022

Pre-institution mediation under s.12A Commercial Courts Act is mandatory.

K. Srinivas Rao v. D.A. Deepa

2013

Mediation in matrimonial disputes; guidelines.

United India Insurance Co. Ltd. v. Ajay Sinha

2008

Permanent Lok Adalat has adjudicatory power in pre-litigation public utility disputes.

✎Memory aids

  • Lok Adalat: 'FBD' - Final, Binding, Deemed decree; no appeal.
  • Permanent Lok Adalat: public utility + pre-litigation + up to Rs 1 crore + can decide on merits.

!Exam traps

  • TrapA Lok Adalat cannot decide on merits; a Permanent Lok Adalat can.
  • TrapAn award of a Lok Adalat is not appealable; the remedy is a writ.
  • TrapCourt-referral to arbitration under s.89 needs the consent of both parties.
  • TrapCommercial suits contemplating urgent interim relief can skip pre-institution mediation.

?Likely questions

  1. Which section of the CPC provides for settlement of disputes outside court? (s.89)
  2. Is a Lok Adalat award appealable? (No)
  3. Which Act established NALSA? (Legal Services Authorities Act 1987)
  4. Pre-institution mediation applies to which suits? (Commercial suits not contemplating urgent interim relief)
