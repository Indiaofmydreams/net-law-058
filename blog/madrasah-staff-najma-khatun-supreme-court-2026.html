Quick answer
  • On 13 July 2026 (Justices Dipankar Datta and Augustine George Masih) the Supreme Court dismissed 49 Article 32 petitions by 350+ madrasah staff in West Bengal.
  • Appointments after the Court's 14 March 2016 stay, without its express permission, were held illegal.
  • Defects: no advertisements, ad hoc committees, unqualified candidates, abuse of the 10% management quota.
  • No salary arrears; the Madrasah Service Commission may fill the posts afresh.

Public jobs are meant to be filled openly, on merit. When recruitment goes around that rule, the people hired are often the ones who suffer when the appointment is cancelled. The Supreme Court faced that hard problem in July 2026 and chose to enforce the rule. This guide explains the history, the reasoning and the constitutional principles involved.

What did the Supreme Court decide in the Najma Khatun case?

On 13 July 2026 Justices Dipankar Datta and Augustine George Masih dismissed 49 Article 32 petitions by more than 350 madrasah staff, holding that appointments made after the Court's stay of 14 March 2016 without its express permission were illegal.

The petitioners had been appointed to government-aided madrasahs and asked the Court to protect their jobs and pay. The Court refused. It treated an appointment made in the face of its own stay order as ex facie illegal, which means illegal on the face of it, and it held that sympathy for individuals cannot validate an appointment procured by disregarding the process.

What was the Madrasah Service Commission Act, 2008?

It created a Commission to recruit teachers and staff for madrasahs in West Bengal. The Calcutta High Court struck it down in 2014 and 2015, the Supreme Court stayed that on 14 March 2016 and upheld the Act in Sk. Mohd. Rafique (2020).

DateEvent
2008Madrasah Service Commission Act enacted
12 Mar 2014 and 9 Dec 2015Calcutta High Court strikes down the Act
14 Mar 2016Supreme Court stays the High Court's decisions

13 Jul 2026Najma Khatun: petitions dismissed

Once the High Court judgments were stayed, the question was what happened to appointments made while the stay was in force. The Court held that such appointments needed its express permission, and that those made without it could not be defended later just because the Act was eventually upheld.

Why were the appointments held illegal?

The Court found no advertisements, ad hoc selection committees, unqualified candidates and misuse of the 10 per cent management quota. It called the appointments a blot on the system.

These findings connect to Article 16(1), which guarantees equality of opportunity in public employment. Recruitment without an advertisement denies everyone else the chance to apply. The principle comes from Secretary, State of Karnataka v. Umadevi (2006), which said that appointments made outside the constitutional scheme are illegal and not merely irregular, and cannot be regularised as of right. The management quota was meant for limited use, and the Court saw it being used as a back door.

Will the staff get salary arrears?

No. The petitions were dismissed, interim orders were vacated and no arrears were awarded, but the Commission may fill the vacancies through a proper process.

Denying arrears reflects the idea that a person who works under an illegal appointment cannot claim the full benefits of a lawful one. Some Court decisions have given limited relief on equitable grounds, so this outcome is a choice and not a rule of law. The affected staff may apply when the Commission advertises the posts, subject to the eligibility conditions in the recruitment rules.

Does the ruling limit minority rights under Article 30?

Reports differ, so be careful. The safe reading is that the case decided the legality of recruitment to aided institutions and did not take away the right of minorities to establish and administer educational institutions.

Article 30(1) protects that right, but the Court has long held in cases such as T.M.A. Pai (2002) and P.A. Inamdar (2005) that aided institutions can be subject to regulation to ensure educational excellence and fair recruitment. One digest describes the judgment as saying there is no absolute right to non-competitive appointments at public expense. Treat that as a summary, not a quotation, and read the judgment before using it in a long answer. For another 2026 education ruling, see the Padam Mehta post.

How can this be asked in UGC NET Law, CLAT PG or AIBE?

Expect questions on Article 16, Umadevi, Article 30, aided institutions and the effect of stay orders.

  • Remember: 13 July 2026; Datta and Masih, JJ.; stay of 14 March 2016; 49 petitions; no arrears.
  • Link it to: Umadevi (2006), T.M.A. Pai (2002), Article 16(1) and the rule of law in public employment.
  • Practise: Unit 2, Unit 1 and previous year questions.
