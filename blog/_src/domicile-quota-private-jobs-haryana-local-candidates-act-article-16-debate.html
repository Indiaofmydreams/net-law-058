Quick answer
  • Haryana's 2020 Act reserved 75% of private jobs up to ₹30,000 a month for locals.
  • The Punjab and Haryana High Court struck it down in November 2023.
  • Constitutional hooks: Article 16(2) and (3), Article 19(1)(d), (e) and (g), Article 14.
  • Debate: local jobs versus one national market and freedom of enterprise.

Several States have tried to reserve private-sector jobs for their own residents. The idea is popular where unemployment is high, but it raises serious questions about the Constitution's promise of one national economy. This guide explains the Haryana law, the High Court's decision and the arguments for and against such quotas.

What does the Haryana law say?

The Haryana State Employment of Local Candidates Act, 2020 required private employers in the State to reserve 75 per cent of jobs paying up to a monthly salary of 30,000 rupees for local candidates, with a mechanism for exemptions and registration on a portal.

The Act applied to companies, societies, trusts and partnership firms with ten or more employees. Employers could seek exemption where suitable local candidates were not available, and the law provided for inspection and penalties. Other States, such as Andhra Pradesh in 2019, passed similar laws, and the same question arose for each.

What did the High Court decide?

In November 2023 a bench of the Punjab and Haryana High Court struck down the Act, reportedly holding that it violated the fundamental rights of employers and employees under Articles 14, 19 and 21 and that the State cannot create a barrier on the basis of residence in private employment.

The State appealed to the Supreme Court. As far as the sources used here show, the final outcome of that appeal has not been reported, so check the status. The case involves the line between regulating private employers and imposing a regional barrier.

Does the Constitution allow domicile reservation?

Article 16(2) bars discrimination in public employment on the basis of residence, but Article 16(3) lets Parliament prescribe a residence requirement for jobs in a State, a power used for Andhra Pradesh and Telangana. The Court has applied the rule strictly.

The Public Employment (Requirement as to Residence) Act, 1957 allowed residence requirements in some areas, but it lapsed in 1974 except for a few places. A State law cannot do what Article 16(3) reserves for Parliament, and the argument against State laws on private jobs is stronger still, since Article 16 does not directly apply to private employers but Article 19 and the unity of the economic area do.

What did Dr Pradeep Jain (1984) say?

The Supreme Court said that "son of the soil" policies are unconstitutional in principle, though some residence-based preference may be justified in admissions to medical colleges subject to limits.

In Dr. Pradeep Jain v. Union of India the Court warned that a growing sense of regional exclusion would endanger national unity, and it later applied the principle in cases such as Sunanda Reddy (1995) and Kailash Chand Sharma (2002). These cases concerned education and public jobs and are cited by both sides: supporters use the exceptions for disadvantage, and critics the central warning about parochialism.

What are the arguments on each side?

For: local youth need jobs, industries benefit from local infrastructure and tax breaks, and the State has a duty to its residents. Against: it breaks the national market, discourages investment, harms migrant workers, may invite retaliation and is probably unconstitutional.

Economists also point out that most migrant workers fill jobs that local residents may not seek, so a strict quota can raise costs. Supporters reply that quotas can be paired with skills training. This discussion links to the broader subject of labour policy in our post on the labour codes. A balanced answer notes that a State may support local employment through training and incentives without a hard quota.
