The NEET-UG 2026 paper leak became a legal story twice: first as an exam-fraud case, and then as a question about how far the State can go in policing student protest. This guide explains the Supreme Court's orders, the power it used and the limits it set.

Why did the Supreme Court quash FIRs against NEET protesters?

On 1 September 2026 a three-judge bench led by CJI Surya Kant used Article 142 to quash FIRs registered against students who protested on 20 July 2026, and barred fresh FIRs, to do complete justice in a matter arising from the NEET-UG paper leak.

Normally, quashing an FIR is a matter for the High Court under its inherent powers or Article 226, or for the Supreme Court in an appropriate proceeding. Here the Court acted in a case already before it, where hundreds of students faced criminal cases after a protest. The order stops a wave of prosecutions that could have damaged the careers of young people who were reacting to a failure of the examination system.

Who is excluded from the protection?

The Court allowed fresh FIRs against 2,873 people with serious criminal antecedents. The protection was meant for ordinary protesting students, not for those with a record of serious offences.

This carve-out shows how the Court balanced two concerns. It did not give blanket immunity to everyone present at the protest, and it left the police free to act against persons whose past record suggested more than student activism. The line is drawn by criminal antecedents, not by participation alone.

What is Article 142 of the Constitution?

Article 142(1) lets the Supreme Court pass any decree or order necessary for doing complete justice in a pending case. It is used where ordinary law offers no adequate remedy, but it cannot be used to override express statutory provisions.

PointPosition
Source of powerArticle 142(1)
ConditionA cause or matter pending before the Court
PurposeComplete justice between the parties
LimitCannot contradict express statutory provisions (Supreme Court Bar Association, 1998; Prem Chand Garg)

The Court has used Article 142 in many contexts, from dissolving marriages to directing investigations. Because it is wide, the Court itself has said it must be used with restraint, which is why this order mattered for students of constitutional law.

What did the Court order in Shailendra Mani Tripathi?

On 28 July 2026 the Court found a prima facie case for an independent investigation and gave interim directions to preserve CCTV and drone footage, protect protesters' data, bar coercive action and release children under 18.

These were protective steps taken early, before the 1 September quashing order. Preserving footage mattered because both sides needed evidence of what happened at the protest. The bar on coercive action and the release of minors reflect the Court's concern about juveniles in custody. On 16 September it dismissed, as premature, a plea to reconstitute the enquiry committee, leaving the investigation structure as it stood.

Is protest a fundamental right in India?

Peaceful assembly without arms is protected by Article 19(1)(b), subject to reasonable restrictions under Article 19(3). Protest that turns violent or breaks valid orders can attract criminal action, which is why courts examine facts case by case.

The right to protest is read together with the right to speech and expression under Article 19(1)(a). The State may impose restrictions in the interests of public order, and prohibitory orders under the Bharatiya Nagarik Suraksha Sanhita are a common example. Courts test whether restrictions are reasonable and proportionate. See how the exam-fraud law was tightened in our note on the Public Examinations amendment.
