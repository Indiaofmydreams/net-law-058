Quick answer
  • In a batch led by Ashwini Kumar Upadhyay v. Union of India (Justices Vikram Nath and Sandeep Mehta, reported 30 April 2026), the Supreme Court held that courts cannot create or expand criminal offences.
  • It declined to direct implementation of the Law Commission's 267th Report and refused continuing mandamus.
  • Criminal law is not silent; the problem is weak enforcement.
  • Remedies: police, magistrate under Section 156(3), complaint, and Lalita Kumari.

Every few months a speech sparks demands for a tougher hate speech law. In 2026 the Supreme Court ruled on a batch of 13 petitions that wanted exactly that. Its answer was not about whether hate speech is harmful. It was about who has the power to define crimes. This guide explains the decision in plain language and shows how it fits into the separation of powers.

What did the Supreme Court decide on hate speech in 2026?

On or around 30 April 2026 Justices Vikram Nath and Sandeep Mehta held that courts cannot create or expand criminal offences without legislative sanction, declined to direct implementation of the Law Commission's 267th report and found that existing law is adequate while enforcement is weak.

The petitioners relied on the Law Commission's 267th Report (March 2017) on hate speech and cited speeches during the COVID-19 pandemic that allegedly targeted religious minorities. The batch of 13 writ petitions sought implementation of the report, action against fake news and communally biased coverage, FIRs against hate speakers, independent investigative committees and enhanced sentences.

Can the Court order Parliament to make a hate speech law?

No. Relying on earlier decisions, the Court held that courts cannot compel the legislature to enact a particular law. The Vishaka exception for interim guidelines did not apply because the field is already occupied by statute.

In Vishaka (1997) the Court issued guidelines on sexual harassment at work because there was no law at all, and said they would hold until Parliament legislated. Here the Court found that the field was not empty. Provisions on promoting enmity between groups, outraging religious feelings, public mischief and related offences, along with allied laws, already form a framework. The problem, it said, lies in enforcement.

What remedies does a person have against hate speech?

A person can approach the Superintendent of Police, seek a magistrate's direction to investigate, or file a complaint before a magistrate. Following Lalita Kumari, an FIR must be registered where a cognizable offence is disclosed. Articles 32 and 226 remain extraordinary remedies.

StepRemedy
1Complaint to the police station; if refused, to the Superintendent of Police
2Application to the magistrate to direct investigation (Section 156(3) CrPC; equivalent BNSS provision)
3Private complaint before a magistrate
4Writ under Article 226 or Article 32, as an extraordinary remedy

The Court also clarified a procedural point: prior sanction under the provisions that need government approval applies only at the stage of cognizance, not when a magistrate orders an investigation. The Court said that, even without an express direction, registration of an FIR is implicit when a magistrate orders investigation of a cognizable offence.

Why did the Court refuse continuing mandamus?

It held that issuing continuing mandamus would amount to anticipating failures in statutory duty. The Court declined to supervise future enforcement on that basis.

The contrast with other 2026 cases is instructive. In the menstrual hygiene case the Court kept the matter alive to monitor compliance, because there was an identified policy failure. In this case it saw no basis to presume future default by authorities. The line is thin, and students should be ready to explain when continuing mandamus is appropriate.

What did the Court say about fraternity?

In a non-operative epilogue, the Court reflected on fraternity in the Preamble, Ambedkar's views, Article 51A and the idea of vasudhaiva kutumbakam, and framed hate speech as undermining fraternity.

The reflection does not create new rights or duties. It is a reminder that the Preamble lists fraternity assuring the dignity of the individual and the unity and integrity of the Nation. The Court also directed the Registry to send the judgment to all High Courts, which may consider practice directions, and left the Union and legislature free to consider the Law Commission's suggested amendments.

How can this be asked in UGC NET Law, CLAT PG or AIBE?

Expect questions on separation of powers, Vishaka, Lalita Kumari, Article 20(1) and the limits of judicial law-making.
