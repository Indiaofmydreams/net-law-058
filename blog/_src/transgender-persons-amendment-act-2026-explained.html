  • It sits uneasily with NALSA v. Union of India (2014), which recognised self-identified gender.
  • The Supreme Court issued notice on 4 May 2026, refused a stay and referred the matter to three judges.

Few 2026 laws raised as many constitutional questions as the amendment to the transgender rights law. It reopens a settled principle from 2014, namely that gender identity is for the individual to determine. This guide explains what the Act changes and what the Supreme Court has done so far.

What is the Transgender Persons (Protection of Rights) Amendment Act, 2026?

It is Act No. 3 of 2026, which received assent on 30 March 2026. It narrows the definition of a transgender person, requires medical board verification before a certificate is issued and adds new offences, amending the 2019 Act.

The Bill was introduced on 13 March, passed by the Lok Sabha on 24 March by voice vote amid an opposition walkout, passed by the Rajya Sabha on 25 March and received assent on 30 March. The speed of passage drew criticism, especially because the Bill was not sent to a committee.

How did the 2026 Act change the definition?

It focuses on socio-cultural identities such as kinner, hijra, aravani and jogta and some intersex persons, and removes categories such as trans men, trans women and genderqueer persons from the definition.

Is self-identification of gender still allowed?

The Act replaces self-identification with mandatory verification by a medical board before a District Magistrate issues the certificate. This is contrary to the self-perceived identity principle laid down in NALSA (2014), which is why it is being challenged.

In NALSA v. Union of India (2014) the Supreme Court recognised transgender persons as a "third gender" and held that the right to self-identify one's gender is part of dignity, autonomy and personal freedom under Articles 14, 15, 16, 19 and 21. Petitioners argue that medical verification reintroduces the stigma and bodily intrusion the Court rejected. The Union's defence is that verification prevents misuse of benefits.

What did the Supreme Court do on the challenge?

On 4 May 2026 the Court issued notice on nine petitions under Article 32, declined to stay the Act and referred the matter to a three-judge bench.

Refusing a stay means the law remains on the books while the challenge is heard. Courts are generally reluctant to stay a statute at the interim stage because laws carry a presumption of constitutionality. The final hearing will decide whether the narrower definition and verification regime pass Articles 14 and 21.

Is the Act in force?

The Act received assent on 30 March 2026, but reports indicate it had not yet been brought into force by notification. Check the Gazette for the commencement date before relying on any provision.

Assent and commencement are different things. A statute can be enacted but come into force on a date the Government notifies. Competitive exams like to test that distinction.
