Citizenship law is a sensitive and contested subject. This post explains the law and the arguments of each side without taking a position.

Citizenship laws decide who belongs in a country, so changes to them raise strong feelings. Two laws are central in India today: the Citizenship Amendment Act, 2019 and Section 6A of the Citizenship Act, which applies to Assam. This guide sets out what each does, what the Supreme Court has said and how both sides argue.

What does the Citizenship Amendment Act, 2019 do?

It lets Hindus, Sikhs, Buddhists, Jains, Parsis and Christians from Afghanistan, Bangladesh and Pakistan who entered India on or before 31 December 2014 apply for citizenship on a faster track, with the residence requirement cut from 11 years to five.

The Act also protects such applicants from being treated as "illegal migrants". It does not apply to areas under the Sixth Schedule of the Constitution or to States covered by the Inner Line permit system. It does not change the citizenship of existing citizens, a point the Government repeatedly stresses. Article 11 gives Parliament power to regulate citizenship by law.

When were the CAA Rules notified, and what is pending?

The Citizenship (Amendment) Rules, 2024 were notified on 11 March 2024, which allowed the Act to be implemented. The Supreme Court declined to stay the Rules at that stage, and the petitions challenging the Act remain pending.

What is Section 6A of the Citizenship Act?

It was inserted in 1985 to give effect to the Assam Accord. It treats migrants who entered Assam before 1 January 1966 as citizens and those who came between that date and 25 March 1971 as eligible to register, subject to conditions.

The Accord was signed after a six-year agitation against illegal migration into Assam. Under Section 6A those who entered after 25 March 1971, the date on which the Bangladesh war began, are treated as foreigners. The provision is specific to Assam, which the challengers said discriminates against Assam by requiring it alone to bear the burden.

What did the Supreme Court decide on Section 6A?

On 17 October 2024 a five-judge Constitution Bench upheld Section 6A by a 4 to 1 majority, with Justice J.B. Pardiwala dissenting, holding that it was a valid response to the Assam problem and that the 25 March 1971 cut-off was reasonable.

The majority, led by Justice Surya Kant and joined by Justices Sundaresh, Misra and CJI Chandrachud, said Parliament could choose different cut-off dates for different problems and that the Assam-specific law was not arbitrary. Justice Pardiwala dissented, saying that the provision had become unconstitutional with the passage of time. The National Register of Citizens exercise in Assam published its final list in 2019, which left out about 19 lakh applicants, and its implementation remains disputed.

What are the arguments on each side of the CAA debate?

Critics argue that religion-based classification violates Article 14 and secularism, as it excludes Muslims and other groups. Supporters argue that it protects religious minorities persecuted in three neighbouring countries and does not take away anyone's citizenship.

The legal question is whether the classification has a reasonable basis and a rational link with the aim, the test under Article 14 from cases such as State of West Bengal v. Anwar Ali Sarkar (1952). Defenders say that the three countries have state religions and that the persecuted are minorities there. Opponents ask why other persecuted groups or countries are left out. Because the case is pending, the Court has not decided it. See also our post on the Bihar SIR verdict for the related issue of proof of citizenship in electoral rolls.
