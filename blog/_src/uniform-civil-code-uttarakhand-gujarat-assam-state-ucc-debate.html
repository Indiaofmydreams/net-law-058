The Uniform Civil Code is a politically and religiously contested subject. This post explains the law and the arguments of both sides without endorsing either.

For decades the UCC has been argued about in Parliament and in courts. In the last two years the debate moved from theory to practice, as States began passing their own codes. This guide explains the constitutional basis, how the three State laws compare and what the objections are.

What is the Uniform Civil Code?

It is the idea of one common set of civil laws on marriage, divorce, succession and adoption for all citizens, in place of separate personal laws. Article 44 of the Constitution says the State shall endeavour to secure it, but it is a Directive Principle and not enforceable.

The Supreme Court has repeatedly urged the Union to act, in Shah Bano (1985), Sarla Mudgal (1995) and John Vallamattom (2003). The 21st Law Commission said in 2018 that a UCC was neither necessary nor desirable at that stage, and suggested reforming each personal law instead. The 22nd Law Commission began fresh consultation in June 2023.

Which States have passed a UCC?

Uttarakhand passed the first State UCC in 2024, and reports say Gujarat passed its law in March 2026 and Assam followed as the third State. Goa has had a common civil code since the Portuguese era.

Goa's code is often cited as proof that a common code can work, though critics point out that it retains some religion-based exceptions. Because the new State laws are recent, how courts treat them will depend on cases still to come.

How do the three State laws differ?

Reports say they share a minimum marriage age of 21 for men and 18 for women, a ban on polygamy, mandatory marriage registration and exclusion of Scheduled Tribes. They differ mostly in how strictly live-in relationship registration and penalties are enforced.

Can a State make a UCC?

Marriage, divorce and succession are in the Concurrent List, so State legislatures can legislate, but a State law that conflicts with a Central law needs the President's assent under Article 254(2). Whether a State can do what Article 44 addresses to "the State" at the national level is a point of argument.

Supporters say Entry 5 gives States the power and that the Directive Principle covers each State. Critics say that a State-wise code produces different "uniform" laws, which defeats the aim of uniformity across the country. Because Articles 25 and 26 protect religious freedom and Article 29 protects culture, any code may also face challenge on those grounds.

What are the main arguments on each side?

Supporters argue that it advances equality, especially for women, and national integration. Critics argue that it risks overriding religious and cultural practices, that mandatory live-in registration violates privacy under Puttaswamy (2017) and that tribal exclusion shows uniformity is selective.

The balanced position in an exam is that Article 44 sits alongside Articles 25 to 29, and the Court has said reform can proceed in stages. A fair answer should avoid saying that either side is "right"; it should present the arguments and the constitutional text.

How can this be asked in UGC NET Law, CLAT PG or AIBE?

Expect questions on Article 44, Shah Bano, Sarla Mudgal, Goa's code and the concurrent legislative power.
