Quick answer
  • 1 August 2024: seven judges held 6:1 that States may sub-classify SCs, overruling E.V. Chinnaiah (2004).
  • Sub-classification needs empirical data, not political convenience.
  • The creamy layer for SC and ST is not law; the Union opposed it in a 2026 affidavit.
  • Debate: reaching the weakest versus unity of the Article 341 class.

Reservation for Scheduled Castes and Tribes is meant to lift the most disadvantaged communities. A long-standing question is whether a few groups within the lists get most of the benefit. In 2024 the Supreme Court said States may create quotas within quotas, and a creamy layer debate followed. This guide explains the decision and the arguments.

What did State of Punjab v. Davinder Singh decide?

On 1 August 2024 a seven-judge Constitution Bench held by 6 to 1 that States can sub-classify Scheduled Castes to give more benefit to the more backward among them, overruling E.V. Chinnaiah (2004), provided they have empirical data and not on whim.

Chinnaiah had held that SCs form a homogeneous class and cannot be divided. The 2024 bench said that homogeneity is not required, and that Article 15(4) and 16(4) allow States to make reasonable classification. Justice Bela Trivedi dissented, holding that only Parliament can alter the list under Article 341 and that sub-classification would amount to tampering with the list. The judgment says that States must show inadequate representation of the sub-class, with data, and cannot give 100 per cent of the quota to a single sub-caste.

Does the creamy layer apply to SC and ST?

For OBCs, Indra Sawhney (1992) excluded the creamy layer. In Davinder Singh, some judges said the principle should apply to SC and ST as well, but these were separate opinions, and the Union has said that the Constitution does not provide for it.

After the 2024 verdict, the Government said that the creamy layer principle does not apply to SC and ST. A PIL asking the Court to direct its application is pending. For the OBC side of the question, read our post on the Rohith Nathan ruling.

What is the Centre's stand in 2026?

In a counter affidavit reported in August 2026 the Union opposed a PIL seeking an income-based creamy layer for SC and ST, arguing that identification rests on historical and social criteria, that Indra Sawhney confined the creamy layer to OBCs and that policy needs a thorough empirical study.

The Union also said that the petition asks the Court to direct the executive on policy, which is beyond judicial review. The petitioners argue that advanced families within SC and ST communities keep capturing benefits. The bench hearing the PIL and the next date were not given in the reports used here, so the status should be checked.

What is the argument for sub-classification and a creamy layer?

Supporters say a few sub-castes capture most benefits, so a quota within a quota reaches the weakest groups. They say Article 341 does not bar States from making sensible distinctions within a class, and that a creamy layer prevents elite capture.

They cite data from State committees that found uneven distribution of benefits. They also point out that the Court has accepted sub-classification among OBCs for decades. States such as Punjab, Tamil Nadu and Andhra Pradesh have tried internal quotas.

What is the argument against?

Opponents say that SCs are a single class under Article 341, that untouchability is the common disadvantage whatever the income, and that sub-classification and a creamy layer can dilute the purpose of reservation and be misused.

They add that the data needed is difficult to collect, particularly without a recent caste enumeration, and that economic criteria do not remove social stigma. This links to the debate on the caste census; see our post on the 2027 census and the 50% cap. A balanced answer gives both views and notes that the judgments are by different benches with different points of focus.

How can this be asked in UGC NET Law, CLAT PG or AIBE?

Expect questions on Articles 15(4), 16(4) and 341, Indra Sawhney, Chinnaiah and Davinder Singh.
