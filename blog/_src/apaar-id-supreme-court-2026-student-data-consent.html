A single academic identity for every student sounds convenient, but it raises serious questions about data and consent. The Supreme Court's 2026 ruling on APAAR ID is a good example of how privacy law shapes government programmes. This guide explains the scheme, the ruling and the privacy framework.

What did the Supreme Court decide on APAAR ID?

In Abhishek Baxi v. Union of India (2026) the Court upheld the APAAR scheme but required the consent form to include an opt-out and limited disclosure of student data to lawful purposes.

The decision is a middle path: the scheme stands, but with safeguards. That pattern, upholding a programme while tightening consent and data use, is typical of how the Court has handled digital identity cases since Aadhaar.

What is APAAR ID?

APAAR stands for Automated Permanent Academic Account Registry, a unique student identity linked to academic records and credits, intended to follow a student through their education.

Is APAAR mandatory?

After the ruling, the consent form must offer an opt-out, which means participation cannot be forced without a genuine choice. Check the scheme guidelines for how consent works for minors.

Consent for minors typically comes from parents or guardians, and that links to the children's data rules in the DPDP Act.

Why is student data privacy a legal issue?

Students are often minors, and their data can be sensitive. The right to privacy under Article 21 (Puttaswamy, 2017) requires that data collection be lawful, necessary and proportionate.

In K.S. Puttaswamy v. Union of India (2017) a nine-judge bench held privacy to be a fundamental right, and the proportionality test was applied in the Aadhaar judgment (2018). The same test applies to any state database of citizens, including students.

What does the DPDP Act say about children's data?

The Digital Personal Data Protection Act, 2023 requires verifiable parental consent for processing a child's personal data and restricts tracking and targeted advertising aimed at children.

Education sector data practices are therefore subject to both constitutional privacy and statutory duties. Compare with the IT law notes in Unit 9.
