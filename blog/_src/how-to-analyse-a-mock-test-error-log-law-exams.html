Quick answers
  • Review time: at least as long as the test took.
  • Error log: question, topic, cause, one-line fix, revisit date.
  • Four causes: knowledge gap, misread, elimination failure, time pressure.
  • Trend: judge across five mocks, not one.

Most aspirants take mock tests, look at the score, feel good or bad and move on. The students who improve are doing something else: they treat each paper as data. This guide shows a review routine that works for any law exam, a simple error log, and how to read your mock trend honestly.

How do I analyse a mock test properly?

Spend as long reviewing as you spent writing. Re-check every question you got wrong, guessed or took too long on, and write each in an error log with its cause: knowledge gap, misread question, elimination mistake or time pressure. Then act on the pattern, not the score.

A mock test is a measurement, not a lesson. The lesson comes from the review. A simple routine that takes about 90 minutes after a two-hour paper:

  1. Mark the paper and record raw score, attempts, accuracy and time per section.
  2. List every wrong answer and every correct guess. Correct guesses hide gaps.
  3. Classify each using the four causes below.
  4. Write the correct reasoning in one line for each wrong answer.
  5. Add the topic to a revision list with a date to revisit.
  6. Retake the questions after seven days without looking at notes.

What should I write in an error log after a mock test?

Write the question number, subject, topic, your answer, the right answer, the cause of the error and a one-line fix. Add a column for time spent. After three or four mocks, sort by cause and subject, and the pattern of where marks leak will be obvious.

What are the four types of mistakes in law MCQs?

Knowledge gap (you did not know it), misread (you knew it but read the question or qualifier wrongly), elimination failure (you narrowed correctly but picked wrongly) and time pressure (you rushed or did not reach it). Each needs a different fix, so label them honestly.

TypeSignFix
Knowledge gapYou could not explain the answer even after seeing itStudy the topic; make a short note and a flash card
MisreadYou knew it as soon as you re-read the questionUnderline qualifiers (only, always, except, not); slow the first read
Elimination failureYou removed two options and chose the wrong one of the restReview the rule that separates the last two options
Time pressureYou guessed because the clock was runningPractise pacing; skip earlier and return

Most aspirants discover that misreads and time pressure are a bigger share of their lost marks than they expected. These are cheaper to fix than knowledge gaps and can add several marks within two weeks.

How often should I take mock tests while preparing?

Start with one full mock every ten days during coverage, then move to one a week, and in the last three weeks take two a week with a full review after each. Never take more mocks than you can properly review; an unreviewed mock is wasted time.

For the last fortnight, match the real exam conditions: same time of day, same duration, paper and pen if the exam is offline, no breaks. The mock count guide gives exam-wise numbers.

How do I know if my mock scores are improving?

Track three numbers across mocks: attempts, accuracy and marks lost to wrong answers. A rising score from rising accuracy is real progress. A rising score from more attempts at falling accuracy is not, especially in exams with negative marking. Look at five mocks together, not one.

One mock can swing by 10 or more marks because of luck in which topics appeared. Plot your last five mocks on a table and look for the trend. If scores are flat, check your error causes: if misreads and time pressure dominate, change your technique; if knowledge gaps dominate, go back to coverage for those subjects. A worked example: reading one CLAT-style mock

Suppose you score 71.5 in a 120-question paper: 104 attempted, 78 right, 26 wrong, so 78 − 26 × 0.25 = 71.5. The score alone says little. Now classify the 26 wrong answers.

CauseWrong answersMarks recoverable if fixedWhat to do
Knowledge gap99 × 1.25 = 11.25Revise the topics; it takes weeks
Misread88 × 1.25 = 10Slow the first read and underline qualifiers; it takes days
Elimination failure55 × 1.25 = 6.25Review the rule that separates the last two options
Time pressure44 × 1.25 = 5Skip earlier; return later

Each wrong answer converted to a right answer is worth 1.25 marks, because you gain the mark and avoid the 0.25 penalty. The log shows that misreads alone are worth about 10 marks, almost as much as the knowledge gaps, and are far quicker to fix. Without the log you would likely have spent the next two weeks reading more, when the faster gain was in reading questions more carefully.

What to do with correct guesses

Mark every answer you got right by guessing and treat it like a wrong answer in your log. A guess that came off tells you nothing about your knowledge, and the same question could cost you a mark next time. Aspirants who skip this step overestimate their readiness by 5 to 10 marks.

A seven-day routine after each full mock

  1. Day 0: take the mock; write only the score.
  2. Day 1: full review and error log.
  3. Day 2 to 3: study the top two weak topics from the log.
  4. Day 4: 40 fresh questions on those topics.
  5. Day 7: re-attempt every question in the log without notes.
  6. Day 8: next mock.
