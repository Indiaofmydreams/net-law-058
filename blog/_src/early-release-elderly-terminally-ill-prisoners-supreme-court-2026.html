Prisons hold many elderly and seriously ill people whose continued detention raises questions of dignity and cost. In 2026 the Supreme Court asked governments to build a clear system for releasing such prisoners. This guide explains what was directed, how it fits Article 21 and what exam questions may follow.

What did the Supreme Court direct on early release of prisoners in 2026?

In NALSA v. Union of India (reported July 2026) the Court directed the Centre and states to frame and notify a premature release policy for prisoners over 70 or terminally ill, with a uniform definition of terminal illness and independent medical boards.

The key move is uniformity. States have different remission and release rules, and many have no specific rule for the dying or very old. By requiring every government to notify a policy, the Court aims to remove arbitrary differences between states.

Who will decide whether a prisoner is terminally ill?

The directions call for independent medical boards at divisional and state levels, so that the decision rests on medical assessment rather than discretion alone.

Does the RPwD Act apply inside prisons?

Yes. On 21 April 2026 the Supreme Court held that the Rights of Persons with Disabilities Act, 2016 applies fully in custodial settings, so prisoners with disabilities are entitled to its protections.

This matters because accessibility, healthcare and reasonable accommodation are not suspended at the prison gate. It extends the idea that prisoners retain all rights not taken away by lawful imprisonment.

Does this mean every elderly prisoner will be released?

No. The Court ordered the framing of a policy. Release will depend on the policy's criteria, which can include the nature of the offence, conduct and risk, not just age or illness.

Release in India works through remission under the Code of Criminal Procedure's successor provisions, state remission policies and constitutional powers of the President and Governor under Articles 72 and 161. The new policy would add a humanitarian track.

Which constitutional right supports early release for the dying?

Article 21 protects life with dignity. Courts have read it to cover humane treatment in custody, which supports the argument that dying or very elderly prisoners should not be kept in conditions that deny dignity.

Cases such as Sunil Batra and Charles Sobraj established that prisoners keep their fundamental rights. See also the related 2026 bail directions in our bail and custody rulings post.
