Quick answer
  • On 21 September 2026 (Justices Ujjal Bhuyan and Atul S. Chandurkar) the Supreme Court held an arrest illegal if written grounds were not given.
  • Grounds must be in a language the accused understands (Article 22(1)).
  • A later chargesheet or cognizance does not cure the defect; no prejudice need be shown.
  • Re-arrest is possible only after written grounds and a Magistrate's approval.

Most people know they have the right to be told why they are being arrested. Fewer know how strictly the Supreme Court now enforces that right. In September 2026 the Court turned the requirement into a bright-line rule with consequences for the investigating officer. This guide explains the rule, how it fits with earlier cases and what the police must do differently.

What did the Supreme Court hold on grounds of arrest in 2026?

On 21 September 2026 Justices Ujjal Bhuyan and Atul S. Chandurkar held that an arrest is illegal if the written grounds, in a language the accused understands, are not furnished, as required by Article 22(1).

Article 22(1) says that no person arrested shall be detained without being informed, as soon as may be, of the grounds for the arrest, and shall not be denied the right to consult a lawyer of their choice. Earlier cases, such as Pankaj Bansal (2023) and Prabir Purkayastha (2024), said the grounds should be given in writing. The 2026 ruling goes further by spelling out the consequences of failure.

Does a later chargesheet cure the defect?

No. The Court held that neither a chargesheet nor cognizance later cures the illegality, and the accused does not need to show prejudice.

The reasoning is that Article 22(1) is a fundamental right, not a technical step. If a later event could cure a violation, officers would have no reason to comply at the time of arrest. By refusing the prejudice test, the Court removed a common defence, which is that the accused knew the case anyway.

Is the accused released on bail?

No. Release follows from illegal detention, not from bail. The accused goes free from that detention, though the investigation need not end.

The difference matters. Bail is a conditional release for a person who is validly in custody. Here the custody itself is unlawful, so the person is simply released, without the usual bail conditions. The investigation can continue, and the person remains answerable to the law. The Court's approach is similar to the way courts treat a violation of the Article 22(2) rule that an arrested person must be produced before a Magistrate within 24 hours.

Can the police arrest the person again?

Yes, but only after supplying written grounds first and then applying to the Magistrate with reasons and an explanation of the lapse, endorsed by a senior officer. The Magistrate should decide it preferably within a week.

This gives the police a path to fix honest mistakes but makes sure that re-arrest is reviewed by a judicial officer. A re-arrest cannot be used as a routine workaround. The Court also said the same procedure applies where Article 22(2) is breached, for example if the person is not produced before a Magistrate within 24 hours of arrest, excluding travel time.

What consequences do officers face?

The investigation is reassigned, a departmental inquiry follows with an entry in the officer's service book, and High Courts may award compensation. The judgment is to be sent to all Directors General of Police.

The Court followed Mihir Rajesh Shah v. State of Maharashtra (2025) on the consequences of non-compliance, in preference to Sri Darshan and Sonam Raghuvanshi, which had taken a different approach to the effect. This is a point of precedent that students should note, because it shows the Court choosing between its own earlier decisions. For a related custody case, see our post on prisoner release.
