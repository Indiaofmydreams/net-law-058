  • New "key functionary" concept with a rebuttable presumption of liability.
  • New Chapter IIIA (Sections 16A to 16L): assets vest in a Designated Authority when registration ends.
  • Maximum jail term cut from five years to one, but prior government approval is needed to begin an investigation.

The Foreign Contribution (Regulation) Act governs how Indian organisations receive money from abroad. It sits at the intersection of national security, freedom of association and the work of NGOs, universities and religious bodies. The 2026 amendment Bill is the most debated change since 2020. It has not yet become law, but it is likely to appear in current affairs questions. This guide explains the proposals and the constitutional issues they raise.

What is the Foreign Contribution (Regulation) Amendment Bill, 2026?

It amends the 2010 FCRA. Introduced in the Lok Sabha on 25 March 2026, it was referred to a Joint Parliamentary Committee on 12 August 2026. It adds a key functionaries concept, a new Chapter IIIA on vesting of assets and a Designated Authority.

The 2010 Act replaced the 1976 law and requires organisations that accept foreign contributions to register or get prior permission, use designated bank accounts and file returns. Certificates must be renewed every five years. The Bill leaves that structure in place and adds new controls on what happens when registration ends. Rules made in 2026 also treat an organisation as having undertaken reasonable activity if it utilised at least 10 lakh rupees of foreign contribution over the last two financial years.

Who is a key functionary under the Bill?

Company directors, partners, trustees, the karta of a Hindu undivided family, office bearers of societies, trusts and associations and others responsible for management. They are presumed liable for the organisation's offences, but can rebut the presumption.

The presumption can be rebutted by showing that the offence took place without the person's knowledge or that they exercised due diligence. This resembles provisions in company and economic offences laws that fix responsibility on those in charge. When an organisation becomes defunct, its last key functionaries must tell the Centre; if they do not, the organisation's foreign contribution vests permanently in the Designated Authority.

What happens to assets when an FCRA registration ends?

Assets and unutilised foreign contribution vest in the Designated Authority when a certificate is cancelled, surrendered or ceases. Vesting is provisional until a fresh certificate is granted; if that does not happen within the prescribed period it becomes permanent.

No restoration in the prescribed periodVesting becomes permanent

The Designated Authority may use vested assets for public purposes, transfer them to central or state bodies or sell them, with the proceeds going to the Consolidated Fund of India. For places of worship, it must entrust management as prescribed and maintain the religious character. Assets only partly created with foreign funds can vest too, though an organisation may seek the return of a distinct portion created from domestic sources.

Does the Bill reduce penalties?

It reduces the maximum imprisonment for violating the Act from five years to one year, but requires the Centre's prior approval before starting an investigation for any offence under the Act.

Supporters see the lower jail term as a softening. Critics focus on the prior approval clause, which they say could shield or delay investigations and gives the executive a gatekeeping role over criminal process. Compare the general rule that a cognizable offence must be investigated on an FIR, which the Supreme Court stressed in Lalita Kumari. Another concern raised by analysts is the absence of an appeal or hearing before renewal is denied, although the Act provides those protections for cancellation.

What constitutional questions does the Bill raise?

Critics argue that vesting of assets raises questions under Article 300A (right to property), Article 19(1)(c) (freedom of association), Article 14 (arbitrariness) and the principle of proportionality, especially where past foreign funds built assets used for domestic work.

The government's position, reflected in earlier rulings such as Indian Social Action Forum (2020), is that receiving foreign contribution is not a fundamental right and Parliament may regulate it in the interest of sovereignty and public order. The 2020 Supreme Court judgment upheld the 2020 amendments but read down parts of them. Expect any 2026 law to be tested on proportionality.

How can this be asked in UGC NET Law, CLAT PG or AIBE?

Expect questions on the FCRA's purpose, the difference between a bill and an Act, Article 300A, Article 19(1)(c) and the role of Joint Parliamentary Committees.
