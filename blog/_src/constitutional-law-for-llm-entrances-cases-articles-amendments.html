Constitutional Law is the single biggest block in nearly every law entrance on this site, and the subject where a plan pays back the most. It is also easy to study badly: a long list of Articles and cases with no structure. This guide gives a study order, Article groups, key cases, an amendment table and a revision routine that you can adapt for CLAT PG, AILET, CUET PG and UGC NET Law.

How do I study Constitutional Law for LLM entrance exams?

Study in a fixed order: structure and sources, fundamental rights, writs, directive principles, then the three organs, federalism, emergency and amendment. Learn each Article block with a theme and an anchor case, and build one sheet per block. Constitutional Law is the largest or one of the largest blocks in every law entrance.

Constitutional Law is the highest-yield subject for CLAT PG, AILET, CUET PG and UGC NET Law. In our CUET PG analysis it is the largest block at roughly eight to twelve of seventy-five questions (editorial estimate), and in UGC NET Law the Constitutional and Administrative Law unit led the past papers on this site. Which Articles should I learn first for law entrance exams?

Start with the fundamental rights block, Articles 12 to 35, then Articles 36 to 51A on directive principles and duties, and the key structural Articles: 72, 74, 123, 124, 141, 142, 200 to 201, 226, 245 to 254, 352, 356 and 368. Learn them as groups with a theme, not as isolated numbers.

Which constitutional cases are most important for entrance exams?

Learn the leading case for each doctrine: Kesavananda Bharati (basic structure), Maneka Gandhi (Article 21 and due process), Puttaswamy (privacy), Indra Sawhney (reservation), S.R. Bommai (federalism and Article 356), Minerva Mills (fundamental rights and directive principles), Kihoto Hollohan (anti-defection) and Davinder Singh (SC and ST sub-classification).

Each of these has an explainer on this site: the basic structure doctrine, Article 21 from Maneka Gandhi to Puttaswamy, fundamental rights versus directive principles and the five writs. Our Case Law Vault carries a wider list organised by subject.

Which constitutional amendments should I learn?

Learn the amendments that created doctrines or institutions: the 1st, 24th, 25th, 42nd, 44th, 52nd, 73rd, 74th, 86th, 91st, 101st, 103rd and 106th. Know the year, the Article or Schedule changed, and the one-line purpose. Amendments often appear in match-the-following and statement questions.

How do I revise Constitutional Law in the last month?

Use a one-page sheet for each Article block, do 40 Constitutional Law questions a week with review, and recall the key cases with the page covered. In the final week, read your error log for this subject and the amendment table once. Do not start new reference books.

Spend about fifty percent more time on Constitutional Law than on any other subject in your final month, because it is the largest block and the most predictable. Constitutional Law traps in MCQs

  • Article 32 versus Article 226: 32 is a fundamental right and applies to fundamental rights only; 226 is wider and a legal right.
  • Basic structure origin versus application: Kesavananda (1973) originated it; Minerva Mills (1980) applied and strengthened it.
  • Emergency grounds: after the 44th Amendment, 'armed rebellion' replaced 'internal disturbance' for Article 352.
  • Fundamental duties: added by the 42nd Amendment and not enforceable by writ.
  • Directive principles: not justiciable, but the courts use them to interpret fundamental rights.

Link each trap to a one-line entry on your sheet and quiz yourself on it weekly. Read the Article 21 explainer twice: once at the start and once in the last fortnight. Article 21 questions appear in almost every law entrance, in different disguises.

How much Constitution is enough?

If you can state the basic structure doctrine, explain each of the five writs, list the Article blocks above and name the leading case for each doctrine, you hold most of what recurring questions ask. The rest is polish and practice. Use the polish stage for statement-based and match-the-following questions, because they decide close scores.
