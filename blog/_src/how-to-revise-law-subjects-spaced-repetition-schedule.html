Most law aspirants study each subject once, move on and then wonder why they forget the early chapters by exam day. Memory needs repetition at the right intervals. This guide gives a revision schedule you can apply to any law exam, shows how to use active recall, and ends with a 30-day calendar for the final month.

How should I revise law subjects for an entrance exam?

Revise in spaced rounds, not one long pass at the end: read once, then recall after one day, three days, one week, two weeks and a month. Each round should be active: close the notes and write what you remember, then check. Passive re-reading feels productive but fades fast.

Learning research on memory is consistent: information you try to retrieve at increasing intervals stays far longer than information you re-read. For law, this applies to cases, section numbers, definitions and doctrines. The cost is discipline; the gain is that your final week becomes a light check, not a panic.

What is a good revision schedule for law exams?

Revise a topic on day one, day three, day seven, day fourteen and day thirty after first learning it. Keep each round shorter than the one before: 30 minutes, 20 minutes, 15 minutes, 10 minutes. The 1-3-7-14-30 pattern covers most law syllabi without extra stress.

What is active recall and how do I use it in law?

Active recall means pulling information out of memory instead of looking at it again. In law, close the book and write the rule, the exceptions and the leading case, or answer a question without choices, then compare with your notes. Even a wrong attempt strengthens memory more than reading.

  • Blank-page method: write everything you know about a topic in five minutes, then fill gaps in another colour.
  • Case cards: case name on the front; facts, holding and article or section on the back.
  • Question first: before reading a chapter, attempt five questions on it; you will read more attentively.
  • Teach it aloud: explain a doctrine to an imaginary student in one minute.

How do I revise when I have only 30 days left?

Split the month into three passes: ten days for core subjects, ten days for regular subjects and ten days for mixed mocks, review and weak spots. In each pass, use short notes and questions only. Do not start new books, and keep a daily 30 minutes for recall of earlier topics.

Should I revise from notes, books or questions?

Use all three in the right order. Revise from your own short notes for the rule, from the bare act for the exact wording, and from questions to test recall. Questions are the best revision tool because they show gaps; books are the worst, because re-reading hides them.

A good rule of thumb is a 30-30-40 split of revision time: 30 percent on notes and sheets, 30 percent on bare acts or key cases, and 40 percent on questions with review. If your notes are long, compress them: see how to make one-page subject sheets.

Common revision mistakes

  • Highlighting as revision. It feels like work and produces little memory. Replace it with a closed-book attempt.
  • Revising only what you like. Aspirants revisit favourite subjects and quietly avoid weak ones. Let the schedule, not mood, decide.
  • Leaving all revision for the end. A topic first met three months ago needs four short rounds; one long round in the last week cannot replace them.
  • No questions in revision. If you do not test yourself, you only learn what you already know.

Pair this schedule with your error log (how to build one) so that the topics you got wrong in mocks return to the top of your next revision round. A revision plan that you can follow on a bad day is better than a perfect plan you abandon by week two, so keep each session short, specific and scheduled.
