Quick answer
  • On 26 May 2026 (Justices J.K. Maheshwari and Atul S. Chandurkar) the Supreme Court held that trauma care is part of Article 21.
  • Interim directions: integrate emergency numbers into 112, operationalise PM RAHAT cashless treatment, set up Good Samaritan grievance systems and trauma registries.
  • Most deadlines are three months; registries four months.
  • Good Samaritan protection comes from Section 134A of the Motor Vehicles Act.

In India, a road accident victim's chances often depend on how fast they reach a hospital and whether a bystander is willing to help. For years, studies and petitions have highlighted that many deaths are preventable. In 2026 the Supreme Court said that access to trauma care is a constitutional right and set deadlines for the State. This guide explains the ruling and each major direction.

What did the Supreme Court hold in SaveLIFE Foundation v. Union of India?

On 26 May 2026 Justices J.K. Maheshwari and Atul S. Chandurkar held that the right to trauma care is an integral part of the right to life under Article 21 and issued interim directions for a uniform road accident trauma care framework.

The petition under Article 32 was filed by SaveLIFE Foundation, a road safety organisation. The Court heard States and Union Territories through affidavits and then issued directions with a four-month period for compliance reports. The reasoning is simple: the State's duty to protect life includes a duty to provide emergency medical care when an accident happens, and the system must work for everyone, not only in some cities.

What is the Good Samaritan protection?

Section 134A of the Motor Vehicles Act protects a person who helps a road accident victim from civil and criminal liability. The Court noted that bystanders hesitate for fear of legal trouble and directed awareness campaigns and grievance systems for Good Samaritans.

The Court called for proper Good Samaritan laws and standardised first-aid training. It directed States to set up physical and digital grievance redressal systems with nodal officers at State and district level within three months, holding monthly meetings and publishing the minutes online. Multi-lingual mass media campaigns on 112, Good Samaritan protections, the grievance system and PM RAHAT are to be run within one month. Earlier, in SaveLIFE Foundation (2016), the Court had already given binding directions to protect Good Samaritans.

What did the Court direct on emergency response?

States and Union Territories must integrate emergency numbers such as 100, 101, 108, 102, 1033 and 1091 into 112 within three months, ensure ambulances meet the AIS-125 standard with GPS linked to 112, and adopt the national EMT training curriculum.

The facility grading covers not just National Highways but also State Highways, major district roads and urban and peri-urban areas, which is important because many serious accidents happen outside highways.

What is PM RAHAT and why did the Court focus on it?

PM RAHAT is the cashless treatment scheme for road accident victims. The Court directed that it be fully operationalised, with designated hospitals, State Health Agency onboarding and police use of the eDAR system, and said non-implementation would violate the Motor Vehicles Act.

The cashless treatment idea addresses a hard reality: hospitals sometimes delay treatment until someone pays. The Motor Vehicles Act provisions on the treatment of road accident victims require that scheme to operate, so the Court treated non-implementation as a breach of the statute and not just a policy gap. States that have not adopted the scheme must do so within three months. The reports differ slightly on the exact timeline for operationalisation, so check the order.

What are trauma registries and why do they matter?

They are databases of trauma cases. The Union must issue data format guidelines within eight weeks, and States must set up State Trauma Registries linked to a Coordinated Trauma Registry within four months.

Data is how a system learns. A registry can show where accidents happen, how long ambulances take and which hospitals have the capability to treat serious injuries. Without it, planning is guesswork. The Court's directions link registries with grading of facilities and the integrated helpline, so that policy rests on evidence.
