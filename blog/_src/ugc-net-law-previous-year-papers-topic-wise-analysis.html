Quick answer
  • We counted 500 Law questions across five papers on this site (2021, 2024, June 2025, December 2025, June 2026).
  • Constitutional and Administrative Law led with 77, then Public International Law (60) and Commercial Law (56).
  • Some units swing a lot: Public International Law ranged from 6 to 18 in a paper.
  • Use old papers to learn how topics are framed, not to guess next year's questions.

Previous year papers are the most honest guide to an exam, because they show what the setter asked rather than what a guide book predicts. Which units are steady and which swing?

  • Steady: Constitutional and Administrative Law (12 to 18 a paper) and Commercial Law (10 to 12) were the most dependable sources of questions.
  • Swinging: Public International Law (6 to 18), Family Law (7 to 17) and Comparative Public Law (3 to 10) changed the most from paper to paper.
  • Rising: Environmental Law and Human Rights and IPR and IT Law were higher in 2024 and June 2026 than in 2021, though five papers is too few to call a trend.

The lesson for planning is that a unit that was light last time can be heavy next time, so a revision plan should cover all ten and weight the big ones.

Do questions repeat?

Exact questions rarely return, but themes do: Fundamental Rights and amendments, treaties and courts in international law, the major Acts in commercial law, and the main statutes in environment and IPR keep reappearing in new wording. When you review an old paper, ask three things for every question you missed: which topic was it, which Act or case sat behind it, and was the mistake knowledge or misreading.

How to use previous year papers

  1. First pass, untimed. Read the paper and mark topics you do not recognise.
  2. Second pass, timed. Solve a full paper in one sitting on the PYQ page.
  3. Error log. Write each miss by unit, topic and reason.
  4. Feed the plan. Send your weakest unit back to its unit notes and revise.
  5. Repeat with the next paper a week later.
