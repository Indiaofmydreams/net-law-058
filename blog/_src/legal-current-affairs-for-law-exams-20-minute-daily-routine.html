Legal current affairs is the part of preparation where aspirants either do too little or far too much. No exam on this site publishes a current-affairs weightage, but recent Acts and judgments do show up. A short, daily and structured habit covers it without eating into your core subjects. This guide gives a twenty-minute routine, source advice, a card format and a weekly review.

How much legal current affairs do I need for law exams?

Less than most aspirants fear. No exam on this site publishes a current-affairs weightage. Recent Acts and landmark judgments appear as a few questions or as the subject of passages, so a short daily habit of twenty minutes beats hours of news. Focus on law, not general news.

The risk is the opposite of ignorance: spending hours on news and neglecting core subjects. Treat current affairs as a thin layer on top of your syllabus. A new Act is a question about its sections; a Supreme Court judgment is a question about the doctrine it applied. Learn each development through the doctrine, and it supports your core subjects instead of competing with them.

What is a good daily routine for legal current affairs?

Spend twenty minutes: five minutes scanning headlines from reliable legal sources, ten minutes understanding one development in depth, and five minutes writing it on a card. Do this on weekdays only and keep weekends for a weekly revision of the week's cards. Skip the rest of the news.

Which sources should I use for legal current affairs?

Use primary and reliable sources: the Supreme Court of India website for judgments and orders, the Gazette or official ministry pages for new Acts, PRS Legislative Research for bill summaries, and established legal news portals for daily updates. Avoid forwarded messages and unverified social media summaries.

This site's own blog covers many recent developments with the legal question explained: for example, the monsoon session 2026 Acts explained, the Labour Codes and the Industrial Relations Code amendment and SC and ST sub-classification after Davinder Singh. Read one article a day in your routine; each is built around the law, not the headline.

How do I note a legal development for an exam?

Use a five-line card: name or topic, date, the question of law, the holding or provision, and the syllabus topic it belongs to. Add one line on 'why it matters for the exam'. A card should take under five minutes and fit on an index card or one phone note.

LineExample
DevelopmentDavinder Singh v State of Punjab (2024)
Date and forumSupreme Court, Constitution Bench, August 2024
Question of lawCan States sub-classify Scheduled Castes for reservation?
HoldingYes, if based on empirical data of inadequate representation; reverses E.V. Chinnaiah
Syllabus linkEquality, Arts 14 to 16; reservation

For the anatomy of a judgment and how to separate holding from observation, see our passage-solving method, which applies equally to reading a judgment extract.

How do I avoid wasting time on news that will not be asked?

Ask three questions before reading deeper: does it change or interpret a law, does it map to a syllabus topic, and would a question writer find it neat to test? If the answer to all three is no, skip it. Politics, opinion and procedural events rarely become questions.

  • Read: new Acts and amendments, constitution bench judgments, rules and notifications, major reports of statutory bodies.
  • Skim: bills pending in Parliament, Law Commission reports, High Court rulings of wide importance.
  • Skip: political reactions, commentary and cases still in the early stage.

A weekly revision of current affairs cards

Once a week, take the week's cards and sort them by subject: Constitution, Criminal Law (including the new codes), Commercial and Corporate, Procedure, Human Rights and Environment. Five minutes per pile is plenty. Attach each card to your one-page subject sheet if it adds a case or section (see one-page sheets). By the last month you will have 25 to 30 cards: a ready list for the final revision.

  • Constitution: constitution bench rulings, amendments, Governor and Speaker powers.
  • Criminal Law: BNS, BNSS and BSA changes and rulings on bail and arrest.
  • Commercial: IBC, arbitration, labour and company law amendments.
  • Rights and environment: Article 21 expansions, environment rulings.
