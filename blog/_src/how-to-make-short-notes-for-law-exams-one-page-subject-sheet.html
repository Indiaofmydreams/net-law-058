Quick answers
  • Format: one page per subject; two to four for the Constitution.
  • Five blocks: definitions, doctrines, cases, sections, exceptions.
  • Build from memory, not by copying.
  • Stop adding notes about three weeks before the exam.

Notes are the most overused and least used tool in law exam preparation: aspirants make hundreds of pages and rarely return to them. The one-page subject sheet fixes this by forcing you to decide what matters, giving you a revision tool you can read in minutes. This guide shows what to include, a sample for Contract Law and a template for any subject.

How do I make short notes for a law exam?

Compress each subject to one page after your first reading: the key definitions, the tests and doctrines, the leading cases, the important sections and the common exceptions. Write in your own words, and rebuild the sheet from memory a week later. If it does not fit on a page, you do not yet know what matters.

Short notes are a tool for revision, not for first learning. Read a subject properly from a standard source, and only then compress. The act of choosing what makes the page is the learning. A sheet you copied from someone else's notes teaches you far less than one you built yourself, even if theirs is neater.

What should a one-page law subject sheet contain?

Five blocks: definitions, tests or doctrines, leading cases, key sections and Articles, and exceptions or traps. Keep each block to five or six lines, and use a table where two concepts must be contrasted. Leave a margin for corrections from mocks and review.

How long should short notes be?

About one page per subject for revision, and two to four pages for a large subject such as the Constitution. A typical entrance syllabus then fits into 30 to 40 pages of notes you can revise in a day. If your notes are longer than that, they are not short notes; they are a book.

The one-page rule forces ruthless choices. If a topic appears in your syllabus but has supplied no question in past papers, give it one line. If it recurs, give it a block. Use your exam's syllabus priority guide or the syllabus tracker (CUET PG, AILET, AIBE) to decide how much page each topic earns.

Should I make notes by hand or digitally?

Either works; choose the one you will actually review. Handwriting slows you down and aids memory, which suits one-page sheets. Digital notes are easier to search and update, which suits long reference material. Do not spend more time formatting than recalling, whatever the tool.

When should I stop making notes and start practising?

Stop making new notes about three weeks before the exam, or when you have covered the whole syllabus once. After that, add only corrections from mocks. Aspirants who keep making notes until the last week trade practice for the comfort of looking organised.

The test of a sheet is whether you can use it: cover it, write what you remember, and compare. If you cannot reconstruct it, review it; if you can, move on to questions. A second sample: compressing negligence in Torts

BlockSheet entry
ElementsDuty of care; breach of duty; damage caused; damage not too remote
Tests and doctrinesNeighbour principle (Donoghue v Stevenson); reasonable person test; res ipsa loquitur; contributory negligence
Leading casesDonoghue v Stevenson (duty to the ultimate consumer); Bolton v Stone (reasonable foreseeability of harm)
StatutesConsumer Protection Act 2019 (deficiency in service); Motor Vehicles Act 1988 (compensation)
Exceptions and trapsVolenti non fit injuria is a defence, not an element; contributory negligence reduces damages, it does not bar them under the modern rule

Notice that each line carries a name, a number or a rule you can test yourself on. If a line reads like a paragraph, trim it to its single most testable fact.

The last box is the most valuable: it records the questions you actually got wrong. Over six weeks it becomes the most personal and useful part of your notes. Review it before every mock, and read it again the night before the exam.

Three mistakes in note-making

  • Copying lectures word for word. You learn typing, not law.
  • Colour-coding everything. If everything is highlighted, nothing is.
  • Never reviewing the notes. A beautiful sheet you never open is worth nothing.
