"Digital arrest" is not an arrest at all. Fraudsters pose as police, customs or CBI officers on a video call, claim the victim is implicated in a crime, and keep them "under watch" while they transfer money to "verify" their innocence. Victims include educated professionals and retired people. The Supreme Court has taken the problem up on its own motion, and its 2026 directions are now the closest thing India has to a national playbook. This guide explains them in plain terms and shows how they connect to criminal law.

What did the Supreme Court direct on digital arrest scams in August 2026?

On 4 August 2026 a bench of CJI Surya Kant and Justices Joymalya Bagchi and V. Mohana told the RBI to adopt a bank SOP on temporary debit holds within four weeks, with grievance redressal and money restoration modules, and directed States to set up cyber crime coordination centres.

The order is a package of directions to different actors rather than a single rule. The RBI must frame the SOP. Banks must follow it and take part in money restoration. The Centre must run an inter-departmental committee that issues advisories and awareness programmes. States and Union Territories must notify State Cyber Crime Coordination Centres within four weeks and move to the e-Zero FIR system. High Courts' Registrars General must bring the grievance mechanism to the notice of courts that handle bank account freezes. The matter was listed again for September.

Is digital arrest a legal concept in India?

No. Digital arrest is a scam label, not a legal procedure. No agency can arrest a person or hold them in custody over a video call. The Court has asked the Centre to consider treating it as a distinct offence with stringent punishment.

Real arrest in India follows the Bharatiya Nagarik Suraksha Sanhita and Article 22: the person is told the grounds, produced before a magistrate within 24 hours and has a right to a lawyer. Fraudsters imitate the look of that process. Today, the conduct is prosecuted under general provisions of the Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita on cheating, cheating by personation and extortion, along with the Information Technology Act's provisions on identity theft and cheating by personation using a computer resource. Because victims are often held on camera for hours or days, the Court has pressed for a stronger, specific offence. Whether Parliament will create one is still open.

How much money has been restored to victims so far?

According to the I4C status report noted by the Court, money was restored in 36,290 cases totalling 18.05 crore rupees, with 57 banks and all States and Union Territories participating.

The Court also noted the CBI's role: it registered digital arrest cases, identified victims through transactions in 67 first-layer bank accounts and searched 93 locations across 16 States. The bench said the mechanism already in place "requires wide adoption, faster disposal and continued follow-up". In other words, the machinery exists but is not reaching enough victims quickly.

How does the RBI debit-hold SOP work?

The SOP lets banks place a temporary debit hold on accounts linked to cyber-enabled financial fraud so stolen money cannot be moved onward. It must include a grievance redressal mechanism, a money restoration module and public awareness about both.

Speed is the whole point. Scam money is usually moved out of the first account within minutes into "mule" accounts, which are accounts opened or rented out to launder proceeds. A short, rule-bound freeze can stop the chain, but a freeze also hurts innocent account holders whose accounts are caught by mistake. That is why the Court insisted on a grievance route and asked High Courts to encourage affected persons to use it first. The balance is between the victim's chance of recovery and the account holder's right to operate their money, which is protected by Article 300A and the freedom to carry on trade under Article 19(1)(g).

What is the difference between a bank freeze and a police seizure?

A bank debit hold is a short, administrative pause under the RBI SOP. A police seizure or freeze under the BNSS is a formal investigative power that must be reported to a magistrate. The SOP aims to speed up the first and standardise how the second is reviewed.

Under criminal procedure, the police may seize property suspected to be stolen or linked to an offence and must report the seizure to the magistrate. Courts have repeatedly said that freezing an account without a clear link to the offence or without a report is illegal. The August directions ask for expeditious disposal of matters on freezing accounts linked to cyber fraud, so that innocent holders are not stuck for years.

What else did the Court ask the Centre to do?

The Centre must examine lowering the monetary threshold for CBI takeover of cyber fraud cases, consider aggregating cases from the same organised network and examine time-based limits on audio and video calls. MeitY, DoT and I4C must file a note on feasibility and alternatives.

The aggregation idea matters because a network may defraud hundreds of people in amounts that are each too small for a central agency but large in total. On compensation, the inter-departmental committee must examine a shared liability and victim compensation framework. Legal Services Authorities were asked to run awareness programmes on prevention, cyber security and recovery.

How can this be asked in UGC NET Law, CLAT PG or AIBE?

Expect questions on Article 21 and cyber crime, the difference between lawful arrest and a scam, mule accounts, freezing of accounts and suo motu jurisdiction.

Stay safe, and read the order

This is an explainer, not legal advice. If you receive a "police" or "CBI" video call demanding money, disconnect and report it to the national cyber crime helpline or portal at once. The matter is continuing, so check the latest order.
