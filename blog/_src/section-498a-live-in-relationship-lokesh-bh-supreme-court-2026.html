Quick answer
  • In Dr. Lokesh B.H. v. State of Karnataka (Justices Sanjay Karol and N. Kotiswar Singh, reported 3 August 2026) the Supreme Court held that Section 498A IPC applies to live-in relationships in the nature of marriage.
  • Test: consenting adults and an intention to marry that is intrinsic to the relationship.
  • Not every live-in relationship attracts criminal liability.
  • The Court stressed Arnesh Kumar safeguards against automatic arrest.

Section 498A of the Indian Penal Code (now Section 85 of the Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita) punishes cruelty by a husband or his relatives. For decades the question has been whether a man who is not legally a husband can be prosecuted. The Supreme Court's 2026 answer is a careful "sometimes". This guide explains the test, the reasoning and what it means for family and criminal law students.

Does Section 498A apply to live-in relationships?

Yes, in some cases. In Lokesh B.H. v. State of Karnataka (reported 3 August 2026) the Supreme Court held that a man can be prosecuted for cruelty to a woman if the live-in relationship was in the nature of marriage and the parties intended to marry. Not every live-in relationship attracts liability.

The appeals arose from the Karnataka High Court's refusal to quash proceedings. The complainant said she and the accused had married under Hindu customs and lived together, while the accused denied a valid marriage. The FIR alleged that he hid a prior marriage, that he and his family harassed her for dowry and that he caused burn injuries to her leg. The Supreme Court did not decide whether those events occurred. That is for trial.

What is a relationship in the nature of marriage?

It is a subset of live-in relationships. The partners must be consenting adults and an intention to marry must be an intrinsic part of the relationship. The indicators are illustrative, not exhaustive, and the initial burden lies on the woman seeking protection.

The phrase "relationship in the nature of marriage" comes from the Protection of Women from Domestic Violence Act, 2005, and was explained in earlier Supreme Court cases such as D. Velusamy and Indra Sarma, which set out features such as shared household, public holding out as a couple and duration. The 2026 ruling uses the concept in a criminal law setting, and adds intent to marry as an essential element.

Why did the Court extend Section 498A?

It reasoned that the provision is not confined to legally valid marriages, that its reformative and deterrent purpose should guide interpretation and that denying protection on the basis of marital status has no rational link with the law's objective and offends Article 14.

The argument is about purpose. Cruelty and dowry harassment do not become less harmful because a marriage turns out to be void or because the couple never registered it. A woman who believed she was in a marriage-like relationship, and who suffers the same treatment, would otherwise be left with weaker remedies. The Court was also concerned about men who might exploit the absence of a valid marriage as a shield.

Is the Domestic Violence Act enough protection?

The Court observed that the Domestic Violence Act is primarily civil and its only penal provision is Section 31, so it is not an adequate substitute for penal protection against cruelty.

The 2005 Act gives remedies such as protection orders, residence orders and monetary relief, enforced mostly through civil process. Penal consequences arise mainly when a protection order is breached. For serious cruelty, such as physical injury or dowry harassment, the criminal law provisions remain important, and the ruling confirms that live-in partners are not excluded.

Can the police arrest immediately in such cases?

The Court directed strict compliance with the Arnesh Kumar safeguards. Reports also say no arrest should be made without a preliminary inquiry. Check the judgment for the exact wording.

Arnesh Kumar v. State of Bihar (2014) required police to apply the checklist in the arrest provisions before arresting in offences punishable up to seven years, which includes Section 498A. Misuse of the provision has been a long-standing concern, so the Court paired the extension of the law with safeguards. The aim is to protect genuine complainants without exposing the accused to routine arrest.
