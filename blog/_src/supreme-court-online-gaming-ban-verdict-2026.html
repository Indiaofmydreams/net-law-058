For years the online fantasy and rummy industry argued that games of skill are outside gambling laws. In 2026 the Supreme Court went the other way when money is staked. This guide explains the ruling, the difference between skill and chance and why the case matters for federalism, tax and constitutional law exams.

Did the Supreme Court uphold the ban on online betting games?

Yes. In 2026 the Supreme Court upheld Tamil Nadu and Karnataka laws banning online games played with stakes, holding that betting on games of skill is gambling. It overruled the contrary view of the Madras and Karnataka High Courts.

The appeals were headed by State of Tamil Nadu v. Junglee Games India. The High Courts had struck down or read down the state laws, partly on the ground that the legislature cannot treat games of skill as gambling. The Supreme Court's answer is that where players stake money on the outcome, the activity falls within betting and gambling, whatever the role of skill.

What is the difference between a game of skill and a game of chance?

A game of chance is decided mainly by luck, while a game of skill depends predominantly on knowledge, judgment and practice. Older decisions treated skill-based games like rummy as outside gambling laws, but the 2026 ruling holds that staking money on skill games can still be gambling.

The "dominant factor" test, developed in cases from the 1960s, asked whether skill or chance predominated. That test protected games such as rummy and, later, fantasy sports from the Public Gambling Act. The 2026 decision does not abolish the distinction in all contexts, but it means a state can legislate against staked games of skill under its power over betting and gambling.

Which entry of the Seventh Schedule covers betting and gambling?

Entry 34 of the State List covers betting and gambling, which is why states, not Parliament, usually legislate on it. The central government regulates gaming through taxation, information technology and other laws.

Upholding the state laws confirms that states can legislate on online gaming even though the activity is carried out over the internet and across state borders.

What did the Court say on 28 percent GST on online gaming?

The Court upheld retrospective 28 percent GST on gaming companies. Operators had argued that the levy, applied to the full value of bets, was unfair and could not be applied backwards, but the Court held the levy valid.

The tax question matters because the full-value rule meant operators could owe large sums on past activity. Retrospective validity means unpaid past liabilities can be recovered, which is a serious financial issue for the sector beyond the ban itself.

Can online gaming companies still operate in India?

Games not played with stakes remain lawful, but companies offering real-money games in states with bans cannot. Operators face state-law bans and a higher indirect tax, so the model for paid skill games has narrowed sharply.

Players should note that the legal position depends on the state. Companies are likely to look to free-to-play or advertising-led models. Any further national framework would need either central legislation under another entry or state laws that follow the same pattern.

How can this be asked in UGC NET Law, CLAT PG or AIBE?

Questions may cover the dominant factor test, Entry 34 of the State List, the scope of state legislative power, and retrospective taxation. A typical question asks which entry covers betting and gambling or what test separates gaming from gambling.
