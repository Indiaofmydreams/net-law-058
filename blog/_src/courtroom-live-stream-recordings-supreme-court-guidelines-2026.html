Live streaming brought courtrooms closer to the public, and it also created a market for court clips. The Supreme Court has now stepped in to limit how recordings are reused. This guide explains the order, the principle of open justice behind it and what content creators, journalists and students should know.

What did the Supreme Court say about court proceeding recordings?

In Harshita Grover v. Union of India the Court, in an interim order reported in July 2026, barred extracting, posting or monetising recordings of court proceedings on digital platforms without permission of the Supreme Court's Secretary General or the High Court's Registrar General.

The order is interim, which means it operates until the Court decides the matter finally. Its focus is the reuse of recordings on digital platforms, particularly where clips are edited or monetised.

Can news outlets still report court proceedings?

Yes. The interim order did not affect reporting by recognised news outlets. The restriction targets extracting and monetising clips, not fair reporting of what happens in court.

What did the Court direct on 30 October 2026?

A three-judge bench led by the CJI directed the Union to frame guidelines against commercial exploitation of courtroom clips, including action in case of breaches.

This moves the issue from a one-off restraint toward a standing framework. The Union's guidelines will say what counts as exploitation and how breaches will be dealt with. Until they are framed and approved, the interim position continues.

Why does open court matter?

Public access to hearings supports transparency and accountability. The Supreme Court recognised live streaming of constitutional matters in Swapnil Tripathi (2018), which is why misuse of recordings has become a policy concern.

Open court is an aspect of fair trial and flows from Articles 19(1)(a) and 21. Open courts are not unlimited: the Court controls its own proceedings, and recordings can be used in ways that distort or sensationalise. The question now is how to keep public access while preventing commercial misuse.

Can recordings be used for memes or monetised channels?

Under the interim order, extracting and monetising recordings without permission is barred. Anyone planning to use court footage commercially should check the final guidelines and seek the required permission.

Copyright, contempt law and IT rules may also apply to misuse of clips. These intersect with the IT law topics in Unit 9.
