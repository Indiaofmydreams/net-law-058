The Sabarimala reference is the biggest religious-freedom case in India in years, and one of the two nine-judge benches pending in the Supreme Court. It is also a rare case where the outcome will matter far beyond the dispute that gave it its name. This guide sets out how the case reached a nine-judge bench, the legal questions in play and what to remember for law exams.

What is the Sabarimala reference?

It is a reference to a nine-judge Constitution Bench of questions arising from review petitions against the 2018 Sabarimala judgment. The bench is examining the essential religious practices doctrine, the meaning of constitutional morality and how Articles 25 and 26 relate to each other.

In 2018, in Indian Young Lawyers Association v. State of Kerala, a five-judge bench by a 4:1 majority struck down the custom that barred women aged 10 to 50 from entering the Sabarimala temple. Review petitions followed. Instead of deciding them, a bench in 2019 referred wider questions to a larger bench, saying the issues affected many faiths and not just Sabarimala.

Why is a nine-judge bench hearing the Sabarimala case?

In 2019 a five-judge bench kept the review petitions pending and referred broader questions on religious freedom to a larger bench. Because the questions cut across several faiths, a nine-judge bench was constituted to settle them authoritatively.

Past decisions leave unclear how far courts can decide what is "essential" to a religion, and how the right of a religious denomination under Article 26 sits beside individual rights under Article 25. A nine-judge bench can settle these questions and, if needed, correct earlier benches. The hearing began on 7 April 2026 and lasted 16 days. Disputes beyond Sabarimala, including the validity of excommunication in the Parsi community, were also examined under Articles 25 and 26.

What is the essential religious practices test?

It is a judge-made doctrine that protects under Articles 25 and 26 only those practices found essential or integral to a religion. Courts decide what is essential, which critics say makes judges arbiters of doctrine, while defenders say it prevents every custom from claiming constitutional cover.

The doctrine goes back to the 1954 Shirur Mutt decision. Since then courts have used it in many contexts, from temple practices to dress. Key points at issue in the reference, as argued, include whether courts should instead defer to the religious community's own understanding of what is essential, whether the test should be retained, narrowed or replaced, and how the State's power to reform under Article 25(2)(b) fits in. The Union challenged the test on the first day of hearings.

What is constitutional morality in the Sabarimala case?

Constitutional morality means adherence to the values of the Constitution, such as equality and dignity, as against prevailing social or religious morality. The 2018 majority used it to say that exclusion of women could not be justified by custom. The reference asks how far that idea can limit religious practice.

Respondents have argued that the Constitution itself envisages reform from within religions and that courts should be cautious about substituting their view for that of the faith. The bench is expected to say whether constitutional morality is a free-standing basis to strike down a practice or only an interpretive aid to the text of the Constitution.

When will the Sabarimala verdict come?

The bench reserved judgment on 14 May 2026 after 16 days of hearing. No date has been fixed for pronouncement, and the verdict is expected before CJI Surya Kant retires in February 2027. Justice B.V. Nagarathna was reported to have begun writing the judgment.

Until the verdict is delivered, the 2018 decision and the 2019 reference order remain the key authorities. Our page will be updated when the judgment is out.

How can this be asked in UGC NET Law, CLAT PG or AIBE?

Expect questions on Articles 25 and 26, the essential religious practices doctrine, the Shirur Mutt case, the 2018 ruling and the composition of benches. A typical objective question asks which doctrine courts use to decide protected religious practices.
