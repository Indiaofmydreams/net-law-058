Quick answer
  • 15 February 2024: a five-judge bench held the Electoral Bond Scheme unconstitutional (unanimous).
  • Reason: anonymity violates the voter's right to information (Article 19(1)(a)).
  • Unlimited corporate donations (Section 182 Companies Act amendment) were arbitrary (Article 14).
  • SBI had to disclose bond data to the Election Commission.

How political parties are funded is one of the least visible parts of democracy, and also among the most important. In 2018 the Government introduced bearer instruments that allowed donors to give anonymously. In 2024 the Supreme Court struck them down. This guide explains the reasoning, the counter-arguments and what remains open.

What did the Supreme Court decide on electoral bonds?

On 15 February 2024 a five-judge Constitution Bench unanimously struck down the Electoral Bond Scheme and the related amendments, holding that anonymous political funding violates the voter's right to information under Article 19(1)(a).

The case was Association for Democratic Reforms v. Union of India. The bench of CJI D.Y. Chandrachud and Justices Sanjiv Khanna, B.R. Gavai, J.B. Pardiwala and Manoj Misra delivered two concurring opinions. The Court said that information about funding helps citizens judge whether policy has been influenced by money, so the voter's right to know includes political contributions. It applied a proportionality test, and the Government's goal of curbing black money could be achieved by less restrictive means.

What else did the Court strike down?

The Court struck down the amendments to the Representation of the People Act, the Income-tax Act and the Companies Act, including the removal of the cap on corporate donations in Section 182, which it held to be manifestly arbitrary under Article 14.

Representation of the People Act, Section 29CExemption from disclosing bond donations
Income-tax Act, Section 13ARelated exemption conditions
Companies Act, Section 182Removal of the 7.5% cap and the disclosure of party names
Finance Act, 2017Amendments enabling the scheme

Before the amendment, a company could give only a share of its average net profit and had to disclose the recipients. The Court said that treating a loss-making company and a profit-making company alike, and removing the limit, allowed companies to buy influence. Its reasoning was that corporate donations are different from individual ones because they are more likely to seek a return.

What did the Court order after striking the scheme?

It directed the State Bank of India to stop issuing bonds and to give the Election Commission details of bonds purchased and encashed, which the Commission was to publish. The data was published in March 2024.

The Court also asked political parties to return unencashed bonds to the purchaser. The disclosure produced public lists of purchasers and recipient parties, and later petitions seeking a special investigation into alleged quid pro quo were not allowed to proceed at that stage, as reported. The judgment is also an example of the Court treating a financial and tax law as subject to a constitutional right.

What were the arguments in favour of the scheme?

The Union argued that it promoted clean money through banking channels and protected donors from retaliation, and that the right to information does not extend to every detail of political funding.

Supporters of the scheme said that earlier cash donations were untraceable and that bonds were bought through KYC-verified accounts. Critics answered that the buyer's identity was known to the State Bank and, in practice, to the Government, while the public and the opposition were kept in the dark, which created an imbalance. The Court accepted this view and rejected the proposition that donor privacy overrides the public interest, though it recognised informational privacy as a value.

What are the options now?

Reform ideas include transparent donation limits, disclosure of donors above a threshold, state funding of elections, tighter rules for electoral trusts and shareholder approval for corporate donations. The Court left Parliament free to design a valid alternative.

Past reports, such as the Law Commission's 255th Report on electoral reforms, have recommended greater transparency. Others, such as proposals for state funding, raise questions of cost and fairness to new parties.
