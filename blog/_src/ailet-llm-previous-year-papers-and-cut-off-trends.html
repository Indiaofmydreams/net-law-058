NLU Delhi's LL.M. is a small, competitive programme, so the cut-off is about rank, not a mark target. This guide shows what has been reported and how to turn it into a preparation plan. We could not verify a mark cut-off, and we describe the rank figures as reported by a third-party compilation, so check the official lists on NLU Delhi's website.

The same page lists no PwD cut-off, and its 2026 figures are projections, not results. Read these as an indication of scale: a General candidate needed to be within roughly the top 50 to 60 ranks across the lists, which tells you how few seats there are.

Why ranks and not marks?

Seats are filled through merit lists in rank order, so the number that matters is the rank of the last admitted candidate in each category. Marks needed shift with the paper's difficulty. An easy paper pushes scores up and the same rank can need many more marks. That is why mock scores must be converted into an estimated rank, not compared with a past mark.

What do previous year papers tell you?

  • The format. The current pattern is reported as 100 MCQs in 120 minutes with 0.25 negative marking. See the pattern guide. Older papers may have a different format, so check the front page.
  • Style. Standalone questions on legal knowledge and application, across the branches.
  • Heavy areas. Analyses commonly report Constitutional Law, Jurisprudence, Criminal Law, Contract and Torts as the largest blocks, but NLU Delhi publishes no weightage, so treat that as a tendency.

We do not have a verified count of topics by year, so we give none. Build your own with the method in how to analyse past papers.

How to use past papers for AILET

  1. Sit each paper in 120 minutes, with the 0.25 penalty applied.
  2. Count right, wrong and blank. See how many marks guessing cost you.
  3. Log each miss by branch and cause.
  4. Repair weak branches with the syllabus tracker and practice arena.
  5. Estimate your rank from mock performance and compare with the category ranks above.
