Which language should a six-year-old learn in? Parents, states and courts have argued over this for decades. In May 2026 the Supreme Court gave an answer anchored in free speech. The decision matters to anyone studying fundamental rights, education law and language policy, and it connects to UGC NET Paper 1 topics on NEP 2020. This guide explains it step by step.

What did the Supreme Court decide in Padam Mehta v. State of Rajasthan?

On 12 May 2026 Justices Vikram Nath and Sandeep Mehta held that a child's right to receive primary education in the mother tongue can be traced to Article 19(1)(a), and that the State cannot impose a medium of instruction at the primary level.

The case arose from a challenge in the context of Rajasthan's language policy. The bench said that education should, as far as practicable, be delivered in the language the child understands best, and that "instruction in the mother language, or a language of choice, fortifies the learner's conceptual clarity". It warned that continued State inaction risks infringing fundamental rights under Part III.

Why is this a free speech right?

The Court reasoned that freedom of speech and expression includes the right to receive information in a form the recipient can understand. Education in a language the child understands best gives conceptual clarity and meaningful access to knowledge.

The link between speech and education has been used before. The Supreme Court has read Article 19(1)(a) to include the right to know and the right to receive information. Applying that to a child in a classroom treats language not just as a subject, but as the channel through which every other subject is learned. If the channel is unfamiliar, the child's right to receive knowledge is effectively impaired.

What did the Court direct for Rajasthan?

The State must take steps to introduce Rajasthani as a subject in all government and private schools, in a phased and progressive manner.

The Court noted that Rajasthani is taught at university level in the State and found it untenable to deny recognition at school level. It saw a substantial gap between NEP 2020's commitment to home, local or regional languages as the medium of instruction and what happens in schools.

Does a language need to be in the Eighth Schedule?

No. The Court said Rajasthan could not rely on the fact that Rajasthani is not in the Eighth Schedule to justify inaction, and found it untenable to deny school-level recognition to a language already taught at university level in the State.

The Eighth Schedule lists 22 languages for purposes such as representation on the Official Language Commission, but it is not a gatekeeper for language rights. Article 29 protects the right of any section of citizens with a distinct language to conserve it, and Article 350A directs States to provide adequate facilities for instruction in the mother tongue at the primary stage for linguistic minorities. The 2026 ruling adds a free speech basis.

Which earlier cases did the Court rely on?

It relied on the 2014 Karnataka constitution bench decision recognising a child's freedom of choice over medium of instruction at primary level under Article 19(1)(a), along with other cases on language and education, and on the RTE Act and NEP 2020.

In the Karnataka case the Court had held that the State could not compel a child to study in the mother tongue where the parents wanted another medium. Padam Mehta looks at the other side of the coin: the State cannot impose a medium either way. The common thread is choice at the primary stage, grounded in the child's understanding. Section 29(2)(f) of the Right of Children to Free and Compulsory Education Act, 2009 says that, as far as practicable, the medium of instruction should be the child's mother tongue.
