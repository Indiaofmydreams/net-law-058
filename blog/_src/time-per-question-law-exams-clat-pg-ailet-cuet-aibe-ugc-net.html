Law exams are not only a test of what you know; they are a test of how much you can answer correctly in the time you have. The exams on this site give very different time per question, and the same pace that works for AIBE would sink you in CLAT PG. This guide gives the numbers and a checkpoint plan for each exam.

How much time do I get per question in law entrance exams?

CLAT PG gives about 60 seconds per question including passages. AILET and CUET PG LLM give about 72 seconds. UGC NET averages 72 seconds across 150 questions in three hours. AIBE, at 100 questions in 3 hours 30 minutes, gives roughly two minutes each.

These are averages. A direct recall question needs 15 to 20 seconds; a case-based or passage question can need two minutes. A pacing plan is a way to bank time on the first and spend it on the second.

How do I pace a 120-question CLAT PG paper?

Use checkpoints: 30 questions by minute 30, 60 by minute 60, 90 by minute 90, leaving 30 minutes for the hardest passages and review. If you are behind at any checkpoint, skip the passage you find slowest rather than rushing every remaining question.

CLAT PG questions are passage-based and the paper has no sections, so the order you attempt matters. Many candidates do the passages they find easiest first, then return. Our 5-step passage method shows how to read quickly and a mock strategy guide covers attempt order in detail.

How do I pace the CUET PG LLM paper?

Aim for 45 minutes on the first pass answering only sure questions, then 30 minutes on questions where you can remove two options, and keep the last 15 minutes for checking. With 75 questions in 90 minutes, a first-pass target of 60 seconds per question leaves time to spare.

Because every wrong answer costs a full mark in CUET PG, accuracy matters more than reaching every question. Our CUET PG mock strategy builds on this plan.

How do I manage three hours in UGC NET Paper 1 and Law together?

Most candidates do Paper 1 in 50 to 60 minutes and give Law the remaining two hours or so, with a checkpoint at 60 minutes. With no negative marking, avoid spending more than 90 seconds on any single question. Fill every answer before the last five minutes.

Paper 1 and Law (058) are one three-hour sitting. A common split is 60 minutes for 50 Paper 1 questions and 120 minutes for 100 Law questions. Practise this split in full mocks, as covered in how to split three hours between Paper 1 and Law.

How do I manage 3 hours 30 minutes in AIBE?

With about two minutes per question, use the first 100 minutes for a full pass answering what you know, then 60 minutes on harder items, and keep 30 minutes for transferring answers and checking. Keep your OMR in sync as you go, so you never have a block to fill at the end.

AIBE is offline and has no negative marking, so unanswered questions are pure waste. If the instructions allow bare Acts, use them only after your first pass, because searching early is the quickest way to run out of time. Read the BCI notice and admit card for what you may carry; our AIBE 22 guide lists what recent sessions allowed.

Train your pace in practice, not just in mocks

Pace is a skill. In the weeks before the exam, do timed sets of 20 questions at your exam's seconds-per-question target and note which questions overran. After a week you will see which question types steal time: lengthy statements, match-the-following items, or assertion-reason questions. Prepare a rule for each: for example, in statement questions, check the statement you are surest of first, and use it to remove options.

  • Week 1 to 2: untimed accuracy first.
  • Week 3 to 4: timed sets at average pace.
  • Last 2 weeks: full papers with checkpoints written on your rough sheet.

Use the Practice Arenas on this site for untimed accuracy work, then time yourself separately when you are ready; they deliberately have no timer, so you can focus on understanding first.

These are starting points, not rules. Adjust them after two or three mocks by noting the checkpoint where you most often fall behind. If you are always behind at the first checkpoint, you are spending too long settling in; if you fall behind later, you are over-investing in difficult questions.

What to do when you fall behind

  • Never speed up everywhere. Rushing every question lowers accuracy across the paper. Cut the slowest question types instead.
  • Skip the longest item first. In CLAT PG that is the passage with the densest text; in UGC NET Paper 1 it is a long reading passage or a data set.
  • Mark and move. Put a clear mark beside a skipped question so that you can find it in under five seconds.
  • Protect the last ten minutes. They are worth more spent filling and checking than spent solving one more hard question.
