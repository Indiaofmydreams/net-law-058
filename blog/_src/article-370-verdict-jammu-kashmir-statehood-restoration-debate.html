  • Ladakh as a Union Territory was upheld; whether a State can be made a UT was left open.
  • Petitions seeking a statehood timeline are pending.

In August 2019 Parliament ended the special status of Jammu and Kashmir and divided it into two Union Territories. In 2023 the Supreme Court upheld the move and, at the same time, called for statehood to return. Three years later, the second half of that ruling is still being argued. This guide explains both halves and the legal arguments.

What did the Supreme Court decide about Article 370 in December 2023?

On 11 December 2023 a five-judge Constitution Bench unanimously upheld the 2019 Presidential Orders that ended the special status of Jammu and Kashmir, holding that Article 370 was a temporary provision and that the President could issue a notification under Article 370(3).

The case was In re: Article 370 of the Constitution. CJI D.Y. Chandrachud wrote for himself and Justices Gavai, Surya Kant and Sundaresh, and Justice Kaul gave a concurring opinion. The Court reasoned that Article 370 was meant to be a transitional measure, that the Constituent Assembly of the State ceased to exist in 1957 and that the President's power survived its dissolution. It also held that the Instrument of Accession did not give the State "internal sovereignty" separate from the Constitution.

What did the Court say about statehood?

Relying on the Solicitor General's statement that statehood would be restored, the Court directed that it be restored at the earliest and that elections be held by 30 September 2024. It did not decide whether Parliament can turn a State into a Union Territory.

Elections were held in 2024 and an elected government took office, but the region remains a Union Territory. The Court left open the question of whether Article 3 allows converting a State into a Union Territory, which matters for the federal structure.

Where are the statehood petitions now?

Petitions seeking a timeline for restoration have been before the Court. Reports say the Court observed in 2025 that the situation after the Pahalgam attack has to be considered, gave the Centre time to respond and listed the matter several times in 2026.

The Centre has said that statehood will be restored at a suitable time. Petitioners argue that the assurance given to the Court needs a timeline, since a Union Territory has limited powers, for example over police and public order. As far as the sources used here show, no final order fixing a date has been reported, so check the latest order.

What are the arguments on each side?

Supporters of the 2019 decision say it integrated the region fully into the constitutional scheme and that statehood can follow when conditions allow. Critics say Article 370 could not be removed without the concurrence of the Constituent Assembly and that prolonged Union Territory status undermines federalism.

The Court dealt with the first objection by reading Article 370(3) as giving the President power to end the provision, and by treating the "Constituent Assembly" reference as having served its purpose. On the second, the debate is not purely legal: the Court's reliance on an executive assurance shows the tension between judicial review and political timing. An exam answer should state the holding, the assurance and the open question.

How does Article 3 apply?

Article 3 lets Parliament form new States and alter the areas, boundaries or names of existing States, after the President refers the Bill to the State Legislature for its views, which are not binding. It was used to create Ladakh and the J&K Union Territory; see our post on the Keralam name change for how Article 3 works in the simpler case of a name.

Because the Legislature of the State was under President's rule in 2019, Parliament exercised the Legislature's functions. Critics say this made the consultation meaningless. The Court did not accept that and said that the power under Article 356 allows Parliament to act in that way.
