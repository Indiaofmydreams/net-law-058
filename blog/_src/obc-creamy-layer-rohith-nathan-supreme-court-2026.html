Every year, thousands of OBC candidates for the civil services and other jobs are told they are "creamy layer" and therefore not entitled to reservation. The deciding factor is often a parent's salary. In 2026 the Supreme Court said that is not how the law works. The judgment matters to aspirants, to administrators and to anyone studying equality law. This guide breaks it down.

What did the Supreme Court hold in Union of India v. Rohith Nathan?

On 11 March 2026 Justices P.S. Narasimha and R. Mahadevan held that OBC creamy layer status cannot be decided on parental salary alone. Status and category of the parent's post are essential, and a 2004 clarificatory letter could not add an income test the 1993 memorandum did not contain.

The case began with UPSC candidates who claimed non-creamy layer status. The Department of Personnel and Training excluded them because of their parents' salaries. Many of the parents worked in public sector undertakings, banks or similar bodies whose posts had not been formally declared equivalent to government posts. The Central Administrative Tribunal and the Madras, Delhi and Kerala High Courts sided with the candidates, and the Union appealed. The appeals were dismissed.

What is the creamy layer?

The creamy layer is the socially and economically advanced section of an Other Backward Class that is excluded from OBC reservation benefits. Indra Sawhney (1992) required its exclusion and the 1993 Office Memorandum lists the categories.

The idea is that reservation under Article 16(4) is meant for backward classes as a whole, not for the families who have already advanced. The 1993 memorandum, issued after Indra Sawhney, sets out categories by the status of the parent, for example children of constitutional office holders and Group A officers, and adds a residual income or wealth test. Importantly, under the explanations to that memorandum, salary and agricultural income are kept out of the income test. The Court said they are "consciously kept outside the common pool".

What was wrong with the 2004 clarification?

It applied a separate income test, treating a candidate as creamy layer if the parent's salary alone or other income alone exceeded 2.5 lakh rupees for three years, where the parent's PSU or private post had no equivalence to a government post. The Court held this diluted the 1993 memorandum and was discriminatory.

The reasoning rests on a basic point of administrative law: an executive clarification can explain a rule but cannot change it. Where a parent holds a post in a public sector undertaking or bank without a formal equivalence, the 2004 letter pushed the candidate into an income test that children of Group C and D government employees never faced. That, the Court held, treats similarly placed people differently without a rational basis.

What were the Court's directions?

The DoPT must reconsider the candidates' claims within six months without counting parental salary income, and supernumerary posts are to be created where needed to accommodate eligible candidates.

Creating supernumerary posts is a recurring tool of the Court in service matters. It avoids displacing candidates who were selected in the meantime while giving relief to those who were wrongly excluded. The Union asked for a clarification of the scope of the ruling, and on 6 November 2026 the Court said the March judgment does not reopen concluded selections.

How does this connect to reservation law more broadly?

The ruling applies the equality code in Articles 14, 15 and 16 and the Indra Sawhney framework: reservation for backward classes under Article 16(4), exclusion of the creamy layer, and a cap of 50 per cent. It sits beside questions about sub-classification and income criteria for the economically weaker sections.

It also shows how courts police the executive's use of office memoranda. Many of the most important entitlements in service law are created and administered by memoranda, not by statute. When a memorandum is clarified by a later letter, the question is always whether the letter is an authentic interpretation or a covert amendment.

Expect questions on Indra Sawhney, Article 16(4), the creamy layer concept and the limits of executive clarifications.
