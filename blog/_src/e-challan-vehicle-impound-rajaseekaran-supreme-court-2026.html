Millions of Indian drivers have an e-challan sitting unpaid in the system. Because the notice arrives by SMS or app and not from a person at the roadside, many ignore it. The Supreme Court's 2026 directions try to change that incentive. This guide explains the order, how e-challans work under the Motor Vehicles Act and the legal points to watch.

What did the Supreme Court direct on unpaid e-challans?

In S. Rajaseekaran v. Union of India, directions reported on 28 September 2026 require States and Union Territories to impound vehicles with unpaid e-challans and freeze related online vehicle services until the dues are cleared.

The petitioner, S. Rajaseekaran, has been associated with road safety litigation before the Court for years. The case is a continuing one, so the directions form part of a larger effort to enforce traffic law and reduce deaths. The details of timelines and exceptions are in the order, which should be read before advising anyone.

How much is unpaid, and why does that matter?

Reports say about 49,194 crore rupees is recoverable across the country and about 26,175 crore rupees had been recovered, leaving a very large pending amount.

The scale explains the Court's concern. If fines are not collected, the deterrent effect disappears, and compliance becomes optional. The Court's approach is to attach a practical consequence to non-payment, which is the loss of the ability to use or transfer the vehicle.

How do e-challans work under the Motor Vehicles Act?

Electronic enforcement through cameras and devices is allowed under the Motor Vehicles Act, which provides for challans to be sent electronically. The owner can pay the fine, compound the offence or contest it before a court.

The 2019 amendment brought in a provision on electronic enforcement of road safety, so evidence from cameras and speed devices can support a challan. Payment of a fine through compounding closes the matter in respect of that offence. Contesting the challan sends it to the court, where the owner can argue that the vehicle was not at the place or that the details are wrong. Many challans are for the registered owner even when someone else was driving, which is a common source of disputes.

Can a vehicle be seized without notice?

The Court's direction is addressed to the authorities, but basic fairness still applies: notice and an opportunity to explain should come first. In a separate 2026 ruling, Hari Dutta Sharma v. State of U.P., the Court condemned the forcible seizure of a vehicle at about one in the night and ordered a refund with interest and compensation.

The two cases read together show the balance. The State can enforce payment, but it must do so within the law and with respect for the owner's rights. Property is protected by Article 300A, which says no person can be deprived of property except by authority of law. A direction to impound for unpaid dues will need a legal basis in the statute and rules, and owners can challenge arbitrary action. Why does this matter for law students?

It shows the Court using continuing directions to enforce road safety law and raises questions about due process, proportionality and the line between administration and adjudication.

The Court has used a similar method in cases about trauma care and road accidents, such as the one in our SaveLIFE Foundation post. Supporters say only judicial pressure forces States to act; critics say the Court should not design administrative systems. A balanced answer gives both views and ends with the point that rights, such as the right to be heard, survive in enforcement.
