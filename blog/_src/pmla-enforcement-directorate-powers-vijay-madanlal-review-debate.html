Quick answer
  • Vijay Madanlal (2022) upheld the ED's powers, Section 45 twin conditions and Section 50 statements.
  • ECIR is not an FIR; but grounds of arrest must be given (Pankaj Bansal, 2023).
  • Critics: reverse burden, hard bail, low conviction rates.
  • A review on limited points is pending.

The Prevention of Money Laundering Act, 2002 gives the Enforcement Directorate strong powers, and opposition leaders, businesspeople and activists have all faced its provisions. Supporters say that these powers are needed against complex financial crime; critics say that bail is almost impossible. This guide explains the key sections and the leading cases.

What did Vijay Madanlal Choudhary v. Union of India decide?

In July 2022 a three-judge bench upheld the core of the PMLA, including the Enforcement Directorate's powers of arrest, search and seizure under Sections 17 and 19, the twin conditions for bail under Section 45 and the use of statements recorded under Section 50.

The Court reasoned that the Act is a special law with an international origin, aimed at the proceeds of crime, and that the offence of money laundering is separate from the scheduled offence. It held that the ED is not a police force, so statements to it are not barred by Article 20(3) in the way statements to the police are. These holdings are the main source of the present debate.

What are the twin conditions for bail?

Under Section 45 the Court must be satisfied that there are reasonable grounds for believing the accused is not guilty and that the accused is not likely to commit an offence while on bail.

In Nikesh Tarachand Shah (2017) the Court struck down an earlier version of the twin conditions, and Parliament amended the section in 2018. The 2022 judgment upheld the amended version. The Court has also said that the conditions do not remove the court's duty to apply Article 21 when delay is long.

Is an ECIR the same as an FIR?

No. The Court held that an Enforcement Case Information Report is an internal document and not an FIR, so the ED need not supply it to the accused, though the grounds of arrest must be communicated.

Critics say that without the ECIR the accused cannot know the case. Supporters say that supplying it early can compromise an investigation. This is one of the points on which the review was heard. The later decisions on written grounds, including Pankaj Bansal (2023), seek to answer the concern that an arrested person must know the basis of the arrest. What safeguards has the Court added since?

In Pankaj Bansal (2023) and Prabir Purkayastha (2024) it required written grounds of arrest. In several bail rulings it stressed that Article 21 applies and that bail is the rule, even under special statutes, when trial is delayed.

Examples include V. Senthil Balaji (2024) and the bail granted in Delhi excise policy cases, where the Court noted the long period of custody. These decisions show a pattern: the Court leaves the statute standing but uses constitutional principles to soften its effect. Some commentators say this is the right balance; others say that only a change in the law can fix the problem.

Is the 2022 judgment under review, and what are the criticisms?

A review petition was heard, and the Court issued notice on limited points, including the non-supply of the ECIR and the reverse burden of proof. Critics also cite low conviction numbers and the use of the Act in political disputes; the Government says that the Act targets proceeds of crime and that attachments recover large sums.

Both sides cite statistics, and students should be careful to present them as claims of each side rather than as settled facts. The legal issues for any answer are the nature of the ED's powers, the compatibility of the reverse burden with Article 21 and the role of courts in bail.

How can this be asked in UGC NET Law, CLAT PG or AIBE?

Expect questions on Sections 3, 19, 24, 45 and 50 PMLA, the cases above and the difference between a scheduled offence and money laundering.
