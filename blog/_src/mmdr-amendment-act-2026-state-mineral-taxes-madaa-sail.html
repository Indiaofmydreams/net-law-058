Quick answer
  • The MMDR Amendment Act, 2026 received assent on 17 August 2026.
  • New Section 9D limits State taxes, cesses and levies on mineral rights and mineral-bearing land unless they follow Central conditions.
  • Past levies not yet deposited or recovered are deemed invalid; amounts already recovered are not refunded.
  • It is widely read as a response to Mineral Area Development Authority v. SAIL (2024).

Mining States say minerals are their wealth and they should be able to tax mining activity. The Centre says a national mineral policy needs one predictable cost structure. In 2024 the Supreme Court sided largely with the States. In August 2026 Parliament passed an amendment that pushes back. This guide explains what the Act does, the case behind it and the constitutional questions it raises.

What is the MMDR Amendment Act, 2026?

It amends the Mines and Minerals (Development and Regulation) Act, 1957. It received assent on 17 August 2026 and limits the power of States to tax mineral rights and mineral-bearing lands unless the tax follows conditions set by the Centre.

The Act makes three main changes. Section 2 extends the Union's declaration of expediency, which is the basis of the Centre's control under Entry 54 of List I, to mines and mineral-bearing lands. Section 3 defines "mineral bearing land". A new Section 9D then restricts State levies, and a new clause (ta) in Section 13 gives rule-making power to the Central Government. The Act applies to the whole country, and its reach into past levies is what has made it controversial.

What was the Mineral Area Development Authority v. SAIL case?

In 2024 a nine-judge Constitution Bench held that States can tax mineral rights under Entry 50 of List II, that royalty is not a tax and that Parliament's limits under Entry 50 must be found in a law on mineral development.

EntryListSubject
Entry 54List I (Union)Regulation of mines and mineral development, to the extent declared expedient by Parliament
Entry 50List II (State)Taxes on mineral rights, subject to any limitations imposed by Parliament by law relating to mineral development
Entry 49List II (State)Taxes on lands and buildings

The earlier position came from India Cement v. State of Tamil Nadu (1990), which treated royalty as a tax. The 2024 judgment overruled that part and held that royalty is a contractual payment for the right to extract minerals, not a tax. It also held that the 1957 Act did not, as it stood, contain limits on State taxation, so States could levy taxes on mineral rights and on mineral-bearing land. The Court pointed out that Parliament could impose limits by legislation. The 2026 amendment is read as Parliament doing exactly that.

What does new Section 9D do?

Section 9D restricts State taxes, cesses and levies on mineral rights and mineral-bearing lands unless they comply with conditions laid down under the Act. It is the core of the amendment.

The reasoning of its supporters is that Entry 50 itself says State taxes on mineral rights are "subject to any limitations imposed by Parliament by law relating to mineral development". So a Central limit is within the scheme of the Constitution. The Centre also argues that uncoordinated State levies would raise costs, distort investment and hurt downstream industries such as steel and power. Rules under the new Section 13 clause (ta) will fill in the details, so the practical impact depends on what those rules say and when they are notified.

What happens to levies imposed in the past?

The Act says levies imposed but not deposited or recovered before commencement are deemed invalid at all material times, notwithstanding any court judgment, while amounts already recovered are not refunded.

This is the part that raises the most legal questions. A legislature can remove the basis of a judgment by changing the law with retrospective effect, but it cannot simply overrule a judgment. The settled test, from cases such as Cheviti Venkanna Yadav and Madan Mohan Pathak, is that Parliament may cure the defect that the court found, but may not directly nullify a binding decision. Here, the 2024 ruling found that the Act contained no limit. The 2026 Act inserts a limit and applies it backwards. Whether that is a valid cure or a direct override is the question a court may have to decide if the Act is challenged. The "not refunded" proviso is meant to avoid reopening settled transactions.

Why does this matter for federalism and for exam answers?

It tests the balance between Entry 54 of List I and Entry 50 of List II, the limits on retrospective validation and the doctrine of separation of powers. It is also a live example of Parliament and the Court conversing over the same subject.

Some commentators describe the Act as protecting national uniformity, while some State governments and critics say it undercuts the fiscal autonomy that the 2024 judgment recognised. Both views belong in a balanced answer. Remember that the Act is new, so as far as the reports used for this guide go, no final judicial ruling on it has been given. Check for any challenge before writing a definitive view in an exam or research paper. The same theme of Parliament acting on the back of a court ruling appears in the IBC amendment.

How can this be asked in UGC NET Law, CLAT PG or AIBE?

Expect questions on Entries 50 and 54, the nature of royalty, legislative validation and the distribution of legislative power under Article 246.
