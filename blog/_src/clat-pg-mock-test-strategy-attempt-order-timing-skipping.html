CLAT PG has no sections, 120 passage-based questions and about a minute for each one, including reading. That makes the order in which you attempt passages and the rule by which you skip questions as important as what you know. This guide gives an attempt order, timing targets, skipping rules for the 0.25 penalty and a review routine built for passage-based mocks.

In what order should I attempt the CLAT PG paper?

Scan the whole paper for two or three minutes, rate each passage as easy, medium or hard by subject and length, and attempt easy ones first, then medium, then hard. Because CLAT PG has no sections, you are free to choose. Finish your first pass by around minute 80.

CLAT PG is one integrated paper of 120 passage-based questions in 120 minutes, as set out in our exam pattern guide. That averages 60 seconds per question including reading. Because passages differ in length and subject, the order you attempt decides how much time you spend on the questions you are best placed to win.

How do I decide whether a passage is easy, medium or hard?

Judge by subject familiarity and length, not by how intimidating the topic sounds. A short passage on a doctrine you know is easy; a long passage on an unfamiliar statute is hard. Spend ten seconds per passage on this decision, and avoid reading it fully until you choose to attempt it.

  • Easy: subject you have studied; short or moderate length; direct questions.
  • Medium: familiar subject but dense reading, or moderate subject with short text.
  • Hard: unfamiliar subject; very long text; questions that need several steps.

Practise this triage in your mocks and note after each one whether your 'easy' set was actually easy. After three mocks you will calibrate. The technique for reading a passage once and extracting answers is in our 5-step passage method.

When should I skip a question in CLAT PG?

Skip when you cannot remove at least two options for a specific reason after about ninety seconds. With +1 and −0.25, the break-even accuracy is 20 percent, so guesses with two options removed pay, while blind guesses barely do. Mark the question and return only if time remains.

The arithmetic is in our negative marking guide: a two-option guess is worth about +0.38 marks, a blind guess about +0.06. The skipping rule therefore depends on elimination quality, not on topic difficulty. If a question still looks open after 90 seconds, you are probably burning time that could earn more elsewhere.

How many marks should I aim for in a CLAT PG mock?

Set targets by stage: in your first mocks, aim for accuracy above 70 percent on attempted questions; in the middle, raise attempts without letting accuracy fall below 75 percent; in the last mocks, hold the best balance. A good final-mock range depends on the cut-off for your target NLU, so check recent trends.

Cut-offs differ by NLU and category, and score expectations vary yearly. Our guide Is 60 a good score in CLAT PG? explains how scores relate to outcomes, and the past paper analysis shows how the paper has behaved.

How should I review a CLAT PG mock?

Review passage by passage: note whether each wrong answer was a reading error, a knowledge gap or a misapplied rule, and whether your attempt order helped. Redo every wrong passage after a week without looking at the answers. Track your seconds per passage and the order you attempted.

Use the error log in our mock analysis guide with two extra columns for CLAT PG: passage subject and time spent on the passage. After four or five mocks you will see which subjects eat your time and which passages are traps. This beats re-reading the same notes. For wider context see the 1-month or 4-month study plan.

Mistakes that cost marks in CLAT PG mocks

  • Reading every passage fully before choosing. You burn five minutes on passages you will not attempt.
  • Chasing a hard passage because you started it. Sunk time is not a reason to keep going.
  • Marking the OMR at the end. Transfer as you go; a block left to the last minute invites shading errors.
  • Treating every passage as equal. A passage with six questions deserves more time than one with three.
  • Not recording your attempt order. Without it you cannot tell whether your order helped or hurt.

Three habits of high scorers

  • They triage every paper in the first three minutes, and stick to their order.
  • They treat time as a budget for each passage, and move on when it is spent.
  • They review for patterns, not only for the answers they missed.
