For information only

Marriage equality is a sensitive and contested subject. This post explains the legal position and both lines of argument without taking a side.

After the Supreme Court decriminalised same-sex relations in 2018, the next question was whether such couples can marry. In 2023 the Court answered that it is for Parliament. This guide explains that decision, what the Court did say about rights and how supporters and opponents argue.

What did the Supreme Court decide in Supriyo Chakraborty?

On 17 October 2023 a five-judge Constitution Bench unanimously declined to recognise a fundamental right to marry or to read same-sex marriage into the Special Marriage Act, holding that it is for Parliament to decide, and refused to strike the Act down.

The bench of CJI D.Y. Chandrachud, and Justices S.K. Kaul, S.R. Bhat, Hima Kohli and P.S. Narasimha delivered four opinions. They agreed that the Court cannot change the statute by reading words into it, but differed on other points. Chandrachud and Kaul would have recognised a right to a civil union, while the majority did not. Throughout, the Court accepted that queer persons have the right to be free from discrimination.

What did the Union assure the Court?

The Solicitor General said a committee headed by the Cabinet Secretary would examine the rights and entitlements of same-sex couples without legal marriage, such as ration cards, joint bank accounts and medical decisions. The Court recorded this.

IssueOutcome in 2023
Fundamental right to marryNot recognised
Special Marriage Act, 1954Upheld; not read to include same-sex couples
Civil unionMinority view favoured it; majority did not
Adoption by queer couplesRestriction upheld by 3:2
CommitteeUnion to examine entitlements through a Cabinet Secretary-led committee

The Court also directed that the police must not harass queer couples and that there should be no forced medical treatment or conversion therapy. These are directions about dignity and are separate from marriage.

Was the judgment reviewed?

Reports say the Court dismissed review petitions in January 2025, finding no error apparent on the record.

A review is not an appeal, and the Court applies a narrow test. The dismissal ended the immediate litigation, though a fresh challenge could be filed on different grounds, and legislative change remains possible. The committee's work has been reported on at points, but as far as the sources used here show, its outcome is not settled, so check official updates.

What are the arguments on each side?

Supporters of recognition argue equality, dignity and the right to choose a partner after Navtej Singh Johar (2018) and Puttaswamy (2017). Opponents argue that marriage is a matter of legislative policy and social and religious tradition, and that courts should not rewrite personal laws.

The majority adopted the second view on the question of institutional role, not on the merits of equality. That distinction is important in an exam answer. The Court did not say that same-sex relationships are unlawful or lesser; it said that creating a new legal institution is a legislative function. The wider rights debate also appears in our post on the Transgender Persons Amendment Act.

How can this be asked in UGC NET Law, CLAT PG or AIBE?

Expect questions on the Special Marriage Act, Navtej Singh Johar, the separation of powers and the Court's power to read down a statute.
