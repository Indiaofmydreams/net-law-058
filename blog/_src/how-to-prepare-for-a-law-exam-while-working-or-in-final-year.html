Quick answers
  • Hours: 15 to 20 a week while working.
  • Structure: two fixed weekday blocks plus long weekend sessions.
  • Small gaps: recall with cards, not new reading.
  • Timeline: longer, so choose the exam date that fits.

Most law aspirants do not have unlimited time. They work in firms or courts, or they are in their final year of LL.B. with classes and internals. The plans in most guides assume eight hours a day. This guide is for everyone else: a realistic weekly timetable, ways to use small gaps, and how to choose an exam that matches the time you actually have.

Can I prepare for a law exam while working full-time?

Yes, if you protect two fixed study blocks every weekday and use weekends for long sessions and mocks. Eight to ten focused hours a week is not enough for a competitive entrance; fifteen to twenty usually is. Plan for a longer timeline of three to five months instead of one or two.

Working candidates fail not for lack of intelligence but for lack of a stable routine. The best approach is boring and fixed: the same two time slots each weekday, the same long block each weekend, and a weekly review. Expect to extend your timeline compared with a full-time aspirant, and choose the exam date that gives you that time; for example, CUET PG LLM (from 2 March 2027, tentative) suits a working candidate better than an exam three weeks away.

How many hours a week do I need if I am working?

Plan for fifteen to twenty hours a week: about two hours on each weekday and five to six hours on each weekend day. Sustained effort over four months beats a short burst. If you can only manage ten hours, extend the timeline and narrow your goals to core subjects.

How do I prepare for a law exam in the final year of LL.B.?

Tie your preparation to your semester: study the subjects your classes cover now, and treat internal and semester exams as revision for the entrance. Add one extra subject block a week from your syllabus tracker, and take a mock every two weeks from the third month of preparation.

The final year is an advantage, because the subjects are fresh. Match your reading to the subject your classes are on that month, then add timed questions on it. Use university study leave for focused revision and mocks. Final-year students are eligible for several of these exams, such as AILET and CUET PG (as reported for recent cycles), so check each notice for the degree-proof timeline.

How can I use my commute and small gaps to study?

Use short gaps for recall, not reading: flash cards of cases and sections, a one-page sheet, or mental recall of the day's topic. Reading new material on a crowded commute rarely sticks. Fifteen minutes of recall twice a day adds about three hours of revision a week.

  • Case cards on your phone or in your pocket (see how to memorise cases and sections).
  • Audio: record yourself reading your one-page sheets and play them back.
  • Mental recall: list the exceptions to a rule or the elements of an offence from memory.
  • Question apps or the Practice Arenas in this site: untimed, so you can do ten questions in a gap.

What should I cut from my routine to make time?

Cut low-value study first: re-reading finished notes, passive videos, social media and long group discussions. Keep the high-value activities: first-time coverage of core subjects, practice questions with review, mocks and recall. If you must drop a subject, drop an occasional one, not a core one.

Before cutting, track one week of actual time. Many candidates find two to four hours a week lost to scrolling. Replace those with fixed study slots rather than hoping you will 'find' time. Give your family or team a clear schedule so that your fixed blocks are protected.

At about 17 hours a week, this plan totals roughly 200 hours: enough for a first-time pass through a law entrance syllabus plus eight to ten hours of mocks. If your timeline is shorter, protect the core subjects and drop the occasional ones. If your job has a busy season, build a lighter plan for those weeks and extend the timeline rather than skipping the exam.

A weekly review that takes ten minutes

Every Sunday evening, answer four questions in writing: What did I cover this week? What did I revise? What score did my last mock show? What is the single change for next week? This small ritual keeps a part-time plan honest and prevents drift, which is the main enemy of working aspirants.
