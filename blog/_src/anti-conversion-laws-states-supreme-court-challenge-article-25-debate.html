Quick answer
  • Stainislaus (1977): propagation under Article 25 does not include a right to convert others.
  • Hadiya (2018): choice of faith is part of personal autonomy.
  • Many States have conversion laws; the Supreme Court has issued notices on challenges, including to laws in 12 States.
  • Debate: preventing coercion versus freedom of conscience.
For information only

Conversion laws are a sensitive and contested subject. This post explains the legal position and both sets of arguments without endorsing either.

Roughly a dozen States have laws that regulate religious conversion, and more have followed. Each has been challenged on constitutional grounds, and the Supreme Court has been asked to settle the matter. This guide explains what the laws do, what the Court said in the leading cases and how the debate runs.

What do anti-conversion laws do?

They prohibit conversion by force, fraud, allurement or inducement, and in many States require advance notice to the authorities and give the State power to investigate. Penalties are higher where women, minors or members of Scheduled Castes and Tribes are involved.

Some laws also treat marriage solely for the purpose of conversion as an offence, and some let relatives or other persons file complaints. The details differ by State. Uttar Pradesh's law of 2021 was amended in 2024 to raise penalties, and Rajasthan passed a law in 2025. Because texts vary, an exam answer should refer to a specific State unless the question speaks generally.

What did Rev. Stainislaus v. State of Madhya Pradesh decide?

In 1977 the Supreme Court upheld the Orissa and Madhya Pradesh laws and held that the right to propagate religion under Article 25 does not include a right to convert another person, though a person may spread their faith.

The Court in Stainislaus reasoned that forcible conversion would infringe the freedom of conscience of the person converted, and that public order allowed the State to act.

What is the other side of the argument?

Critics say the laws are vague, shift the burden of proof, allow third parties to complain and chill free choice of faith, protected in cases such as Hadiya (2018) and Puttaswamy (2017). They also say existing criminal law already covers coercion and fraud.

They point to terms like "allurement" which can include a gift or an offer of free education, and to notice requirements that force a person to disclose a private decision. Supporters answer that vulnerable people are exploited through inducement, that the laws target the means of conversion and not the conversion itself, and that States have a duty to protect public order and conscience. Both arguments can be tested against Article 25, which is subject to public order, morality and health.

What is pending in the Supreme Court?

Petitions challenge several State laws. Reports say the Court issued notice in 2025 on Rajasthan's law and in 2026 on a petition by the National Council of Churches in India challenging laws in 12 States. Interim relief applications have been pending.

Another batch, led by a civil rights group, has also challenged the State laws and sought interim relief since April 2025. The Court has asked the Union and States for responses. Until a decision, the laws remain in force, subject to any court orders in individual cases.
