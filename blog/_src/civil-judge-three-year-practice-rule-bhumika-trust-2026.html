If you plan to join the judicial services, this is the most important recent ruling for you. In 2025 the Supreme Court restored a three-year practice requirement, and many young graduates found themselves locked out of upcoming exams. Review petitions followed, and the 2026 judgment reshapes the route into the judiciary. This guide explains the rule in plain language, with the dates that matter.

What did the Supreme Court decide in Bhumika Trust v. Union of India?

On 21 August 2026 the Court dismissed review petitions against its May 2025 ruling but modified it: the three-year Bar practice requirement for Civil Judge (Junior Division) becomes one year of practice plus structured training and clerkship, with a transitional scheme until 31 March 2027.

The earlier ruling was in the long-running All India Judges Association litigation, which has shaped judicial service conditions for decades. It restored the three-year practice rule. The Court kept the principle that new judges need exposure to the legal profession, but accepted that the sudden return of the rule without any transition caused hardship to young lawyers and law graduates. Its answer is a middle path that replaces part of the Bar practice with supervised training inside the system.

Can fresh law graduates apply for Civil Judge exams now?

During the transitional period, for notifications issued from 20 May 2025 to 31 March 2027, law graduates may apply without the practice requirement. They are deemed to have completed one year of practice, and selected candidates serve as trainee judicial officers.

No certificate of practice is needed for that period. The dates matter: what counts is the date of the recruitment notification, not the date of the exam. A candidate applying under a notification issued before 1 April 2027 falls under the transitional scheme. A state that notifies after that date applies the permanent rule.

What is the rule from 1 April 2027?

For notifications on or after 1 April 2027, candidates need at least one year of actual practice in the district courts, verified by a certificate, followed by one year of academy training and one year of clerkship. LL.M. study does not count as practice.

The practice certificate is tied to recorded participation in effective judicial proceedings under a mechanism the Court prescribed, so merely enrolling with a Bar Council is not enough. Time spent on higher education such as an LL.M. does not count.

How long are training and clerkship, and what pay do trainees get?

Selected candidates complete one year at the State Judicial Academy and then a clerkship, six months under a district judge and six months under a sitting High Court judge. Trainees receive half the gross pay of a Judicial Magistrate First Class during this period.

Confirmation to a regular post, with the full pay scale, depends on a satisfactory and reasoned evaluation report from the supervising High Court judge. Under the transitional scheme both the academy year and the clerkship year count as years of Bar practice, which is how the Court squares the new route with its principle of prior exposure.

Was the decision unanimous?

No. CJI Surya Kant and Justice A.G. Masih formed the majority. Justice K. Vinod Chandran dissented, finding no error in the earlier judgment and objecting to the burden of two years of half-pay training and clerkship on recruits.

His dissent makes three points. Two years on half pay burdens recruits and the exchequer, and strains courts that are already short of judges. Making confirmation depend on one High Court judge's satisfaction creates arbitrary hurdles and two classes of recruits. And judicial service cannot be equated with the civil services, because a judicial officer exercises independent adjudicatory discretion from the first day. The scheme will be reviewed after five years, with data on recruitment quality, training and officer performance.
