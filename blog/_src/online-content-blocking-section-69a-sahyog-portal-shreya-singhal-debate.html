Quick answer
  • Section 69A allows blocking on Article 19(2) grounds, with recorded reasons and a review committee; upheld in Shreya Singhal (2015).
  • Section 79(3)(b) notices through the Sahyog portal are a different route with fewer safeguards, critics say.
  • Karnataka High Court reportedly upheld the portal against X Corp in September 2025.
  • Debate: speed and safety versus over-removal and free speech.

When a post disappears from social media, the reason is usually a government direction or a company decision. Indian law has two main routes for government takedown, and which one applies matters for your rights. This guide explains Section 69A, the Sahyog portal and the arguments about each.

What is Section 69A of the IT Act?

It lets the Central Government direct blocking of online information in the interest of sovereignty, defence, security of the State, friendly relations, public order or to prevent incitement to an offence, following the Blocking Rules, 2009 with reasons recorded in writing.

The 2009 Rules set up a designated officer and a committee of senior officials to examine each request. Orders are normally confidential, which critics say stops the affected speaker from challenging them. The Rules also provide for emergency blocking, to be reviewed afterwards. The grounds mirror Article 19(2), which lists the reasonable restrictions on free speech.

What did Shreya Singhal (2015) say about it?

The Supreme Court struck down Section 66A but upheld Section 69A and the Blocking Rules, because they contain safeguards: grounds limited to Article 19(2), a review committee and written reasons.

ProvisionShreya Singhal outcome
Section 66AStruck down as vague and overbroad
Section 69A and Blocking RulesUpheld: narrow grounds, reasons, review
Section 79(3)(b)Read down: intermediary must act only on a court order or a government notification within Article 19(2)

The reading down of Section 79(3)(b) is the key to the next debate: the Court required "actual knowledge" through a court order or a lawful government notification, so that intermediaries are not forced to judge legality themselves.

What is the Sahyog portal?

It is a Central Government portal through which authorised agencies send takedown notices to intermediaries under Section 79(3)(b) of the IT Act. Critics say it bypasses the safeguards of Section 69A, and the Government says it helps coordinate removal of unlawful content.

Under Section 79, an intermediary keeps "safe harbour" immunity from liability for user content only if it removes unlawful content after notice. Critics argue that using Section 79 notices to remove content effectively skips the review committee and reasoned order that Section 69A requires. The Government answers that Section 79 is for notices on unlawful content in general, and Section 69A is for blocking orders.

What did the Karnataka High Court decide in the X Corp case?

Reports say that in September 2025 the High Court dismissed X Corp's petition, held that the portal and notices under Section 79(3)(b) were valid and said that a foreign company cannot claim Article 19 rights. X was reported to have appealed.

The court was reported to have held that Section 69A and Section 79 operate in different fields. The appeal is a leading example of the dispute, and the outcome could shape intermediary liability. Check the status of the appeal and any Supreme Court involvement. The wider free speech theme, including the limit on creating offences, also appears in our post on hate speech.

What are the arguments on each side?

For wider takedown powers: unlawful content spreads fast, and slow processes allow harm, including fraud and incitement. Against: unclear standards, no hearing for the speaker, little transparency and the risk of removal of lawful speech.

Suggestions made in commentary include publishing blocking statistics, giving notice to the originator, creating independent oversight and ensuring that Section 79 notices meet the standard in Shreya Singhal. An exam answer should link both routes to Article 19(1)(a) and 19(2) and to the safeguards the Court found essential.
