Many LLM aspirants keep the judiciary as a backup or a main goal. It is a demanding exam but offers prestige and stability. This guide covers eligibility, the practice rule and pay in plain terms.

Can I sit for judicial services after LLM?

Yes, an LLB (or integrated degree) is the required qualification; an LLM is not required. Most states ask for enrolment as an advocate and, following the Supreme Court's 2025 direction, a minimum of three years of practice for entry-level posts, as reported.

Rules differ by state, so check the High Court or Public Service Commission notice. See our judiciary page.

What is the three-year practice rule?

In 2025 the Supreme Court restored a minimum practice requirement for the entry-level Civil Judge (Junior Division), reported as three years, which states have been implementing. Check your state's notice for how it applies to you.

Practice may be shown by certificates from senior advocates or judicial officers. Plan to join practice early if this is your goal, and note that the AIBE certificate of practice is a part of enrolment.

What are the exam stages?

Most states use a preliminary exam, a mains written exam and an interview. Papers cover procedural and substantive law, language and sometimes local law. Stages and weightage differ by state, so read the notification.

StageUsual content
PrelimsObjective test on core laws
MainsDescriptive papers, judgment writing, language
InterviewPersonality and legal awareness

What does a civil judge earn?

Pay follows the judicial pay structure by state, with entry basic often reported in the range of roughly ₹70,000 to ₹80,000 or more, plus allowances. Exact scales differ by state, so confirm in the notice.

Perquisites include housing and official facilities. Posting can be in smaller towns.

How does promotion work?

Civil Judge (Junior Division) can rise to Senior Division and then to District Judge through seniority and merit, with a share of District Judge posts filled by direct recruitment from advocates under Article 233.
