The method in one line

Read for the question, find the legal principle, test each option against the passage, remove extremes and then decide. Use the same steps every time.

CLAT PG is a passage-based paper: 120 questions in 2 hours. Candidates who know the law still lose marks because they read slowly or choose an option that is true in general but not supported by the passage. A fixed method fixes both problems. This is a five-step routine you can practise this week.

The five steps

  1. Skim the questions first (10 seconds). Note the type: is it "according to the passage", "which can be inferred", "which weakens", or "what is the principle"?
  2. Read the passage once, marking the claim and the reason. Underline the author's main point and the legal rule or case mentioned. Ignore examples on the first read.
  3. State the principle in your own words. One line: "Parliament can amend, but not destroy the Constitution's core." If you cannot, reread the key sentence.
  4. Test each option against the passage. Is it supported, contradicted or just outside the passage? Only supported options survive.
  5. Eliminate extremes, then decide. Options with "always", "never", "only" or "all" are usually too strong unless the passage says the same.

Worked example

In Kesavananda Bharati v. State of Kerala (1973), a bench of thirteen judges held, by a narrow majority, that Parliament's power to amend the Constitution under Article 368 is wide but not unlimited. Parliament may change many provisions, including fundamental rights, but it cannot alter or destroy the basic structure of the Constitution. The Court did not list the basic structure exhaustively. Later decisions identified features such as judicial review, the rule of law and the separation of powers as part of it.

Q1. Which statement is best supported by the passage?

  1. Parliament cannot amend fundamental rights at all.
  2. Parliament can amend the Constitution, but not so as to destroy its basic structure.
  3. The Court gave a complete list of basic features.
  4. The decision was unanimous.

Answer: B. A is too strong: the passage says fundamental rights can be changed. C contradicts "did not list the basic structure exhaustively". D contradicts "narrow majority".

Q2. Which of these would the passage treat as a limit on Parliament's amending power?

  1. Changing the language of a schedule
  2. Abolishing judicial review altogether
  3. Amending a fundamental right in a minor way
  4. Adding a new article on a social subject

Answer: B. The passage names judicial review as part of the basic structure, so removing it altogether would breach the limit. The other options are ordinary amendments.

The traps to watch for

TrapWhat it looks likeFix
True but unsupportedA correct legal statement that the passage never makesAsk "where in the passage is this?"
Extreme wording"Always", "never", "only", "all"Check whether the passage is that absolute
Reversed relationshipCause and effect swappedReread the sentence that links the two ideas
Half-right optionFirst half matches, second half adds a new claimRead to the end of every option
Outside knowledgeYou answer from memory of the case, not the passageCheck whether the question says "according to the passage"

How to practise this in the last days

  • Do two timed sets of 20 passage questions a day. Give yourself 20 minutes per set.
  • After each set, mark the sentence in the passage that decided every answer, including the ones you got right.
  • Keep an error log with three headings: misread, did not know the law, fell for a trap. Fix the biggest category first.
  • Use the practice arena, which explains why each wrong option is wrong, and a full mock test every two days.
