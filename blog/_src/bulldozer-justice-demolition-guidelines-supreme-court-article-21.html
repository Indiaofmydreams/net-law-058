Quick answer
  • 13 November 2024 (Justices Gavai and Viswanathan): executive cannot punish by demolishing a house.
  • Guidelines: 15-day notice, personal hearing, reasoned order, video recording.
  • Not covered: structures on public land and court-ordered demolitions.
  • Violations can attract contempt and compensation from officers.

"Bulldozer justice" refers to the demolition of homes of people accused of crimes, often within days of the accusation. The practice became a symbol of a larger debate on due process. In November 2024 the Supreme Court set out guidelines for demolitions across the country. This guide explains the rules and the arguments on each side.

What did the Supreme Court say about bulldozer demolitions?

On 13 November 2024 a bench of Justices B.R. Gavai and K.V. Viswanathan held that the executive cannot punish an accused by demolishing a home, because that would violate separation of powers and the rule of law, and laid down pan-India guidelines.

The Court said that deciding guilt is the work of the judiciary, and that punishing a person's family by taking away shelter is unconstitutional. It relied on Article 21, which the Court has read to include the right to shelter, and on Article 300A on deprivation of property only by authority of law. The Court also used the idea of the "right to a home", describing a house as the culmination of years of effort.

What are the main guidelines?

Authorities must give a show-cause notice at least 15 days before demolition, served by registered post and put on the property, allow a personal hearing, pass a reasoned final order, give time to challenge it and record the demolition on video, with a digital portal for notices.

NoticeShow-cause notice at least 15 days before; registered post; displayed on the structure

Time to challengeOwner given time to appeal before demolition
RecordingVideography of the demolition and an inspection report
TransparencyNotice on a digital portal and information to the District Magistrate

The notice must state the nature of the violation, the details of the structure and the grounds for demolition, so that the owner can respond.

Are there exceptions?

The guidelines do not apply to unauthorised structures in public places such as roads, railway lines and water bodies, or where a court has ordered demolition.

The Court made clear that it was not protecting encroachment on public land. This exception is important because it answers a common objection: that the guidelines stop all action against illegal construction. They do not; they require that action follows a fair process. The Court also said that authorities cannot demolish only part of a locality selectively while ignoring others, since that suggests a punitive motive.

What happens if officers violate the guidelines?

The Court said that violation can lead to contempt and prosecution, and that the officer responsible may have to restore the property at personal cost and pay compensation.

This links to the broader theme of State accountability, which is discussed in our post on compensation for illegal State action. Critics later said that the effect of the guidelines depends on enforcement, and that petitioners have approached the Court alleging violations. Supporters say that the guidelines give courts a clear standard to apply.

Why is it debated?

Supporters of strict action say that illegal structures must be removed and that delay helps encroachers. Critics say demolition is used as punishment without trial, often against particular communities, and that the notice rules are not always followed.

The legal test in both views is the same: is the demolition under a valid law, after a fair process, and not as a penalty for alleged crime? The municipal laws of States already provide for notice and appeal, so the guidelines add a uniform national minimum. A balanced answer states that the Court was dealing with process and not approving or condemning any particular demolition.

How can this be asked in UGC NET Law, CLAT PG or AIBE?

Expect questions on Article 21, Article 300A, natural justice and the doctrine of separation of powers.
