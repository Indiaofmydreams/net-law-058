Few candidates read the bond clause until after the offer, but it can decide whether a PSU job fits a long-term plan such as judiciary or a PhD. This guide explains how bonds work, how courts view them and what to check.

Why do PSUs ask for a service bond?

PSUs spend money on training and want trainees to stay for a minimum period. A bond promises that, if you leave early, you will pay a stated amount. It protects the employer's investment and is common in executive trainee programmes.

The bond is a contract term, and the offer letter usually has it as an annexure.

What are typical bond terms?

Terms differ by company: a minimum service of around one to three years and a bond amount that can run to several lakh rupees, reported in some notices. The exact amount and period are stated in the advertisement and offer, so read them.

What happens in the training and probation period?

Selected candidates are trainees or probationers for a period, often about a year, with training at a centre and on the job. Confirmation depends on performance, and the stipend or salary is as per the offer.

Law officers may rotate through legal, contracts and HR functions before final posting.

Are service bonds enforceable?

Courts apply Section 27 of the Contract Act and the reasonableness test. Bonds that merely recover genuine training cost are generally upheld, while excessive penalty is judged under Section 74. Each case turns on its facts.

The Supreme Court has examined bond cases involving public employment and training costs, so read recent rulings if you face a dispute. Related reading: Unit 6.

What should I check before signing?

Check the bond amount, the period, the rule on resignation or notice, whether the amount reduces over time and what happens if you leave for higher studies or another government job. Ask HR in writing if unclear.

If you plan to sit for judicial services, ask how the bond affects release timing.
