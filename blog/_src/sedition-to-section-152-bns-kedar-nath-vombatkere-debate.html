Quick answer
  • Kedar Nath Singh (1962) limited sedition to incitement to violence.
  • 11 May 2022: Supreme Court put Section 124A in abeyance.
  • Section 152 BNS replaced it: secession, armed rebellion, subversive activities, or endangering sovereignty, unity and integrity.
  • Debate: national security versus free speech under Article 19(1)(a).

Sedition is among the oldest and most criticised offences in Indian criminal law. When the Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita replaced the IPC, the word disappeared, but critics say the idea did not. This guide compares the old and new provisions and sets out the arguments.

What was sedition under Section 124A IPC?

Section 124A punished words, signs or representation that bring or attempt to bring hatred, contempt or disaffection towards the Government established by law, with imprisonment up to life. The Supreme Court limited it in Kedar Nath Singh (1962) to acts that incite violence or public disorder.

The provision was introduced by the British in 1870 and was used against freedom fighters. The Constitution Bench in Kedar Nath Singh upheld it but read it narrowly. Later, in Balwant Singh (1995), the Court held that casual slogans without incitement were not sedition. Even so, critics said that police continued to use the provision against journalists, students and activists.

What did the Court do in 2022?

On 11 May 2022 in S.G. Vombatkere v. Union of India, the Court put Section 124A in abeyance, directing that no new FIRs be registered and pending cases be kept on hold until the Government re-examined the provision.

The Union told the Court it would reconsider the provision. The effect of the order was that existing accused could seek bail and no new cases were to be filed. Parliament then replaced the IPC with the BNS, effective 1 July 2024, and the petitions raised the question of what to do with the new provision.

What is Section 152 of the BNS?

It punishes whoever, by words, signs, electronic communication or financial means, excites or attempts to excite secession, armed rebellion or subversive activities, or encourages feelings of separatist activities or endangers the sovereignty, unity and integrity of India. The punishment is up to life imprisonment or seven years, with a fine.

The text includes an explanation that comments expressing disapproval of Government measures, with a view to lawful change, do not amount to the offence.

Is Section 152 the same as sedition?

The Government says the word sedition was dropped, but critics say the new offence is wider and has a higher punishment. Supporters say it is more precisely tied to threats to the nation and not to criticism of the Government.

Critics focus on terms such as "subversive activities" and "separatist activities", which are not defined, and on the reach to electronic communication, which could cover social media posts. Supporters say that the offence needs a real tendency to endanger the nation, in line with Kedar Nath Singh. Challenges to Section 152 are reported as pending, so the outcome is not known. What did the Law Commission say?

In its 279th Report (2023) the Law Commission recommended retaining sedition with procedural safeguards, such as a preliminary inquiry before an FIR, and stressed that the provision should be used only against incitement to violence.

The Commission said that repeal would leave India without a specific offence against threats to the State, and pointed to laws in other countries. Opponents of the report said that safeguards do not remove the chilling effect. The Parliamentary Standing Committee that examined the BNS Bill in 2023 also discussed the provision. A balanced view includes both reports, the Court's narrow reading and the text of the new section.
