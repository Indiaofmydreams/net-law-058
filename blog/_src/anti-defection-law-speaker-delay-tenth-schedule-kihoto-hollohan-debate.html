Quick answer
  • Tenth Schedule (1985): disqualification for voluntarily giving up membership or defying the whip.
  • Kihoto Hollohan (1992): upheld; Speaker's decision is subject to judicial review.
  • Keisham Meghachandra (2020): decide within three months; consider an independent tribunal.
  • 2025 Telangana order repeated the three-month limit.

When elected members switch parties, the Speaker decides whether they lose their seats. Because the Speaker usually belongs to the ruling party, delays and doubts about neutrality have become routine. The Supreme Court has repeatedly stepped in. This guide explains the law, the key decisions and the arguments for change.

What is the anti-defection law?

The Tenth Schedule, added in 1985, disqualifies a member of Parliament or a State Legislature who voluntarily gives up membership of their party or defies the party whip on a vote, with exceptions for a merger of two-thirds of the legislature party.

The original Schedule also allowed a split of one-third of a party, but the 91st Amendment (2003) removed that exception because it encouraged group defections. The Schedule also covers independents who join a party after election and nominated members who join a party after six months. The aim was to stop the "Aaya Ram, Gaya Ram" politics of the 1960s.

Who decides disqualification?

The Speaker or Chairman decides. In Kihoto Hollohan (1992) the Supreme Court upheld the Schedule but held that the Speaker acts as a tribunal and that the decision is open to judicial review, though not at an interim stage except in limited cases.

Paragraph 7 of the Schedule, which barred courts from reviewing the Speaker's decision, was struck down in Kihoto Hollohan because it needed ratification by States and did not have it. That is why judicial review remains.

What did Keisham Meghachandra Singh (2020) say?

The Court held that Speakers should decide disqualification petitions within three months in the absence of exceptional circumstances, and suggested that Parliament consider a permanent independent tribunal in place of the Speaker.

The decision arose from a Manipur case in which a petition against a minister was left undecided for years. The Court said that unresolved petitions defeat the Tenth Schedule's purpose, since the member keeps enjoying the post while the petition is pending. It made the three-month limit a rule of timing, but did not fix any consequence for breach, which is a weakness critics point out.

What happened in the Telangana case in 2025?

Reports say that on 31 July 2025 a bench of Justices B.R. Gavai and A.G. Masih directed the Speaker to decide the disqualification petitions against several MLAs within three months and criticised the delay, noting that Parliament should reconsider the Speaker's role.

The reports also say the Court warned that if a Speaker does not decide, the Court may draw an adverse inference. What happened after the three months should be checked from current sources, since compliance and further litigation can alter the position. The case shows the pattern: courts set time limits, and Speakers' responses vary. What are the arguments for and against reform?

For reform: the Speaker's party loyalty makes the process partisan, delay lets defectors stay, and an independent tribunal or the Election Commission could decide quickly. Against: the Speaker's role is established in the Constitution, a tribunal would need an amendment, and judicial review already provides a check.

The Dinesh Goswami Committee (1990) and the Law Commission's reports proposed that the President or Governor, on the advice of the Election Commission, decide such matters. Critics of the whole law say that it limits free speech inside the legislature and strengthens party leaders. Supporters answer that without it, governments would be unstable. A balanced answer presents both and highlights that the Court has used only timing and review, not a new body.
