Quick answer
  • Subramanian Swamy (2016) upheld Sections 499 and 500 IPC; reputation is part of Article 21.
  • The offence now sits in Section 356 BNS (adds community service).
  • In July 2025 a bench orally said it was time to decriminalise; no ruling yet.
  • Debate: reputation and dignity versus free speech and the chilling effect.

A harsh remark in a speech or a news report can lead to a criminal complaint, a court summons and even a prison term. India is one of the few large democracies where defamation is still a crime. This guide explains the law, the Supreme Court's 2016 decision and the arguments made by those who want it changed and those who want it kept.

Is defamation a crime in India?

Yes. Sections 499 and 500 of the IPC made defamation a criminal offence punishable with simple imprisonment up to two years, a fine or both. The Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita carries the offence into Section 356, which also adds community service as a punishment.

Section 499 defines defamation as words, signs or visible representations that harm a person's reputation, and lists ten exceptions, such as imputations made for the public good, fair comment on the conduct of public servants and truth in the public interest. A complaint is usually filed by the aggrieved person before a Magistrate, and the courts have had to deal with a stream of complaints by politicians and companies.

What did Subramanian Swamy v. Union of India decide?

In May 2016 a two-judge bench upheld the constitutional validity of criminal defamation, holding that the right to reputation is part of Article 21 and that the offence is a reasonable restriction on speech under Article 19(2).

The Court rejected the idea that Article 21 only protects the individual against the State, and said it also covers dignity against private harm. It also held that the Magistrate should examine the complaint carefully before summoning the accused. The reasoning is often contrasted with Shreya Singhal (2015), where the Court struck down a different speech offence for vagueness.

What did the Court say in 2025?

In July 2025 a bench headed by Justice M.M. Sundresh orally observed that it was time to decriminalise defamation, in a case involving a news portal. These were oral remarks and not a ruling, so the law has not changed.

The remark attracted attention because the 2016 decision came from a smaller bench, and later changes in thinking about proportionality and free speech might justify a second look. Until a larger bench revisits the question or Parliament amends the law, the 2016 decision is the binding position. Students should therefore avoid saying that defamation has been decriminalised.

What is the argument to keep the offence?

Supporters say reputation is a core aspect of dignity, that civil damages are slow and inadequate and that the offence deters false and malicious statements, with exceptions for truth in the public good and fair comment.

They add that the offence is rarely punished with a prison term and that the exceptions in Section 499 protect honest reporting. They also argue that online speech can destroy reputations quickly and permanently, so the law needs a strong deterrent. This reasoning was accepted in the 2016 decision.

What is the argument to decriminalise?

Critics say that prison for speech has a chilling effect, that it is used by the powerful to harass critics and journalists and that many democracies, including the UK since 2009, have abolished criminal defamation.

They argue that civil remedies such as damages and apologies are enough and that a criminal process with its attendant arrest and trial is itself a punishment. They also note that the Parliament's own revision of the criminal code in 2023 did not drop the offence but added community service.
