Quick answer
  • On 18 September 2026 (Justices Pardiwala and Chandran) the Supreme Court struck down Rules 18A and 18B of the Maharashtra Poisons Rules (2026 INSC 1009).
  • Rules had been added in 2011 after the 1991 Mumbai hooch tragedy (93 deaths).
  • Grounds: proportionality, Article 14 arbitrariness and Article 19(1)(g).
  • The Court added non-binding guidelines for States on supply-chain control and health response.

Methanol is an industrial chemical used in paints, fuels and labs, and it is also the poison in most deadly hooch tragedies. Regulating it means protecting public health without crippling legitimate industry. In September 2026 the Supreme Court said the Maharashtra rules did not strike that balance. This guide explains what the rules did, why the Court struck them down and what it asked the States to do instead.

What did the Supreme Court decide in Balaji Formalin v. State of Maharashtra?

On 18 September 2026 Justices J.B. Pardiwala and Chandran struck down Rules 18A and 18B of the Maharashtra Poisons Rules, holding that they failed the test of proportionality and were arbitrary under Article 14.

The petitioner was a company dealing in chemicals, which challenged the rules as burdens on its legitimate trade. The Court agreed that the means chosen did not have a rational and necessary link with the aim, even though the aim of preventing hooch deaths was legitimate and important.

What did Rules 18A and 18B require?

Rule 18A required colourant and bitterant additives in methanol and made sellers verify the buyer's licence. Rule 18B provided for confiscation through a Form A procedure. They were inserted in 2011 after the 1991 Mumbai hooch tragedy that killed 93 people.

RuleContent
18AColourant and bitterant additives; seller to verify buyer's licence
18BConfiscation under a Form A procedure

The reasoning behind the rules was to make methanol unattractive to those who would use it in illicit liquor. A bitter additive and a colour would make it hard to pass off as drinking alcohol. The industry argued that additives spoil methanol for lawful industrial uses and that the confiscation procedure lacked fairness.

Why were the rules struck down?

The Court found that they did not pass the proportionality test, were arbitrary under Article 14 and unreasonably restricted the trade of legitimate methanol users under Article 19(1)(g).

The proportionality framework comes from Puttaswamy (2017) and Modern Dental College (2016): a legitimate aim, a rational connection, necessity (no less restrictive but equally effective alternative) and balance between benefit and burden. The Court found the rules failed on necessity and balance. The ruling is also an example of delegated legislation being struck down, since the rules were made under a statute and can be examined for arbitrariness, a ground confirmed in Shayara Bano (2017) for legislation as well.

What guidelines did the Court give the States?

It issued about twelve to fifteen non-binding guidelines, including enforcement along the supply chain, tamper-evident sealing, a health response cell for mass casualties and de-addiction support.

The Court also pointed to the experience of States that prohibit liquor, such as Gujarat, in explaining why enforcement and public health response are as important as rules on chemicals. The Registry was told to send the judgment to the High Courts and the Chief Secretaries. Because the guidelines are non-binding, their strength depends on how States adopt them.
