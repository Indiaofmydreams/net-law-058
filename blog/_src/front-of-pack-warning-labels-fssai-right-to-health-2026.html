Quick answer
  • In 3S and Our Health Society v. Union of India the Supreme Court is pressing FSSAI to adopt front-of-pack warning labels on foods high in sugar, salt and fat.
  • On 13 August 2026 (Justices J.B. Pardiwala and K. Vinod Chandran) it gave the Union two weeks to reconsider and warned of its own directions.
  • Constitutional anchors: Article 21 (health) and Article 47 (nutrition and public health).
  • The Court asked: "Should India remain as an underdeveloped country?"

Most Indian shoppers cannot tell at a glance how much sugar, salt or fat is in a packet of biscuits or a cold drink. The back-of-pack table is small and technical. The Supreme Court has now asked why India does not follow countries that put clear warnings on the front. The litigation is a good example of how public health, consumer rights and constitutional duties meet. This guide explains the case and the legal ideas behind it.

What is the Supreme Court doing on front-of-pack labels?

In 3S and Our Health Society v. Union of India the Court has been pressing FSSAI to adopt front-of-pack warning labels on foods high in sugar, salt and fat. On 13 August 2026 it asked the Union to reconsider its stand within two weeks and warned it would pass directions itself.

The case is a writ petition that has gone through several hearings. In February 2026 the Court found FSSAI's affidavit on compliance unsatisfactory and asked for a further report. In August the bench heard the matter again and heard that the Union opposed the labels. The Court asked FSSAI to consult experts on the format of the label, including colours, interpretive words, numbers, letters or symbols.

What did FSSAI and the Union argue?

The Additional Solicitor General told the bench that front-of-pack labelling could not be implemented because Indian dietary standards differ from international ones. The Court rejected the argument, asking whether India should remain an underdeveloped country.

Objections of this kind are common in regulatory fights: that foreign models do not fit local diets, that industry would face costs or that voluntary measures are enough. The Court's reply was that the point of a label is to help consumers make informed choices, and that Indian conditions are a reason to design a suitable format, not a reason to do nothing. It also suggested that implementation should not be difficult since the elements are already in the government's view.

Which constitutional provisions did the Court rely on?

The Union was asked to consider the issue under Article 21, which includes the right to health, and Article 47, which makes raising the level of nutrition and public health a duty of the State.

ProvisionRole in the case
Article 21Right to life includes the right to health and informed choices
Article 47Directive Principle: State must improve nutrition and public health
Food Safety and Standards Act, 2006Statutory basis for FSSAI's labelling regulations
Consumer Protection Act, 2019Consumer's right to be informed

Directive Principles are not enforceable by themselves, but the Court has long read them with Article 21 to give content to fundamental rights. That is why Article 47 appears in a health labelling case.

What evidence did the Court cite?

It cited a 2025 UNICEF report that overweight among school-age children and adolescents rose from 2 per cent to 10 per cent between 2002 and 2022, the Economic Survey on ultra-processed foods and NFHS data on obesity. It also pointed to Chile and Canada.

The bench noted that nearly 80 per cent of items sold near schools are packaged and warned that refined sugar, refined flour and trans fats raise the risk of heart disease, obesity and hyperactivity in children. It referred to Chile, which began requiring "high in sugar" style warnings in 2016 and later tightened its thresholds, and to Canada's front-of-package symbols. These examples show a global trend toward simple, mandatory warnings.

Is there a final judgment on warning labels yet?

Not in the reports available for this guide. The August hearing ended with interim directions and a deadline, so check the latest order before relying on any final position.

If the Court eventually directs a label format, FSSAI would have to amend its labelling regulations under the Food Safety and Standards Act. The legal question for students is how far a court can direct a regulator to make subordinate legislation, a point that connects to the separation of powers and to the Court's use of continuing mandamus in health and environment cases.

How can this be asked in UGC NET Law, CLAT PG or AIBE?

Expect questions on Article 21 and health, Article 47, the role of FSSAI and the consumer's right to information.
