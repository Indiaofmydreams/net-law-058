Quick answer
  • The Supreme Court (2026 INSC 849) held that a Section 70 summons does not make a person an accused.
  • The Section 69 arrest authorisation must be communicated before arrest; email and mobile service is allowed.
  • Arrest needs the Commissioner's reasons to believe tied to a Section 132 offence.
  • Arrest cannot be used to force ITC reversal or tax payment.

GST officers have wide powers to summon, search and arrest. Business owners often ask when an officer can lawfully arrest and what they can do about it. In 2026 the Supreme Court set out a clearer process for GST arrests, building on earlier rulings. This guide explains each rule in the form of the questions people ask most often.

What did the Supreme Court decide in the Sunil Biyani GST case?

The Court held that a summons under Section 70 does not make a person an accused, that the Section 69 arrest authorisation must be communicated before arrest, and that arrest cannot be used to force payment of tax or reversal of input tax credit.

The reported date of the judgment varies between sources, so this guide does not rely on it; the citation given is 2026 INSC 849. The ruling follows Radhika Agarwal v. Union of India (2025), which held that the safeguards of criminal procedure apply to GST arrests, and that officers are not police officers for FIR purposes but must follow the law on arrest.

Can the GST department arrest someone without telling them why?

No. The Court required the Commissioner's order authorising arrest to be communicated before the arrest, and service by email to the registered GST address and by mobile was allowed.

Grounds of arrestGiven under Article 22(1) and Section 69(2)

Communicating the order in advance matters because it lets a person seek anticipatory bail. This also connects to the rule on written grounds that the Court tightened for arrests generally; see our post on grounds of arrest.

What are reasons to believe?

They are the Commissioner's recorded reasons, based on material, that the person has committed an offence under Section 132 that is arrestable. They must be tied to a specific offence and not a general suspicion of tax evasion.

Section 132 lists GST offences such as issuing invoices without supplying goods and claiming input tax credit on such invoices. Arrest is allowed only for the more serious ones, usually above a prescribed tax amount. The Court's insistence on a recorded link between the material and the offence is a check against arrests as pressure tactics. An officer who cannot explain the reasons risks having the arrest set aside.

Can a court give interim protection after refusing anticipatory bail?

No. The Court held that a court that rejects anticipatory bail cannot grant separate interim no-arrest protection, and it set aside the Bombay High Court's one-week protection.

The reasoning is that anticipatory bail is itself the remedy against arrest. If a court decides that the case is not fit for it, giving protection through another route would defeat the decision. A person who needs time can seek a stay in a higher court by proper application. For anticipatory bail to be available at all, the person must know of the arrest order, which is why the communication rule is linked to it. Section 482 BNSS is the provision on anticipatory bail.

Can arrest be used to recover tax?

No. The Court said arrest cannot be used as a tool to force an input tax credit reversal or payment of tax, which has its own recovery process.

GST has two tracks. The civil track is assessment, demand and recovery under sections such as 73 and 74, which can be appealed. The criminal track is prosecution for offences under Section 132. Arrest belongs to the second. Using it to extract money blurs the tracks and, as the Court saw it, violates the liberty guarantee in Article 21. Taxpayers who pay under pressure should know the payment does not remove the officer's duty to follow the law.

How can this be asked in UGC NET Law, CLAT PG or AIBE?

Expect questions on Article 22, arrest powers under taxing statutes, anticipatory bail and the difference between summons and accusation.
