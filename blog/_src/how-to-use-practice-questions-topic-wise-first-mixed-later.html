Quick answers
  • Order: topic-wise, subject-wise, mixed, full mocks.
  • Volume: 30 to 60 reviewed questions a day.
  • Review: read explanations for wrong and guessed answers.
  • Timing: add a timer when untimed accuracy is steady above 70 percent.

Practice questions are the single most effective tool in law exam preparation, and the most misused. Aspirants do them too early, too fast, in the wrong order and without review. This guide gives a four-stage ladder, a daily volume that works, a way to learn from explanations and a way to use bookmarks, with the Practice Arenas on this site as the tool.

Should I practise questions topic-wise or in mixed sets?

Start topic-wise, then move to mixed. Topic-wise practice right after studying fixes the rule in memory and shows gaps. Mixed sets later train you to identify the topic from the question itself, as the real exam demands. Moving too early to mixed sets slows learning; staying topic-wise too long hides how you handle the whole paper.

The pattern is a ladder with four rungs. Each rung adds difficulty: more subjects, more time pressure, less context. Move up when your accuracy on the rung is above 70 percent for core subjects.

How many practice questions should I do every day?

Thirty to sixty questions a day is a good range during coverage, with review taking as long as solving. Quality beats volume: ten questions with careful review teach more than fifty answered and forgotten. In the last month, raise it to fifty to eighty with mixed sets, still keeping review time.

Count only questions you review. If you do 60 and review 20, you have done 20. A good daily structure: 30 questions on the day's topic (stage 1), 15 on an earlier subject (spaced repetition), and 15 mixed. Log every error as in our error-log method.

How do I use explanations to learn from practice questions?

Read the explanation for every question you got wrong and every question you guessed right. Ask three things: why is the correct option right, why is each wrong option wrong, and what trap makes this question tricky? Then restate the rule in your own words on your sheet or card.

The explanation is where the learning happens. This is why the Practice Arenas on this site give, for every question, the answer, the reasoning, the reason each other option fails and the trap behind it. If you do only the first step, you are checking answers, not learning.

  • Why right: link the answer to a rule, section or case.
  • Why others wrong: note the specific flaw in each distractor.
  • The trap: note the wording or confusion that causes mistakes.
  • One-line takeaway: write it on your sheet or card.

Should I bookmark questions or add notes while practising?

Yes, but sparingly. Bookmark questions that taught you something new or that you got wrong for a knowledge reason, and add a one-line note on the lesson. Do not bookmark everything: a bookmark list of 300 questions is unreadable. Review bookmarks weekly and clear the ones you now get right.

Each Practice Arena on this site (CLAT PG, AILET, CUET PG, AIBE) has a Bookmarks tab and a My mistakes notebook that fill as you practise. Use My mistakes for wrong answers and Bookmarks for questions you want to revisit for other reasons; review both weekly.

When should I start timing my practice?

Start timing once your untimed accuracy in a subject is steady above 70 percent. Before that, speed work only entrenches errors. Time a set of 20 questions at your exam's seconds-per-question target, and compare accuracy at pace with untimed accuracy. A drop of more than ten points means your pace is too high.

The Practice Arenas are deliberately untimed, so they are best for stages 1 and 2. For stage 3, set a timer yourself using the numbers in our seconds-per-question guide. Keep a note of the accuracy you get at exam pace, and use it to decide your attempt strategy for negative marking: see when to guess and when to skip.

What a good explanation looks like

Take a question from the CUET PG Practice Arena: The doctrine of the basic structure was first articulated in which case? A weak review stops at 'Kesavananda Bharati'. A good review records four things:

  1. Why right: Kesavananda Bharati (1973), a 13-judge bench, held Parliament can amend under Art 368 but cannot destroy the basic structure.
  2. Why the others fail: Golak Nath (1967) said fundamental rights could not be amended at all; Shankari Prasad (1951) upheld wide power; Minerva Mills (1980) applied the doctrine, it did not originate it.
  3. The trap: Minerva Mills is tempting because it is so often cited with the doctrine.
  4. The takeaway for your sheet: 'Kesavananda 1973 = origin; Minerva Mills 1980 = application'.

This is the format the Practice Arenas follow for every question, so you can copy the habit into your own notes.

Common mistakes in practising questions

  • Doing questions before reading the topic. You learn from the answers only if you have a base to attach them to.
  • Redoing the same set until you remember the answers. You are testing memory of options, not the topic.
  • Skipping hard questions in review. The hard ones are where marks are lost.
  • Practising only favourite subjects. Track the number of questions per subject and balance against the exam's weightage.
  • Never doing a full mock. Stages 1 to 3 build skill; stage 4 builds stamina and judgement.

Combine practice with revision: topics from your error log return in the next stage-1 session after seven days, as described in the spaced repetition schedule.

A sample week at stage 1 and 2

Monday to Thursday: study one topic a day, then 15 to 20 questions on it. Friday: 40 questions across the four topics, reviewed. Saturday: 30 mixed questions from earlier weeks plus error-log revision. Sunday: rest or a half mock. This gives you roughly 200 reviewed questions a week without long hours.
