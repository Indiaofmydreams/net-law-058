Quick answer
  • The collegium is a judge-made system (1993, 1998), not a constitutional text.
  • The 99th Amendment and NJAC Act, 2014 were struck down in 2015 by 4:1 (Fourth Judges Case).
  • Core debate: judicial independence versus transparency and accountability.
  • Reform options: written criteria, a secretariat, a revised Memorandum of Procedure.

"Judges appointing judges" is one of the best-known features of India's constitutional system, and one of the most disputed. The text of Article 124 speaks of appointment by the President after consultation with the Chief Justice and other judges. The practice grew out of three Supreme Court decisions. This guide explains the history and the arguments for and against change.

What is the collegium system?

It is the system by which the Chief Justice of India and the four senior-most Supreme Court judges recommend appointments and transfers of judges. It is not in the Constitution; it was created by the Supreme Court in the Second and Third Judges Cases.

The shift in 1993 was a response to experience under executive-led appointments, including the supersession of judges in the 1970s, which many saw as an attack on independence.

What did the Fourth Judges Case decide?

In October 2015 a Constitution Bench held by 4 to 1 that the 99th Constitution Amendment and the NJAC Act, 2014 were unconstitutional because they threatened judicial independence, a part of the basic structure. Justice Chelameswar dissented.

The majority said that primacy of the judiciary in appointments is part of the basic structure, since independence of the judiciary is. It was concerned that the Law Minister and "eminent persons" could outvote or block the judicial members. Justice Chelameswar argued that the collegium lacked transparency and that the Amendment deserved a fair chance. After the decision the Court invited suggestions to improve the Memorandum of Procedure, which governs appointments.

What was the NJAC?

The National Judicial Appointments Commission was to have six members: the CJI, two senior Supreme Court judges, the Law Minister and two eminent persons. A nomination could be blocked if any two members did not agree.

The eminent persons were to be chosen by a committee of the Prime Minister, the Chief Justice and the Leader of the Opposition. Supporters said it would add diversity and public legitimacy. Opponents pointed out that the two-member veto could allow the executive and non-judicial members to stop judicial choices, with no reasons recorded.

Why do critics oppose the collegium?

They say it is opaque, lacks written criteria, can favour insiders and is not accountable to anyone. They point out that judges choose judges, which is unusual among major democracies.

Concerns include delay in clearing names, disagreement between the collegium and the Government, uneven representation of women and social groups, and lack of reasons. The Court has taken some steps, such as publishing collegium resolutions and the reasons for some decisions. Critics say this is partial. Parliamentary committees and political leaders have repeatedly called for a better system, and demands intensified after the 2025 cash-at-residence controversy; see our post on removal of judges.

Why do defenders support it, and what reforms are proposed?

Defenders say executive control over appointments risked political pressure on judges before 1993, and that independence is best served when the judiciary has the leading voice. They suggest reforming the collegium's transparency rather than replacing it.

Reform ideas include published eligibility criteria, a permanent secretariat to collect data, recorded reasons for appointment and rejection, time limits for the Government to respond and wider consultation with the Bar. Others suggest a new constitutional amendment with a different design, such as a commission with a judicial majority. Whether such a design would pass the basic structure test is a question for the Court. A fair answer presents both views and stresses that the Constitution's text and the Court's interpretation differ.

How can this be asked in UGC NET Law, CLAT PG or AIBE?

Expect questions on Articles 124, 217 and 222, the Judges Cases and the basic structure.
