The death penalty debate in India usually asks whether it should exist. This case asked a different question: if it exists, must the method be hanging? The Supreme Court's August 2026 answer keeps the law as it was, but the way it reached that answer is a useful lesson in how precedent works. This guide explains the arguments, the earlier case and the practical position.

What did the Supreme Court decide on hanging in 2026?

On 18 August 2026 Justices Vikram Nath and Sandeep Mehta dismissed Rishi Malhotra v. Union of India, refused to refer Deena v. Union of India (1983) to a larger bench and upheld hanging as the mode of execution under Section 354(5) CrPC.

The petition under Article 32 was pending since 2017. Section 354(5) of the Code of Criminal Procedure said that when a person is sentenced to death, the sentence should direct that he be hanged by the neck until dead. The Bharatiya Nagarik Suraksha Sanhita carries the provision forward as Section 393(5). The Court did not find that the petitioner had shown that Deena's factual or scientific basis had been displaced.

What did the petitioner argue?

The petitioner argued that hanging causes prolonged suffering and violates Article 21, and proposed lethal injection, shooting, electrocution or the gas chamber. He also argued that military laws allowing shooting should apply to civilians.

He also asked the Court to recognise a right to die with dignity. The argument built on a shift in Article 21 jurisprudence: from Maneka Gandhi, which requires procedure to be fair, just and reasonable, to cases that treat dignity as part of life. The Union responded that no botched execution by hanging had been shown in India, while the petitioner's side pointed to botched executions elsewhere by other methods.

What did Deena v. Union of India hold?

In 1983 a Constitution Bench upheld hanging as a method of execution, finding that it was not cruel or barbarous and that alternatives did not show a distinct or demonstrable advantage.

The Court noted that the method has been largely unchanged for over 150 years. A smaller bench cannot overrule a Constitution Bench, so the petition needed either a reference to a larger bench or proof that Deena's basis had changed. The bench found neither.

Why did the Court reject lethal injection?

The Court found no concrete evidence that lethal injection is demonstrably superior or more humane. It also called the argument based on military laws irrelevant, since those statutes govern a separate class of persons.

Military law has its own code and its own modes of punishment for a distinct group, so it does not give a template for civilians. On lethal injection, the Court weighed the competing claims and held that it had not been shown to be less painful. It said that in 2023 it had asked the Union to explore less painful alternatives, and that the Attorney General later said a committee was under consideration, but no update on its constitution had been given.

Is the question closed for good?

No. The Court said future challenges are not barred if compelling scientific, medical or empirical evidence emerges, and the Union may constitute an expert body to review execution methods.

The result is therefore narrow: on the evidence before it, the Court saw no reason to disturb Deena. That is a typical judicial approach to precedent. Whether Parliament or the executive decides to adopt another method is a policy choice, and the Court did not forbid it.
