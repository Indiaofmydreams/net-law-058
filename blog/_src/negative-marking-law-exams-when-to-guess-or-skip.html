Negative marking changes the game in some exams and not at all in others. Aspirants who treat all five exams the same either leave marks on the table (skipping in AIBE) or lose them (guessing blind in CUET PG). This guide gives the arithmetic, a decision rule you can apply in ten seconds, and exam-by-exam advice for the exams on this site.

Is there negative marking in law entrance exams?

It depends on the exam. CLAT PG and AILET deduct 0.25 for every wrong answer. CUET PG LLM gives plus four and minus one. AIBE and UGC NET have no negative marking, so you should attempt every question in those two. Always confirm in the current notice.

Should I guess in CLAT PG and AILET?

Guess only when you can eliminate at least two options. With plus one and minus 0.25, break-even accuracy is 20 percent, so a pure four-way guess is barely positive and risky. A two-option guess has an expected gain of about 0.38 marks per question.

The arithmetic is simple. If you pick an answer with probability p of being right, your expected score is p × 1 − (1 − p) × 0.25. At p = 25 percent (blind) that is +0.06. At 33 percent (one option removed) it is +0.17. At 50 percent (two removed) it is +0.38. The gain from guessing between two options is six times the gain from a blind guess.

Expected value is an average. On a single paper, a streak of unlucky guesses can cost you several marks. The rule is not 'never guess blind'; it is 'never rely on blind guesses'.

Should I guess in CUET PG LLM with plus four and minus one marking?

Yes, but only with two or three options eliminated. Break-even accuracy is still 20 percent, yet each wrong answer costs a full mark against four for a right one. A 50 percent guess is worth about plus 1.5 marks, while a blind guess is worth only plus 0.25.

The ratio of reward to penalty is 4 to 1, so the break-even point is 20 percent, the same as in CLAT PG. What changes is the variance: because each mark is bigger, a bad streak of blind guesses hurts more. How do I decide whether to skip or answer a question?

Use a ten-second test: can you remove two options for a specific legal reason, such as a wrong Article, a wrong section or a statement that is too absolute? If yes, answer. If your reason is only a feeling, mark it for a second pass and move on.

Train this in mocks, not on exam day. After each mock, tag every wrong answer as one of three types and note how many would have been better skipped.

  • Knowledge gap: you did not know it and guessed blind. Should have skipped.
  • Elimination worked but failed: you removed two options and picked wrongly. The decision was right; accept it.
  • Careless: you knew it and misread the qualifier. Fix with a slower first read.

If more than a third of your wrong answers fall in the first type, your guessing is costing marks, and the fix is skipping more, not studying more. The same error log method is explained in our passage-solving guide.

What should I do when there is no negative marking?

Attempt every question. In AIBE and UGC NET a blank can never earn marks and a guess costs nothing, so leave nothing unanswered. Work through sure answers first, then use elimination, and spend the last two minutes filling every remaining bubble or click.

For UGC NET, where Paper 1 and Law are in one three-hour sitting, check the clock at two points so that the last ten questions of Law do not go blank. For AIBE (offline), mark answers on the OMR as you go and do not leave a block of 15 questions to the end. Both exams are covered in our AIBE 22 guide and UGC NET December 2026 schedule.

A worked example: the same 100 questions, three different strategies

Take a CLAT-style paper with 120 questions. Candidate A answers all 120 and gets 78 right: 78 − 42 × 0.25 = 67.5. Candidate B answers 100, getting 76 right and 24 wrong: 76 − 6 = 70. Candidate C answers 90 with 74 right and 16 wrong: 74 − 4 = 70. B and C score higher than A while knowing fewer answers, because they skipped the questions where they had no basis to choose. The gap is small in CLAT PG and AILET, where the penalty is 0.25, but it is large in CUET PG, where the same pattern costs far more.

Run the same exercise on the +4 and −1 scale: 75 questions, attempting 75 with 52 right gives 52 × 4 − 23 = 185. Attempting 66 with 50 right and 16 wrong gives 200 − 16 = 184, almost the same with nine fewer risky questions. The point is not that skipping always wins; it is that attempting weak questions adds little and adds variance.

Common mistakes with negative marking

  • Treating every skipped question as a loss. A skipped question you would have got wrong is a saved mark.
  • Changing answers at the last minute without a reason. Change only if you spot a specific error, such as a misread qualifier.
  • Guessing in a pattern (all C's). Examiners randomise positions; pattern guesses are blind guesses.
  • Ignoring the cut-off context. If the cut-off is high, you need more attempts at high accuracy, not blind ones; see what a good CLAT PG score looks like.
Verify before you rely on it

Marking schemes can change between sessions. Confirm yours in the current notice or information bulletin.
