This is a sensitive and contested subject. The post explains the law and the arguments of both sides and does not take a position.

Disputes over old mosques and temples keep returning to the courts, and nearly all of them raise the same statute. The Places of Worship (Special Provisions) Act, 1991 was passed to stop them. Its own validity is now under challenge. This guide explains what the Act does, what the Supreme Court has said and the arguments on each side.

What does the Places of Worship Act, 1991 say?

It bars conversion of the religious character of any place of worship from what it was on 15 August 1947 and bars suits to change that character. The Ram Janmabhoomi-Babri Masjid dispute was excluded from the Act.

SectionContent
3No conversion of a place of worship from one religious denomination to another
4(1)Religious character stays as on 15 August 1947
4(2)Pending suits or proceedings about conversion abate
4(3)Exceptions, including ancient monuments protected by law
5Act does not apply to the Ayodhya dispute
6Penalty for violation: up to three years and a fine

The Act was passed when the Ayodhya movement was at its height, with the aim of keeping the peace by closing the door on other claims.

What did the Ayodhya judgment say about the Act?

In 2019 the Constitution Bench said that the Act is a legislative instrument to protect secular features of the basic structure and imposes a non-retrogression obligation, meaning that the State cannot go back on the commitment to preserve religious character.

These observations are frequently cited by both sides. Defenders of the Act say they confirm its validity. Challengers say they were made in passing in a case about a different dispute and are not a final ruling on the Act's constitutionality. The question of how binding such observations are is a typical issue of precedent in constitutional law.

Who is challenging the Act, and on what grounds?

Petitions, including one by Ashwini Upadhyay and others, argue that the Act bars judicial remedy, that the 1947 cut-off is arbitrary and that it violates Articles 25, 26 and 29. Other parties defend it and seek to be impleaded.

The petitioners argue that the right to approach a court under Articles 32 and 226 cannot be taken away by a statute, and that past historical wrongs should not be shut out. Supporters of the Act argue that India's secular framework needs a fixed date and that reopening old disputes would risk public order. The Court has held that judicial review is part of the basic structure, so the effect of Section 4 on court access is a central legal question.

What did the Supreme Court order in December 2024?

A special bench led by CJI Sanjiv Khanna directed that no fresh suits be registered and no effective interim or final orders, including surveys, be passed in existing suits relating to places of worship until the challenge is decided.

The order was prompted by a number of suits on sites such as Gyanvapi in Varanasi, the Shahi Idgah in Mathura and the mosque in Sambhal. The Union was given time to file a response. The interim order is meant to prevent further disputes while the Court decides the validity of the Act. Reports in 2026 show that related matters continue to reach the Court, so check for current hearings.

What are the main arguments for and against the Act?

For: it protects peace, secularism and the constitutional promise of equal respect for all faiths. Against: it blocks access to courts, treats 1947 as an arbitrary date and ignores earlier acts of destruction. Both arguments are about balancing history and present order.
