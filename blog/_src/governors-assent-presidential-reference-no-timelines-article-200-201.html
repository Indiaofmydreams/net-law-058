Quick answer
  • Advisory opinion of 20 November 2025: no judicial timelines for Governors or the President.
  • No deemed assent under Article 142.
  • Three options under Article 200: assent, reserve, return. No indefinite withholding.
  • Limited mandamus for prolonged, unexplained inaction.

When a State Assembly passes a Bill, the Governor's decision decides whether it becomes law. Several opposition-ruled States said Governors were sitting on Bills for months. The Supreme Court first set deadlines, then, on a Presidential Reference, took a different view. This guide explains both and what the law now says.

What did the Supreme Court say in the Presidential Reference opinion of 20 November 2025?

A five-judge Constitution Bench led by CJI B.R. Gavai held unanimously that courts cannot fix timelines for Governors and the President to act on Bills, that there is no deemed assent, and that decisions under Articles 200 and 201 are not justiciable on merits before a Bill becomes law.

The President, Droupadi Murmu, made the reference under Article 143(1) on 13 May 2025, with 14 questions. The bench also included Justices Surya Kant, Vikram Nath, P.S. Narasimha and Atul S. Chandurkar. The Court treated the issue as one of separation of powers, saying that the Court cannot take over the constitutional role of another authority.

What was the Tamil Nadu case?

On 8 April 2025 a two-judge bench in State of Tamil Nadu v. Governor held that Governors and the President must act within set timelines and treated ten Tamil Nadu Bills as assented. The 2025 reference opinion overruled that approach.

The earlier ruling was widely noticed because it applied Article 142 to declare assent. The later opinion said that doing so takes over a constitutional authority's function.

What can the Governor do with a Bill?

Under Article 200 the Governor may assent, reserve the Bill for the President or return it to the Legislature with comments. The Court rejected the idea of a fourth option of withholding a Bill indefinitely.

The Union had argued that a Governor could simply withhold assent. The Court held that this would defeat the legislature and the federal structure. A Money Bill cannot be returned with comments. If the Legislature passes the Bill again, the Governor must assent, as the proviso states, unless it is reserved for the President. The Court also said that the Governor has discretion in choosing among the options and is not bound by the Council of Ministers' advice for this particular function, which departs from the normal rule in Shamsher Singh (1974).

Can courts ever intervene?

Yes, in a limited way. In cases of prolonged, unexplained and indefinite inaction, the Court said it may issue a limited mandamus asking the Governor to act within a reasonable time, without commenting on the merits.

This keeps a check against abuse without allowing the Court to fix dates or decide the merits. Article 361 gives the Governor personal immunity from court proceedings, but the Court said the office is still within the Court's limited jurisdiction for this purpose. Critics say the "reasonable time" standard leaves too much uncertainty, while supporters say it respects both the Governor's office and the legislature.

What are the arguments on each side?

States argue that delay without reasons lets a nominated Governor override an elected legislature. The Union and supporters of the opinion argue that the Constitution gave no deadlines, and that courts should not write them in. Reform proposals include amending Article 200 or following the Sarkaria and Punchhi Commission suggestions on Governors.

The Punchhi Commission (2010) examined the role of Governors and suggested time limits for decisions on Bills. Both Commissions' recommendations were not made binding. The question is a continuing one in Centre-State relations; a related constitutional dispute is in our post on the CEC appointment law.
