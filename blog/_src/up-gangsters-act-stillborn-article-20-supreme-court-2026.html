Quick answer
  • On 20 August 2026 (Justices J.B. Pardiwala and K. Vinod Chandran, judgment by Justice Chandran) the Supreme Court called the UP Gangsters Act, 1986 "stillborn".
  • Reason: it prescribes punishment but creates no distinct offence, which offends Article 20(1).
  • The 2021 Rules on Gang Charts exceed the rule-making power in Section 23.
  • The Court did not decide constitutional validity and the ruling does not affect charges under other laws.

State laws on organised crime are often defended as necessary and attacked as draconian. Uttar Pradesh's Gangsters Act, in force since 1986, has been used widely, including against ordinary people. In August 2026 the Supreme Court looked at its structure and found something unusual: a law that sets punishments without defining a crime. This guide explains the reasoning in simple terms and what it means for criminal law.

Why did the Supreme Court call the UP Gangsters Act stillborn?

On 20 August 2026 Justices J.B. Pardiwala and K. Vinod Chandran held that the Act defines gangs and gangsters but creates no distinct offence. Its punishment provisions attach to activities already punishable under other laws, so it creates a status, not a crime.

The case came from a dispute over elections to the Fatehgarh (Farrukhabad) Bar Association. Two advocates were booked under Sections 2 and 3 of the Act, and the Allahabad High Court refused to quash the proceedings. Section 2 defines "gang" and "gangster" and lists anti-social activities, but those listed activities, such as offences against person and property, are already offences under other laws. The Court said the Act punishes a person for being a gangster without saying what the criminal act is.

What is Article 20(1)?

Article 20(1) provides that no person shall be convicted except for violating a law in force at the time of the act, and no penalty can be greater than that prescribed then. It reflects the principle nullum crimen, nulla poena sine lege.

The Latin maxim means no crime and no punishment without law. A person must be able to know in advance what conduct is criminal. A statute that punishes a status, defined by executive paperwork, is vulnerable because the offence is effectively determined by an official's judgment, not by the legislature's definition. The Court applied this principle to hold that punishment must rest on an offence created by law.

What is a Gang Chart and why did it matter?

A Gang Chart is the document used under the 2021 Rules to determine gang status. The Court criticised the Act for allowing detention and trial on the strength of a Gang Chart alone and held that the Rules exceed the rule-making power under Section 23.

The point on delegation is a separate legal argument. Creating an offence is a legislative function. Rules made by the executive under a delegated power cannot create offences or fill in a missing one. A statute may delegate details, but not the core of criminal liability. This is a standard topic in administrative law, tied to the doctrine of excessive delegation.

Did the Court strike down the Act?

No. The Court expressly did not decide the Act's constitutional validity. It held the Act stillborn for want of an offence, allowed the appeals of two advocates and quashed the proceedings against them.

The Court also did not approve or reject the Allahabad High Court's Full Bench judgment on the Act, and did not settle the other constitutional challenges. Those questions remain open. After the ruling, the Allahabad High Court sought the State's response to the "stillborn" observation, which shows that the debate is far from over.

Does this mean gangsters cannot be prosecuted?

No. Charges under other penal laws are unaffected and must proceed under the ordinary criminal procedure and the relevant penal statute.

The ruling is about the structure of one law, not about leniency toward organised crime. Prosecutors can still invoke offences such as extortion, criminal intimidation, murder, dacoity or criminal conspiracy under the Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita, and the new organised crime provisions in that Code. The distinction students should draw is between a special law that defines a new offence and one that merely attaches heavier consequences to a label.

How can this be asked in UGC NET Law, CLAT PG or AIBE?

Expect questions on Article 20(1), delegated legislation, special laws on organised crime and the difference between status and conduct in criminal law.
