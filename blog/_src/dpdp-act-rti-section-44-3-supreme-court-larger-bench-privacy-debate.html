Quick answer
  • Section 44(3) DPDP Act amends Section 8(1)(j) RTI Act: personal information exempt, no public-interest override.
  • 16 February 2026: Supreme Court issued notice and referred the challenge to a larger bench; no stay.
  • Also challenged: Section 36, Rule 23 and Board independence.
  • Debate: privacy (Article 21) versus transparency (Article 19(1)(a)).

Two rights meet head-on in this dispute: the right to privacy, recognised in 2017, and the right to information, which has exposed corruption for two decades. The Digital Personal Data Protection Act, 2023 changed the RTI Act's exemption for personal information, and the change is now before the Supreme Court. This guide explains the provision and the arguments.

What does Section 44(3) of the DPDP Act do?

It amends Section 8(1)(j) of the Right to Information Act so that personal information is exempt from disclosure without the earlier public-interest override. Petitioners say this creates a blanket exemption for personal information.

Before the amendment, Section 8(1)(j) exempted personal information that had no relationship to any public activity or interest, or would cause unwarranted invasion of privacy, unless the authority was satisfied that larger public interest justified disclosure. It also had a proviso that information which cannot be denied to Parliament cannot be denied to a citizen. The amendment removed these qualifications, as the petitioners read it.

What did the Supreme Court do on 16 February 2026?

A bench of CJI Surya Kant and Justices Joymalya Bagchi and Vipul M. Pancholi issued notice to the Union and referred the challenges to a larger bench, saying there was no question of staying the Act and that some issues needed ironing out.

The CJI called the issue "complex but interesting" and said the Court needs to balance the two rights. Counsel for the petitioners relied on Subhash Chandra Agarwal (2019), where the Court dealt with the RTI Act's exemptions, though the bench observed that it did not directly consider Section 8(1)(j).

What else is challenged?

Section 36 of the DPDP Act and Rule 23 of the DPDP Rules, 2025, which let the Central Government call for information from data fiduciaries and intermediaries, and the independence of the Data Protection Board, on grounds of Articles 14, 19 and 21.

The petitioners say that these provisions give the State wide access to personal data without safeguards, while the Act also exempts State bodies in many situations under Section 17. They question whether a Board appointed by the Government can be independent. The Rules were notified in 2025 with phased commencement, so some obligations take effect later.

What do the petitioners argue?

They argue the law is disproportionate and uses a sledgehammer instead of a chisel, that it removes the balance between privacy and the public interest and that it weakens the RTI Act as a tool against corruption.

In their view, a public servant's salary, assets or the beneficiaries of a scheme could be refused as "personal information", which would hurt accountability. They apply the four-part proportionality test from Puttaswamy (2017): legitimate aim, suitability, necessity and balance. The claim is that a less restrictive way, such as keeping the public-interest test, was available.

What do supporters of the amendment say?

Supporters say that personal data needs strong protection after Puttaswamy, that the RTI Act's old text led to confusion over what is personal and that the new law protects individuals, including from misuse of RTI for harassment.

They add that the DPDP Act has its own safeguards, that information about public duties is not private and that the exemption can be read to protect only truly personal data.
