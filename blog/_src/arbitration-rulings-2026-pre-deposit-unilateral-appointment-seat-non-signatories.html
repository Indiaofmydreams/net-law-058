Quick answer
  • Pre-deposit: 10% deposit clause above ₹1 lakh referred to a larger bench (17 Aug 2026).
  • Unilateral appointment clauses in public contracts violate Article 14 (Bhadra International).
  • Seat: governing law and jurisdiction clauses help choose between two venues (12 Oct 2026).
  • Non-signatories can be impleaded; fraudulent Section 9 admissions can be recalled.

Arbitration is the main route for business disputes in India, so each Supreme Court ruling shapes contracts across the economy. Five 2026 decisions stand out. Each answers a question that lawyers and students ask often: can a contract make you pay to arbitrate, who can appoint the arbitrator, where is the seat, who is bound and what if fraud is alleged. This guide covers them together.

What did the Supreme Court refer to a larger bench on arbitration in 2026?

On 17 August 2026, in Santosh Associate v. HSIIDC, Justices Misra and Manmohan referred questions on contract clauses requiring a 10 per cent pre-deposit before arbitration to a larger bench, doubting the approach in S.K. Jain (2009).

The clause in this case required a contractor to deposit 10 per cent of the claim, for claims above 1 lakh rupees, before the dispute could be referred. In S.K. Jain v. State of Haryana (2009) a three-judge bench upheld a similar clause, and ICOMM Tele (2019) agreed in a related setting. The 2026 bench doubted them and framed six questions, labelled A to F, for a larger bench. Until that bench rules, the earlier cases remain the binding precedent for such clauses.

Are unilateral arbitrator-appointment clauses valid in public contracts?

In Bhadra International v. Airports Authority of India (2026 INSC 6), the Court held that clauses letting one party appoint the arbitrator in public contracts violate Article 14.

CasePoint
TRF Ltd. v. Energo Engineering (2017)A person ineligible to be an arbitrator cannot nominate one
Perkins Eastman (2019)A party cannot unilaterally appoint the sole arbitrator
Bhadra International (2026)In public contracts, unilateral appointment violates Article 14

The reasoning builds on the principle of neutrality: a decision-maker chosen by one side can appear biased. In public contracts, the State is a party, and Article 14 requires fairness and non-arbitrariness. The ruling is therefore stronger than the earlier ones, because it treats the clause as a constitutional problem and not only as a statutory one under the Arbitration and Conciliation Act, 1996.

How is the seat decided when there are two venues?

In Arcis Clean Energy v. Parshva, decided on 12 October 2026, the Court said the governing law and jurisdiction clause help decide which venue is the seat.

The seat of arbitration decides which courts supervise the arbitration. BALCO (2012), Indus Mobile (2017) and BGS SGS Soma (2019) established that the "venue" in an arbitration clause is usually the seat unless something shows a contrary intention. When a contract names two venues, the other clauses give the clues. If the contract says that courts of a certain city have exclusive jurisdiction and that the law of that place governs, the seat is likely there. This reading respects what the parties wrote.

Can a non-signatory be made a party to arbitration?

Yes, in suitable cases. In the Hemant D. Shah HUF matter in October 2026 the Court allowed a non-signatory to be impleaded, on the basis of conduct and related doctrines.

The leading authority is Cox and Kings v. SAP India (2023), where a Constitution Bench accepted that the group of companies doctrine can bind a non-signatory in some cases, based on the parties' mutual intention shown through conduct, and not merely on a shared parent. The 2026 order is an application of this approach. A non-signatory that took part in negotiating, performing or benefiting from the contract is the usual target. A bare relationship is not enough.

What if a Section 9 admission was obtained by fraud?

In Orris Infrastructure v. Rakesh Gupta, decided on 30 September 2026, the Court held that an admission made fraudulently in a Section 9 petition can be recalled, and that fraud does not automatically end a corporate insolvency process.

The case shows two principles. Fraud unravels everything, so a court can recall a prior order when it learns that the other party was deceived. But the Insolvency and Bankruptcy Code process, once started, has its own logic, and the Court did not treat fraud as a switch that stops it automatically.
