Quick answers
  • Method: plan backwards from the exam date.
  • Phases: cover first, then mock, then revise; last 3 days are light.
  • Priority: core subjects about half your time.
  • Daily hours: 4 to 6 focused hours is enough for most.

Strategy beats effort in law entrance exams because every exam has more syllabus than time. The aspirants who do well are rarely the ones who study the most hours; they are the ones who decided early what to cover, what to repeat and what to leave. This guide gives a method that works for UGC NET, CLAT PG, AILET, CUET PG and AIBE, with the dates published so far, and answers the planning questions aspirants ask most.

How do I make a study plan for a law entrance exam?

Start from the exam date and count backwards: reserve the last week for light revision, the block before it for mock tests, and use everything earlier to cover the syllabus once. Then divide the covering time by subject priority, not equally, and fix a weekly rhythm.

Most aspirants plan forwards: they open the syllabus, start at the first subject and hope time lasts. Backward planning flips this. You decide what the final month must look like first, and what is left becomes your covering time. If that covering time is too short for the whole syllabus, you find out on day one, not in the last week, and you can cut low-value topics deliberately.

  1. Fix the date. Use the official notice, not a coaching post. If the date is tentative, plan for the earlier possibility.
  2. Block the last 3 days for rest, documents and a light skim of your own notes. No new topics.
  3. Reserve the revision block, roughly the last 20 to 25 percent of your total time.
  4. Reserve the mock block, about 25 percent, overlapping with revision. Full papers, reviewed properly.
  5. Use the rest for first-time coverage of the syllabus, ordered by priority.
  6. Add a buffer day every week for what slipped. Plans without buffers fail by week three.

How many weeks do I need to prepare for a law exam?

For a first attempt at a subject-heavy exam such as CUET PG LLM or UGC NET Law, ten to fourteen weeks is comfortable. For CLAT PG or AILET, eight to twelve weeks works if your LL.B. basics are solid. For AIBE, four to six weeks of focused revision is common.

How do I divide my time between subjects in a law exam?

Rank subjects into three tiers using the syllabus and past papers: core subjects get about half your time, regular subjects about a third, occasional subjects the rest. Within each tier, start with your weakest subject, because every subject is only worth a few questions.

Equal time per subject is the most common mistake. In most law entrances, a handful of subjects (Constitutional Law, Criminal Law, Contract, Torts, Jurisprudence) supply a large share of the paper, while a dozen smaller subjects supply a question or two each. Should I study new topics in the last month before the exam?

Only in the first fortnight of that month, and only for high-priority gaps. In the last two weeks switch to mocks, error review and revision of what you already know. New material late in the game rarely sticks and crowds out revision that would have.

The last month is for converting knowledge into marks. A topic you half-learn in the final week usually gives you one or two uncertain answers; revising a core subject for the third time protects five or six. Make a list of gaps by priority at the start of the final month, close the top three, and refuse the rest.

How many hours should I study every day for a law exam?

Four to six focused hours is enough for most candidates; seven to eight is a ceiling, not a target. Quality matters more than clock time: two hours of reading, one hour of recall without notes, and one to two hours of timed questions with review beats eight hours of passive reading.

A weekly template you can copy

Once you know your total weeks, give every week the same shape so that you never decide what to study. A simple week: four days of new coverage in two subject blocks, one day of timed questions across subjects, one day of full mock or half-mock plus review, and one buffer-or-rest day. As the exam nears, shift coverage days into mock and revision days, following the weighted blocks above.

  • Morning block: hardest subject, fresh reading.
  • Midday block: a different subject or case file, so that you do not fatigue on one topic.
  • Evening block: 30 to 40 questions, with the error log from passage practice or your subject MCQs.
  • Before sleep: 15 minutes of recall: write what you remember from today without looking.
