A trial that takes two decades punishes a person long before it proves anything. The Supreme Court has said many times that delay can be a violation of fundamental rights. In July 2026 it applied that idea to a foreign exchange case that had been pending for 23 years. This guide explains the facts, the two legal points and how to use them in an answer.

What did the Supreme Court decide in the Standard Chartered FERA case?

On 21 July 2026 a bench of Justices J.B. Pardiwala and Misra quashed a FERA complaint and summons after a delay of about 23 years, holding that it violated the right to speedy trial under Article 21.

The complaint was under the Foreign Exchange Regulation Act, 1973 and related to about 30 lakh rupees. The FERA was repealed and replaced by FEMA in 1999, but cases for earlier violations continued. The accused had faced proceedings for more than two decades, and the Court found no explanation that justified keeping the prosecution alive. It cited Kailash Chandra Kapri (2026) on delay.

Why did the Court interfere when revision was available?

The Bombay High Court had refused to use Section 482 because revision was available. The Supreme Court set that aside, citing Dhariwal Tobacco, and said the existence of another remedy does not bar the inherent power where the case is otherwise fit for quashing.

The position, from Dhariwal Tobacco Products v. State of Maharashtra (2009), is that a High Court should not reject an inherent power petition merely because a revision lay. Alternative remedy is a rule of caution, not a bar. This is a useful procedural point: students often assume Section 482 cannot be used where another remedy exists.

What is the opportunity notice under Section 61(2) FERA?

The proviso to Section 61(2) requires an opportunity of notice to the person concerned before a complaint is filed. The Court held it mandatory, and its absence was a ground for quashing.

The idea is a pre-prosecution safeguard. A person is entitled to know the allegation and respond before being dragged into a criminal court. Where a statute says that a complaint can be made only after a notice or opportunity, skipping the step makes the complaint defective. The principle is comparable to pre-litigation notice requirements in many statutes, and courts treat them strictly when they protect the accused.

What is the right to speedy trial?

It is part of the right to life and personal liberty under Article 21, recognised since Hussainara Khatoon (1979) and developed in A.R. Antulay (1992). Delay alone can justify quashing in suitable cases.

Hussainara Khatoon arose from undertrials who had spent years in jail without trial. In A.R. Antulay, a Constitution Bench laid down factors for judging delay: length, reasons, assertion of the right and prejudice. Courts have since refused to adopt a rigid time limit. In another 2026 matter reported in digests, Vickki Yadav v. State of U.P., an undertrial held for about nine years was granted bail on the same reasoning. Is delay always fatal to a prosecution?

No. Courts weigh the length of delay, the reasons, the conduct of the accused and prejudice. Serious offences and delay caused by the accused weigh against quashing.

A fair answer therefore separates two situations. Where the delay lies with the State and the offence is relatively minor, quashing is likely. Where the accused has absconded or caused adjournments, or the offence is grave, courts prefer to order a time-bound trial instead. The case should be used as an example of the first situation, not as a general rule that every old case must end.
