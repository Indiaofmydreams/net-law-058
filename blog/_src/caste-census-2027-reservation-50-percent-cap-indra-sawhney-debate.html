Caste enumeration and reservation are contested subjects. This post explains the law and the arguments on both sides without taking a position.

India has not counted every caste in a census since 1931, though Scheduled Castes and Tribes have been counted. Census 2027 changes that. The decision connects to the law on reservation, because courts have asked for data to justify quotas. This guide explains the notification, the legal context and the arguments.

What did the Government notify about caste in Census 2027?

On 14 August 2026 the Home Ministry notified 40 questions for the population enumeration phase under the Census Act, 1948. Caste is to be counted across all groups for the first time since 1931, with question 10 changing from SC or ST to SC, ST or caste.

The notification was signed by the Registrar General. Other new items reported include Aadhaar, voter ID, mobile number and digital literacy. The Government announced earlier, in April 2025, that caste would be part of the census. A challenge seeking to exclude caste enumeration from the census was reported dismissed by the Supreme Court.

When will the enumeration happen?

The reference date is 1 March 2027 for most of the country, with enumeration in February 2027. For Ladakh and snow-bound areas of Jammu and Kashmir, Himachal Pradesh and Uttarakhand, the reference date is 1 October 2026 and enumeration runs from 1 to 30 September 2026.

The census is conducted under the Census Act, 1948, a Central law on a subject in Entry 69 of the Union List. States therefore cannot conduct a census, but they have run their own surveys.

Why does caste data matter legally?

Article 16(4) permits reservation for backward classes not adequately represented, and courts have asked for quantifiable data. In Indra Sawhney (1992) the Court set a general 50 per cent cap, so claims to raise quotas depend on data.

The Court has said in cases such as M. Nagaraj (2006) and K. Krishna Murthy (2010) that States must show backwardness and inadequate representation with data. Without a full caste count, States rely on their own surveys, which courts have scrutinised. A national census would give a common data set, which could support or undercut claims on either side.

What is the 50 per cent cap, and what happened to higher quotas?

Indra Sawhney (1992) held that reservations should ordinarily not exceed 50 per cent, with exceptions in extraordinary situations. In 2021 the Court struck down the Maratha quota that took the total above that limit, and Bihar's raise to 65 per cent was set aside by the Patna High Court in 2024.

The 10 per cent quota for economically weaker sections was upheld in 2022 by a 3 to 2 majority in Janhit Abhiyan, which some read as relaxing the cap for a class outside Article 16(4). Supporters of raising the cap say that new data may show larger backward populations; opponents say the cap protects merit and equality of opportunity. A fair answer shows both lines and notes that the cap is a judicial rule, not a constitutional text.

What are the criticisms and the Bihar and Telangana surveys?

Opposition leaders noted that the notification does not use a pre-prepared state-wise caste list, as the Bihar and Telangana surveys did, and raised questions about how castes will be recorded.

Bihar published a caste survey in 2023 and Telangana conducted one in 2024. Supporters of a national count say it brings uniform, official data, while critics worry about social division and political use of the numbers. Others raise technical concerns about how ambiguous or overlapping caste names will be coded. These questions will affect later debates on reservation policy and sub-classification; see our post on SC and ST sub-classification.
