A growing number of LL.M. aspirants write CLAT PG, AILET and CUET PG in the same season. They overlap heavily in subjects but differ in format, and treating them as three separate preparations wastes time. This guide gives the dates, an overlap table and a combined calendar that builds one core and then adds what each exam needs.

Can I prepare for CLAT PG, AILET and CUET PG together?

Yes. The three exams share a core of Constitutional Law, Criminal Law, Contract, Torts and Jurisprudence, and no rule stops you from writing all of them. Build one shared base first, then add exam-specific skills: passage practice for CLAT PG, branch breadth for AILET and 30-plus subjects for CUET PG.

What is common between CLAT PG, AILET and CUET PG LLM?

The core subjects are common: Constitutional Law, Criminal Law, Contract, Torts, Jurisprudence and Family Law appear in all three, along with Property, Company and Administrative Law. The differences are format and breadth: CLAT PG tests passages, AILET direct questions on thirteen branches, CUET PG direct questions on 30-plus subjects.

How do I plan a single calendar for three LL.M. entrance exams?

Plan in three phases: a shared core from the start, then a CLAT PG and AILET sprint with passage and mock practice, then a breadth phase for CUET PG. Treat the first two as one cycle and the third as a second cycle three months later, using the same error log throughout.

Should I prepare for CLAT PG first or CUET PG first?

Prepare in the order of the exam dates, because the later exam benefits from the earlier work. CLAT PG and AILET come first and build the core and reading skills; CUET PG then needs breadth. Preparing for CUET PG first spreads effort thinly over 30-plus subjects before you have a firm core.

A candidate who builds a solid core for CLAT PG and AILET has about 60 percent of the CUET PG core ready. The remaining work is the regular and occasional subjects, which fit into a ten-week phase with the CUET PG 3-month plan. Doing it the other way round tends to leave candidates with shallow knowledge everywhere and weak passage skills.

How do I avoid burning out when writing several exams?

Use one error log, one set of sheets and one weekly routine for all three, take a rest week after each exam and avoid comparing your performance in one with the others. Treat each exam as practice for the next. Keep one day a week free of study.

  • One system: shared notes and error log reduce overhead.
  • Rest week after the first block: studies show that tired candidates misread more.
  • Different goals for each exam: a seat at an NLU is a different goal from a central university seat; pick targets before you start.
  • No post-exam replay: after each exam, analyse once and move on.

What to track on one page

Keep a single page with three columns: exam, date and your current mock average. Update it weekly. Seeing three trendlines on one page stops the common mistake of studying only for the nearest exam and letting the others drift.
