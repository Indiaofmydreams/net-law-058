Girls miss school or drop out because of a lack of clean toilets and sanitary products. That is not just a health problem; it is an equality and education problem. In January 2026 the Supreme Court accepted that argument and turned it into enforceable directions. This guide explains how the Court reasoned and what schools and States must do.

What did the Supreme Court decide in Dr. Jaya Thakur v. Union of India?

On 30 January 2026 Justices J.B. Pardiwala and R. Mahadevan held that lack of menstrual health facilities in schools undermines equality and acts as a barrier to education, violating Articles 14, 21 and 21A read with the RTE Act, and issued directions to all States and Union Territories.

The petition was filed by Dr. Jaya Thakur, a social worker. She sought relief for girls in Classes 6 to 12, but the Court treated the issue broadly. It said menstrual hygiene is tied to dignity, privacy and bodily autonomy, relying on earlier decisions such as Puttaswamy on privacy and Common Cause on dignity.

Which Articles did the Court rely on?

Article 14 (equality), Article 21 (dignity and menstrual health as part of the right to life) and Article 21A (right to education), together with the Right to Education Act, 2009.

ProvisionHow it applies
Article 14Absence of facilities hits girls disproportionately; substantive equality needs facilities
Article 15(3)State may make special provision for women and children
Article 21Menstrual health is part of dignified life
Article 21A and RTE ActInadequate facilities are a barrier to free and compulsory education

The Court read Section 3 of the RTE Act together with Section 3(2), which bars additional costs to students, and followed earlier decisions holding that basic toilet facilities form part of the right to free and compulsory education.

What facilities must schools provide?

Functional, gender-segregated toilets with usable water in every school, free sanitary napkins that meet prescribed standards, and safe, hygienic and environmentally compliant disposal.

The Court also directed NCERT and SCERT to include gender-responsive curricula, so that menstruation is taught as a normal biological process and stigma is addressed. District Education Officers must conduct periodic inspections of compliance. These measures combine infrastructure, supplies and education, which is why the ruling is seen as comprehensive.

What is continuing mandamus?

It is a technique where the Court keeps a matter alive to monitor compliance with its directions. Here the Registry was told to list the matter after three months with compliance reports from the States and Union Territories.

The Supreme Court has used continuing mandamus in environmental cases such as the Taj Trapezium litigation and in cases about prisons and homes for children. Supporters say it works where governments routinely fail to comply. Critics say it can turn the Court into an administrator. In another 2026 case, the Court declined to use continuing mandamus in anticipation of failure, which shows it uses the tool selectively.

Do the directions apply to private schools?

Reports do not say clearly whether the directions extend to private schools. The ruling speaks to "every school" and addresses State and Union Territory governments, so the safest reading is that government schools are covered and private schools should check the judgment and State orders.
