Stray animals sit at the crossroads of public safety, animal welfare and municipal duty. The Supreme Court has been supervising the issue since the stray dog directions of 2025, and 2026 added new rulings on both dogs and cattle. This guide explains what the Court actually decided, what the Animal Birth Control Rules require and what it means for residents and municipal bodies.

What did the Supreme Court order on stray dogs in 2026?

A three-judge bench of Justices Vikram Nath, Sandeep Mehta and N.V. Anjaria issued comprehensive directions in May 2026. It affirmed its 7 November 2025 directions, which exclude certain premises from re-release of sterilised dogs, and upheld the Animal Welfare Board of India's SOP.

The case began as a suo motu matter after reports of child injuries, and the Court has been revisiting it since 2025. The question in May 2026 was whether the 7 November 2025 directions needed to be modified or extended to gated housing societies, complexes, parks and other public places in light of Rule 11(19) of the Animal Birth Control Rules, 2023.

What is Rule 11(19) of the Animal Birth Control Rules, 2023?

Rule 11(19) deals with returning sterilised and vaccinated dogs to the area from which they were picked up. The Supreme Court held that its 2025 directions exclude premises such as gated societies, housing complexes and parks from that re-release.

The ABC framework is built on the "catch, neuter, vaccinate and return" model. The 2023 Rules, made under the Prevention of Cruelty to Animals Act, 1960, set out how dogs are to be sterilised and returned, who runs the programme and how complaints are handled. The Court's view is that return to the place of capture is not automatic for every kind of premises.

Can stray dogs be removed from housing societies?

Under the Court's directions, sterilised dogs cannot simply be re-released into gated societies, housing complexes, parks or similar public places. Removal and management must follow the Animal Birth Control Rules and the directions, not ad hoc action by residents.

Residents' welfare associations should work through the local body and recognised animal welfare organisations, and should not take enforcement into their own hands.

Who is liable for injuries caused by stray animals?

In Nisha v. Municipal Council Sangrur (31 July 2026) the Court awarded 15 lakh rupees to the family of a person killed by a stray bull, calling a civil suit after nearly two decades unjust, and urged a national compensation mechanism for animal accidents.

The Court said sending the claimants to a civil suit after so long would leave them "remediless". It recommended national measures on cattle laws, a compensation mechanism for bovine accidents, animal tagging and shelter management. The ruling treats municipal failure to manage stray animals as something for which the State can be called to account under Article 21.

Is feeding stray dogs legal in India?

Compassion for animals is protected by law, and the ABC Rules recognise community feeding in designated ways. The Court's directions focus on managing and relocating dogs through sterilisation and vaccination, not on criminalising feeding as such.

The balance the Court is trying to strike is between the rights of residents, especially children, to safe public spaces and the duty to treat animals humanely under Article 51A(g). Questions about specific feeding spots are generally for local bodies and the Animal Welfare Board's guidelines.

How can this be asked in UGC NET Law, CLAT PG or AIBE?

Likely topics include the Prevention of Cruelty to Animals Act, 1960, Article 51A(g), the "catch, neuter, vaccinate, return" idea and the State's liability for failing to control a public danger.
