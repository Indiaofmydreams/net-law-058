A paper leak damages a whole cohort of candidates at once. After the NEET-UG 2026 leak, Parliament moved to tighten the law passed in 2024. This guide explains what the amendment changes, what happened with NEET and how it connects to criminal and constitutional law.

What is the Public Examinations Amendment Bill, 2026?

It amends the Public Examinations (Prevention of Unfair Means) Act, 2024 to raise minimum jail terms and fines for exam malpractice, require investigation within two months and provide for Special Fast Track Courts. It was passed in the Monsoon Session 2026.

What happened in the NEET-UG 2026 paper leak?

The NEET-UG 2026 exam was disrupted by a paper leak, the Supreme Court pressed the Union on preventing repeat leaks and the exam was rescheduled for 22 June 2026. Student protests followed and led to separate Supreme Court orders.

The Court asked the Union what it was doing to stop leaks from recurring. The protests and the police action that followed are the subject of a separate set of orders, including an Article 142 order quashing FIRs. Read our note on the NEET protest FIRs.

What does the 2024 Act on public exam fraud cover?

The Public Examinations (Prevention of Unfair Means) Act, 2024 makes paper leaks, cheating, tampering with answer sheets and organised exam fraud punishable offences for central public examinations, including those conducted by the NTA, UPSC and SSC.

It was passed after a series of leaks in recruitment and entrance tests. The amendment builds on that base. It does not create a new offence regime from scratch, so questions about definitions and scope still turn on the 2024 Act.

Why does the bill require investigation within two months?

Exam fraud cases often drag on for years, leaving aspirants uncertain and offenders unpunished. A two-month investigation timeline and Special Fast Track Courts are meant to speed up justice and deter organised leak rackets.

Time limits in criminal procedure are often directory in practice, so a missed deadline does not automatically end a case. The real test is whether investigating agencies are resourced and supervised to meet them.

Is a paper leak an offence only for organisers?

The Act targets organised leaks and service providers more severely than individual candidates. Whether an examinee is punishable depends on the specific provision and role. Read the Act and the bill's text for the exact offences and penalties.

For many aspirants the practical lessons are about conduct: use only official channels, never pay anyone for "leaked papers", and report fraud. Cheating, impersonation and paying for leaked material can all attract criminal liability.
