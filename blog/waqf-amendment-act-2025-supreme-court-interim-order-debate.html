Quick answer
  • Interim order of 15 September 2025 (CJI Gavai and Justice Masih): the Act was not stayed as a whole.
  • Stayed: the five-year practice clause (until rules) and parts of the Collector inquiry provision.
  • Not stayed: removal of waqf by user; non-Muslim members allowed within caps (4 of 22; 3 of 11).
  • Final decision on Articles 14, 25, 26 and 300A challenges is for the Court.
For information only

This subject is strongly contested. The post sets out the legal arguments made by each side and does not take a position.

The Waqf (Amendment) Act, 2025 changed how waqf property is defined, registered and governed. Supporters call it long-overdue reform of poorly managed property; critics call it an interference with a religious community's affairs. The Supreme Court heard the challenge and issued an interim order in September 2025. This guide explains that order and the two sets of arguments.

What did the Supreme Court do on the Waqf Amendment Act in September 2025?

On 15 September 2025 a bench of CJI B.R. Gavai and Justice A.G. Masih stayed some provisions, including the five-year practice condition until rules are framed, and parts of the Collector inquiry provision, but did not stay the Act as a whole.

The Court reserved its interim order in May 2025 after five days of arguments. It applied the usual presumption that a law passed by Parliament is constitutional and said a stay is justified only in rare cases, so it stayed only the provisions it found prima facie problematic.

What is waqf by user?

It is a doctrine under which land used for a long time for religious or charitable Muslim purposes was treated as waqf even without a written deed. The 2025 Act removed it for the future, and the Court did not stay that change at the interim stage.

The Court took the prima facie view that deleting it is not arbitrary, and that mandatory registration and deed requirements are not arbitrary either. Opponents say that many old mosques, graveyards and dargahs have no deeds and could lose protection. Supporters say that the doctrine was misused to claim private and public land, and that registration brings certainty.

Can non-Muslims sit on Waqf bodies?

The Court did not stay the provision but directed, noting the Solicitor General's statement, that the Central Waqf Council have no more than four non-Muslim members out of 22 and a State Board no more than three out of 11.

Central Waqf Council224

A non-Muslim chief executive officer is allowed, though the Court said a Muslim should be appointed where possible. The legal question is whether Article 26(b), which lets a religious denomination manage its own affairs in matters of religion, covers the administration of waqf property, or whether property administration is a secular matter under Article 26(d), which is subject to law.

Why did the Court stay the Collector's role and the five-year clause?

It held the proviso that stops treating a property as waqf during a Collector's inquiry to be arbitrary at the prima facie stage, saying that title disputes belong before a judicial authority. The five-year practice requirement was stayed because there is no mechanism to verify it.

The Court said that the five-year requirement can take effect only after the Union frames rules on how to determine whether a person has practised Islam for five years. It also said that mutawallis must not create third-party rights in disputed property until the Tribunal decides.

What are the main arguments on each side?

Petitioners argue violations of Articles 14, 25, 26 and 300A: unequal treatment compared with other endowments, interference with religion and deprivation of property. The Union argues waqf is a secular property regime, and that reform protects both waqf property and the public.

The cases most often cited are Shirur Mutt (1954) on the line between religious and secular matters, and Ismail Faruqui (1994) on the State's power over religious places. For the broader religious-freedom debate, read our post on the Sabarimala reference. A fair answer states both views and notes that the interim order is not a final ruling.

How can this be asked in UGC NET Law, CLAT PG or AIBE?

Expect questions on Articles 25 and 26, essential religious practice, waqf law and the standard for staying a statute.

  • Link it to: Shirur Mutt (1954), Ismail Faruqui (1994), Article 26 and the presumption of constitutionality.
  • Practise: Unit 2, Unit 7 and previous year questions.

The Court did not stay the provision but directed, noting the Solicitor General's statement, that the Central Waqf Council have no more than four non-Muslim members out of 22 and a State Board no more than three out of 11.

Why did the Court stay the Collector's role?

It said that deciding title disputes belongs to a judicial or quasi-judicial authority, not a revenue officer, and held the proviso that stops treating a property as waqf during an inquiry to be arbitrary at the prima facie stage.

Is the case decided?

The order was interim. The petitions challenging the Act on Articles 14, 25, 26 and 300A grounds are for final decision, so check the Court's website for the latest.
