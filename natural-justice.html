18 practice questions with answers

1The two main rules of Natural Justice are:

  1. Audi alteram partem and Res judicata
  2. Nemo judex and Stare decisis
  3. Nemo debet esse judex in propria causa and Audi alteram partem
  4. Audi alteram partem and Nemo tenetur
Show answer and explanation

Answer: C. Nemo debet esse judex in propria causa and Audi alteram partem

The two pillars of natural justice are: (1) Nemo debet esse judex in propria sua causa — no one shall be a judge in his own cause (rule against bias); (2) Audi alteram partem — hear the other side (right to fair hearing).

2'Nemo debet esse judex in propria sua causa' means:

  1. Right to a fair hearing
  2. Both parties must be heard
  3. No one shall be a judge in his own cause — Rule against bias
  4. The decision must be a speaking order
Show answer and explanation

Answer: C. No one shall be a judge in his own cause — Rule against bias

This maxim means that a person who has an interest in the outcome of a decision should not be the one to make that decision. It is the foundation of the rule against bias in natural justice.

Report this question

3Which case is regarded as the leading English authority on the rule against bias (pecuniary interest)?

  1. R v. Sussex Justices (1924)
  2. Dimes v. Grand Junction Canal (1852)
  3. Ridge v. Baldwin (1964)
  4. Cooper v. Wandsworth Board (1863)
Show answer and explanation

Answer: B. Dimes v. Grand Junction Canal (1852)

In Dimes v. Grand Junction Canal (1852), Lord Chancellor held shares in the canal company. His decree was set aside even though there was no actual bias — pecuniary interest causes automatic disqualification. Concept note: Types of Bias: (1) Pecuniary bias — automatic disqualification (Dimes); (2) Personal bias — some connection with a party; (3) Subject matter bias — pre-judgment; (4) Departmental bias — decision-maker is part of the same department. Test in India: 'Real danger' or 'Reasonable suspicion' of bias — A.K. Kraipak (1970).

Report this question

4The leading case that revived the rules of natural justice and held they apply even to administrative decisions affecting rights or legitimate expectations is:

  1. Ridge v. Baldwin (1964) [HL]
  2. Maneka Gandhi v. Union of India (1978)
  3. Cooper v. Wandsworth Board (1863)
  4. A.K. Kraipak v. Union of India (1970)
Show answer and explanation

Answer: A. Ridge v. Baldwin (1964) [HL]

Before Ridge v. Baldwin (1964), natural justice was confined to strictly judicial or quasi-judicial functions. The House of Lords overturned this narrow view and held that natural justice applies wherever rights are affected.

5In A.K. Kraipak v. Union of India (1970), the Supreme Court held that:

  1. Natural justice principles apply to administrative functions also — distinction between administrative and quasi-judicial is obsolete
  2. Government servants have no right to be heard
  3. Natural justice applies only to judicial tribunals
  4. The selection committee was validly constituted
Show answer and explanation

Answer: A. Natural justice principles apply to administrative functions also — distinction between administrative and quasi-judicial is obsolete

In A.K. Kraipak (1970), a member of the selection committee was also a candidate. The SC held that natural justice principles apply to administrative functions as well — the boundary between judicial, quasi-judicial and administrative has become thin.

6The doctrine of 'Legitimate Expectation' in administrative law means:

  1. A person has a reasonable expectation based on previous practice, promise, or policy that procedural fairness will be observed before adverse action
  2. A citizen can legitimately claim a Constitutional right
  3. Government's expectation from citizens
  4. The expectation of winning a case
Show answer and explanation

Answer: A. A person has a reasonable expectation based on previous practice, promise, or policy that procedural fairness will be observed before adverse action

Legitimate Expectation arises when a public authority has made a representation (by conduct, policy, or promise) that creates a reasonable expectation in a person's mind that they will be heard before being adversely affected. Leading cases: GCHQ Case (1985 UK); Navjyoti Coop. v. Union of India (1992 India).

7'Speaking Order' requirement in natural justice means:

  1. The decision must be made in open court
  2. Orders must be announced orally
  3. The affected party must be given an opportunity to speak
  4. The decision-maker must record reasons for the decision
Show answer and explanation

Answer: D. The decision-maker must record reasons for the decision

A 'speaking order' or 'reasoned order' is one where the authority records its reasons for the decision. Leading case in India: Bhagat Raja v. Union of India (1967) — quasi-judicial orders must be reasoned orders.

8Which of the following is NOT an element of 'audi alteram partem'?

  1. Right to be represented by a lawyer in all proceedings
  2. Notice of the charge/case against the party
  3. Opportunity to present evidence
  4. Opportunity to file a written reply
Show answer and explanation

Answer: A. Right to be represented by a lawyer in all proceedings

Right to legal representation is not an absolute requirement of audi alteram partem. It may or may not be required depending on the nature of proceedings and statutory provisions. The other three options are core components of the right to fair hearing.

9The rule of natural justice can be excluded by:

  1. Express statutory provision or necessary implication
  2. Urgency or emergency situations
  3. Impracticability
  4. All of the above
Show answer and explanation

Answer: D. All of the above

Natural justice can be excluded by: (1) express statutory provision; (2) necessary implication from statute; (3) urgency/emergency where prior hearing is impossible; (4) impracticability. However, exclusion must be justified and must not violate Fundamental Rights.

10In Gullapalli Nageswara Rao v. A.P. State Road Transport Corp. (1959), the Supreme Court held:

  1. The government need not give reasons for nationalisation
  2. Tribunal decisions are final
  3. Natural justice is not applicable to economic regulation
  4. An officer who heard objections to a scheme cannot himself sanction it — investigator cannot be adjudicator
Show answer and explanation

Answer: D. An officer who heard objections to a scheme cannot himself sanction it — investigator cannot be adjudicator

In Gullapalli Nageswara Rao (1959), the SC held that the Secretary who had heard the objections to a road transport nationalization scheme could not himself sanction it — this violated natural justice as he would be judging his own hearing.

11'Institutional bias' or 'Departmental bias' arises when:

  1. The whole department/institution has a pre-determined stance likely to influence the adjudicator
  2. The judge personally knows the parties
  3. The judge has pecuniary interest
  4. The lawyer is biased
Show answer and explanation

Answer: A. The whole department/institution has a pre-determined stance likely to influence the adjudicator

Departmental or institutional bias arises when the adjudicator belongs to the same department whose policy or decision is being challenged. The environment itself creates a bias. Discussed in Gullapalli Nageswara Rao and State of U.P. v. Mohd. Nooh (1958).

Report this question

12Can the principle of natural justice be waived?

  1. Yes — if the party with the right knowingly and voluntarily waives it
  2. Only by Parliamentary statute
  3. Never — it is a mandatory requirement
  4. Only by the Supreme Court
Show answer and explanation

Answer: A. Yes — if the party with the right knowingly and voluntarily waives it

Natural justice rights can be waived by the person entitled to them, provided the waiver is voluntary, knowing, and unambiguous. This is a well-established principle in both English and Indian administrative law.

Report this question

13The maxim 'Justice must not only be done but must also be seen to be done' is associated with:

  1. Delegated legislation
  2. Natural justice — bias rule
  3. Stare decisis
  4. Res judicata
Show answer and explanation

Answer: B. Natural justice — bias rule

This famous dictum was laid down by Lord Hewart CJ in R v. Sussex Justices (1924). It underpins the rule against bias — not only must actual bias be absent, but the appearance of bias must also be eliminated.

Report this question

14The doctrine of 'Post-decisional hearing' — giving opportunity to be heard after the decision — is:

  1. Generally not a valid substitute for pre-decisional hearing; courts look at circumstances
  2. Completely valid and satisfies natural justice always
  3. Required in all administrative decisions
  4. Prohibited by law
Show answer and explanation

Answer: A. Generally not a valid substitute for pre-decisional hearing; courts look at circumstances

Post-decisional hearing is generally not an adequate substitute for a pre-decisional hearing. In Maneka Gandhi (1978), the Court held that impounding a passport without prior hearing was invalid even though subsequent remedies were available.

15'Reasoned decision' as a requirement of natural justice is NOT mandatory when:

  1. The decision is merely recommendatory
  2. The statute expressly excludes it
  3. It concerns national security or confidential matters
  4. All of the above
Show answer and explanation

Answer: D. All of the above

Duty to give reasons can be excluded when: (1) the decision is merely recommendatory and not final; (2) the statute expressly excludes the requirement; (3) reasons would endanger national security or confidential information; (4) where the context makes it impractical.

16Which case established that the right to cross-examine witnesses is NOT always part of natural justice in administrative proceedings?

  1. Ridge v. Baldwin
  2. Board of Education v. Rice (1911)
  3. Russell v. Duke of Norfolk (1949)
  4. A.K. Kraipak
Show answer and explanation

Answer: C. Russell v. Duke of Norfolk (1949)

In Russell v. Duke of Norfolk (1949), the court held that the requirements of natural justice depend on the circumstances of the case, the statutory framework, and the nature of inquiry. Cross-examination of witnesses is not always required — it depends on whether it would materially assist the inquiry.

17In which case did the SC hold that natural justice is flexible and depends on statutory context, nature of power, and consequences for the individual?

  1. Union of India v. Col. J.N. Sinha (1970)
  2. A.K. Kraipak v. Union of India (1970)
  3. Swadeshi Cotton Mills v. Union of India (1981)
  4. Maneka Gandhi v. Union of India (1978)
Show answer and explanation

Answer: C. Swadeshi Cotton Mills v. Union of India (1981)

In Swadeshi Cotton Mills v. Union of India (1981), the Supreme Court held that natural justice is not a monolithic or inflexible concept. Its content and extent depend on: (1) the statutory framework; (2) the nature of the power exercised; (3) the consequences for the person affected; (4) public interest considerations.

18Under the 'Doctrine of Fairness' in administrative law, the concept of fairness:

  1. Is identical to natural justice
  2. Applies only to courts
  3. Is narrower than natural justice
  4. Is wider than natural justice and can apply even where there is no traditional NJ requirement — based on good administration
Show answer and explanation

Answer: D. Is wider than natural justice and can apply even where there is no traditional NJ requirement — based on good administration

The 'duty to act fairly' has emerged as a broader concept than the traditional rules of natural justice. It looks at the overall fairness of the administrative process and applies even in situations where the strict rules of natural justice may not be triggered. It evolved in English and Indian administrative law post-Maneka Gandhi.
