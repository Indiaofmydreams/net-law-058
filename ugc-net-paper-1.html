What Paper 1 is

The compulsory first paper of UGC NET, common to every subject.

Paper 1 is the General Paper on Teaching and Research Aptitude (code 00). Every candidate attempts it, whether the subject is Law, History, Commerce or any of the 80+ others.

UGC NET is a single three-hour computer-based session with two objective papers. Paper 1 has 50 questions and 100 marks, and Paper 2 (your subject) has 100 questions and 200 marks. Every question carries 2 marks. Recent sessions have had no negative marking, so an attempted question can only add marks.4 Because Paper 1 counts for one third of your total, it can decide whether a strong subject score is enough.

It is also the part of the exam where preparation transfers most evenly: the syllabus does not depend on your degree, so a graduate in any discipline starts from the same point.

Question split by unit

How the 50 questions are designed to spread across the ten units.

The official Paper 1 syllabus has ten units, and the paper has 50 questions, so the design is about five questions, or ten marks, from each unit.4

Read this carefully. About five per unit is the structure of the syllabus, not a published guarantee for each session. NTA does not announce the exact count per unit in advance, and published analyses of previous papers from 2019 to 2025 report that Teaching Aptitude, Research Aptitude and Logical Reasoning have often carried a little more than five, with the other units sometimes carrying fewer.5 Treat five per unit as your planning assumption, not a promise.

What it means for you. Because every unit carries about the same weight, no unit is safe to skip. A unit you ignore can cost around ten marks, which is more than the typical gap between cut-off outcomes in some categories (see the cut-off trend).

How to approach it

A balanced plan for a paper where every unit counts.

Two kinds of units, two ways to prepare

Skill units (Comprehension, Mathematical Reasoning, Logical Reasoning, Data Interpretation) reward practice. You improve by solving many questions, checking the explanation, and repeating the types you miss. Knowledge units (Teaching Aptitude, Research Aptitude, Communication, ICT, People, Development and Environment, Higher Education System) reward learning and recalling facts and concepts. Short notes first, then MCQs to pull the facts back from memory.

Practise by retrieval, not re-reading

Experiments consistently find that answering questions on material helps you remember it longer than studying the same material again, and reviews rank practice testing among the most effective study techniques.12 Spreading your sessions across days helps as well.3 This is why Paper 1 on this site is built as a question bank with explanations.

Pacing

At about one minute per question, Paper 1 takes roughly 50 minutes of the three hours, leaving a few minutes to review. That is a planning target, not an exam rule; read the instructions in the bulletin. See also the time-management FAQ.

No strategy can promise a result. These are methods with good evidence behind them, applied to a paper whose structure is known.

Starting from scratch

For students from any stream who have never prepared for Paper 1.

You do not need a teaching or research background. Paper 1 is designed as a general paper, so a sensible order and steady practice are enough to begin.

Here is a sample four-week start. Adjust it to your date; the study plans show how to stretch or compress it.

  1. Week 1: Teaching Aptitude and Research Aptitude. These two units are the most closely tied to the paper's purpose and the most predictable.
  2. Week 2: Comprehension, Communication and ICT. Read one passage daily, then work through ICT terms.
  3. Week 3: Mathematical Reasoning, Logical Reasoning and Data Interpretation. Short, daily problem sets build speed.
  4. Week 4: People, Development and Environment, and Higher Education System, then revise. Revisit your mistakes from every unit.

Use the Exam Strategy Engine to adapt the order to your time and weak spots, and the Pomodoro timer to keep sessions focused.
