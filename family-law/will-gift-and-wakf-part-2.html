19 practice questions with answers

1Under Section 59 of the ISA, 1925, a person who is ordinarily insane can make a valid will:

  1. With two doctors as witnesses
  2. During a lucid interval — the burden is on those propounding the will to prove the lucid interval
  3. Never — such a person can never make a will
  4. If they were sane when they started the will
Show answer and explanation

Answer: B. During a lucid interval — the burden is on those propounding the will to prove the lucid interval

Section 59 ISA — a person who is ordinarily insane may make a will during a lucid interval; the propounder must prove the lucid interval existed at the time of making the will.

2'Animus testandi' means:

  1. Intention to create a trust
  2. Testamentary intention — a document must reflect the maker's intention to make posthumous disposition of property to be a valid will
  3. Intention to gift property
  4. Intention to revoke a will
Show answer and explanation

Answer: B. Testamentary intention — a document must reflect the maker's intention to make posthumous disposition of property to be a valid will

Animus testandi = testamentary intention; a document must reflect the maker's genuine intention to make posthumous disposition of property — this is an essential element of every valid will.

3Under Muslim Law, a testamentary disposition (wasiyat) differs from a Hindu will primarily in that:

  1. A Muslim cannot bequeath more than 1/3 of estate after debts to non-heirs; bequest to an heir is invalid without other heirs' consent
  2. A Muslim will requires court registration
  3. A Muslim will must be in Arabic
  4. A Muslim will is irrevocable once made
Show answer and explanation

Answer: A. A Muslim cannot bequeath more than 1/3 of estate after debts to non-heirs; bequest to an heir is invalid without other heirs' consent

The 1/3 rule and the prohibition on bequest to heirs (without consent of other heirs at the time of death) are cardinal differences between Muslim wasiyat and a Hindu/secular will.

Report this question

4Under Muslim Law, can a non-Muslim be a beneficiary under a Muslim's will (wasiyat)?

  1. Only if the non-Muslim converts before the testator's death
  2. Only if the bequest is for religious purposes
  3. Yes — a non-Muslim can be a beneficiary under a wasiyat even though a non-Muslim cannot inherit under Muslim intestate law
  4. No — only Muslims can inherit under a Muslim will
Show answer and explanation

Answer: C. Yes — a non-Muslim can be a beneficiary under a wasiyat even though a non-Muslim cannot inherit under Muslim intestate law

Under Muslim Law, the bar on non-Muslims inheriting intestate does not apply to testamentary succession — a Muslim may bequeath up to 1/3 of estate to a non-Muslim.

5Under Muslim Law, a gift (Hiba) of 'mushaa' (undivided share in indivisible property like a house) is:

  1. Always void
  2. Valid only with court approval
  3. Valid — under Hanafi law, gift of mushaa in indivisible property is upheld; under Shia law all mushaa gifts are fully valid
  4. Valid only if the co-sharer consents
Show answer and explanation

Answer: C. Valid — under Hanafi law, gift of mushaa in indivisible property is upheld; under Shia law all mushaa gifts are fully valid

Mushaa under Hanafi law: gift of undivided share in divisible property requires partition first; in indivisible property it is valid. Under Shia law, all mushaa gifts are valid regardless.

Report this question

6The three essential elements of a valid Hiba (gift) under Muslim Law are:

  1. Offer, acceptance, registration
  2. Written deed, two witnesses, Qazi's approval
  3. Declaration (Ijab), Acceptance (Qabool), Delivery of possession (Qabza)
  4. Consideration, witnesses, registration
Show answer and explanation

Answer: C. Declaration (Ijab), Acceptance (Qabool), Delivery of possession (Qabza)

The three essentials of Hiba: (1) Ijab — declaration by the donor, (2) Qabool — acceptance by the donee, (3) Qabza — delivery of possession. Registration is not required.

7Under Muslim Law, gifts to blood relations (ziawi arham) are:

  1. Revocable within 6 months
  2. Irrevocable once made and possession delivered
  3. Always revocable
  4. Valid only with court order
Show answer and explanation

Answer: B. Irrevocable once made and possession delivered

Under Muslim Law, gifts to blood relations (ziawi arham) are irrevocable once the gift is complete (declaration + acceptance + possession). Gifts to strangers, however, are revocable by court order.

8Under Muslim Law, a gift made during 'Marz-ul-Maut' (death illness — where death actually results) is treated as:

  1. Void only if made to a non-Muslim
  2. Fully valid like a normal Hiba
  3. Subject to the same rules as a wasiyat — valid only up to 1/3 of estate, not to a legal heir without consent
  4. Void in all circumstances
Show answer and explanation

Answer: C. Subject to the same rules as a wasiyat — valid only up to 1/3 of estate, not to a legal heir without consent

A gift during marz-ul-maut (where death actually results from the illness) is treated as a testamentary disposition subject to the 1/3 rule — cannot exceed 1/3 without heirs' consent; cannot be to a legal heir.

9A Wakf can be created by:

  1. Oral declaration, written deed or by conduct — no particular form required; oral wakfs are recognised
  2. Written deed only
  3. Registration only
  4. Court order only
Show answer and explanation

Answer: A. Oral declaration, written deed or by conduct — no particular form required; oral wakfs are recognised

Under Muslim Law, no particular form is required for creating a Wakf — it can be oral or written; however the Wakf Act, 1995 requires registration of wakf properties with the Wakf Board.

10The principle 'once a Wakf, always a Wakf' means:

  1. The Mutawalli can convert Wakf property to personal property
  2. Once validly created, a Wakf is irrevocable and permanent — the property vests in God perpetually for the specified purpose
  3. Wakf can be converted to private property after 99 years
  4. A Wakf can be revoked by the Wakif's heirs
Show answer and explanation

Answer: B. Once validly created, a Wakf is irrevocable and permanent — the property vests in God perpetually for the specified purpose

The irrevocability of Wakf is a fundamental principle — 'once a Wakf, always a Wakf'; neither the Wakif nor the heirs nor the Mutawalli can revoke or dissolve it.

11In Mohd. Ismail Faruqui v. Union of India (1994), the Supreme Court held regarding mosque/Wakf property that:

  1. Wakf and private property have identical status
  2. Mosques have special inviolable status
  3. Wakf property can never be acquired
  4. A mosque is ordinary Wakf property and can be acquired by the State under the Land Acquisition Act — offering namaz is not restricted to a specific mosque
Show answer and explanation

Answer: D. A mosque is ordinary Wakf property and can be acquired by the State under the Land Acquisition Act — offering namaz is not restricted to a specific mosque

Mohd. Ismail Faruqui (1994) — SC held that offering namaz is not an essential Islamic practice limited to a specific mosque; State can acquire mosque land under compulsory acquisition.

12The Wakf (Amendment) Act, 2025 introduced all of the following EXCEPT:

  1. Mandatory registration of Wakf properties on a central digital portal
  2. Non-Muslim members on the Central Wakf Council and State Wakf Boards
  3. Removal of the concept of 'Wakf by user' (prospectively)
  4. Compulsory conversion of all Wakf properties into Government property
Show answer and explanation

Answer: D. Compulsory conversion of all Wakf properties into Government property

The Wakf (Amendment) Act, 2025 did not nationalise Wakf properties. It provided for non-Muslim members, removed 'Wakf by user' prospectively and mandated a central portal for registration. (Some provisions were subject to Supreme Court interim orders.)

13Under the Wakf Act, 1995, disputes relating to Wakf properties are adjudicated by:

  1. Wakf Tribunals constituted under Section 83 of the Act
  2. Revenue courts
  3. Supreme Court only
  4. Civil courts
Show answer and explanation

Answer: A. Wakf Tribunals constituted under Section 83 of the Act

Section 83 of the Wakf Act, 1995 — Wakf Tribunals are constituted to decide all disputes relating to Wakf properties; civil courts have no jurisdiction in such matters.

Report this question

14The Central Wakf Council was established under which Section of the Wakf Act, 1995?

  1. Section 107
  2. Section 9
  3. Section 5
  4. Section 83
Show answer and explanation

Answer: B. Section 9

Section 9 of the Wakf Act, 1995 establishes the Central Wakf Council as an advisory body to the Central Government on matters relating to Wakf.

15'Sadaqa' under Muslim Law differs from 'Hiba' (gift) in that:

  1. Sadaqa is a gift for religious/charitable purposes made to seek God's pleasure — once delivered it is irrevocable
  2. Sadaqa is only for land
  3. Sadaqa requires two witnesses
  4. Sadaqa requires consideration
Show answer and explanation

Answer: A. Sadaqa is a gift for religious/charitable purposes made to seek God's pleasure — once delivered it is irrevocable

Sadaqa = charity gift for religious merit; once delivered it is irrevocable — unlike hiba to a stranger which can be revoked. Sadaqa creates a moral obligation and is made purely for God's pleasure.

16Under Section 108A of the Wakf Act, 1995, the Act has an overriding effect over:

  1. The Constitution of India
  2. Any other law relating to Wakf — Wakf Act prevails to the extent of inconsistency
  3. Only state laws
  4. Only the Limitation Act
Show answer and explanation

Answer: B. Any other law relating to Wakf — Wakf Act prevails to the extent of inconsistency

Section 108A Wakf Act, 1995 — the provisions of the Act have effect notwithstanding anything inconsistent in any other law. (Section 107 deals with the Limitation Act.)

17Probate of a will is compulsory under the Indian Succession Act in:

  1. Everywhere in India
  2. Only metropolitan cities
  3. Probate is never mandatory
  4. In the territories of the original High Courts at Calcutta, Bombay and Madras — mandatory in these areas for Hindu/Sikh/Jain/Buddhist wills
Show answer and explanation

Answer: D. In the territories of the original High Courts at Calcutta, Bombay and Madras — mandatory in these areas for Hindu/Sikh/Jain/Buddhist wills

Section 213 read with Section 57 ISA — probate is compulsory for wills of Hindus, Buddhists, Sikhs and Jains within the original jurisdiction of the Calcutta, Bombay and Madras High Courts.

18'Letters of Administration' under the Indian Succession Act, 1925 are granted when:

  1. The deceased has left a valid will
  2. The deceased has died intestate — the court grants letters of administration to administer the estate
  3. The deceased was a Muslim
  4. Property is above Rs. 1 crore
Show answer and explanation

Answer: B. The deceased has died intestate — the court grants letters of administration to administer the estate

Letters of Administration are granted in case of intestacy (no will) or where no executor is named; Probate is granted where there is a valid will with a named executor.

19Under Muslim Law, a Wakf-alal-Aulad means the Wakif can:

  1. Create a revocable endowment
  2. Retain benefit for himself and then pass it to descendants before it goes to charitable purposes
  3. Never retain any benefit for himself
  4. Only create Wakf for mosques
Show answer and explanation

Answer: B. Retain benefit for himself and then pass it to descendants before it goes to charitable purposes

Wakf-alal-Aulad is a private/family wakf where the Wakif can retain life interest for himself, then for descendants, and ultimately for charitable purposes. The key case is Abu Fata Mahomed v. Russomoy (1894).
