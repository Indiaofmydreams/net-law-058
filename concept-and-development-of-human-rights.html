23 practice questions with answers

1The three generations of human rights were proposed by:

  1. Jack Donnelly
  2. Amartya Sen
  3. John Rawls
  4. Karel Vasak
Show answer and explanation

Answer: D. Karel Vasak

Karel Vasak (French jurist, 1979) proposed the three generations framework, inspired by the French Revolutionary ideals of liberté, égalité, fraternité. First generation = civil/political; second = economic/social/cultural; third = solidarity/collective rights.

2The Magna Carta (1215) is significant in human rights history because:

  1. It established that the king is subject to law and recognized rights of barons/freemen
  2. It created universal rights for all people
  3. It was the first declaration of fundamental rights
  4. It abolished slavery in England
Show answer and explanation

Answer: A. It established that the king is subject to law and recognized rights of barons/freemen

Magna Carta 1215 - King John's concession to barons - established the principle that even the sovereign is subject to law. It guaranteed due process of law and laid early foundations of rule of law and civil liberties.

3The natural law theory of human rights is associated with:

  1. Jeremy Bentham
  2. John Locke and Hugo Grotius
  3. John Austin
  4. H.L.A. Hart
Show answer and explanation

Answer: B. John Locke and Hugo Grotius

Natural law theory holds that human rights are inherent in human nature and discoverable by reason, independent of positive law. John Locke (rights to life, liberty, property), Hugo Grotius, and Thomas Hobbes are key theorists.

4The UDHR was adopted by the UN General Assembly on:

  1. 10 December 1947
  2. 10 December 1948
  3. 10 December 1966
  4. 24 October 1945
Show answer and explanation

Answer: B. 10 December 1948

UDHR was adopted by the UN General Assembly on 10 December 1948 by Resolution 217 A(III). There were 8 abstentions (Saudi Arabia, South Africa, Soviet bloc) and no opposing votes. December 10 is Human Rights Day.

5Human rights are characterized by the Vienna Declaration 1993 as:

  1. Alienable, divisible, dependent
  2. Relative, alienable, independent
  3. Universal, inalienable, indivisible, interdependent and interrelated
  4. Cultural, specific and conditional
Show answer and explanation

Answer: C. Universal, inalienable, indivisible, interdependent and interrelated

Vienna Declaration and Programme of Action 1993 (Para 5) affirmed: human rights are Universal, Inalienable, Indivisible, Interdependent and Interrelated. No hierarchy exists between civil-political and socioeconomic rights.

6The 'right to development' as a human right was declared in:

  1. UN Declaration on the Right to Development, 1986
  2. ICCPR 1966
  3. ICESCR 1966
  4. Vienna Declaration 1993
Show answer and explanation

Answer: A. UN Declaration on the Right to Development, 1986

The UN Declaration on the Right to Development was adopted by UNGA Resolution 41/128 in 1986. It recognized the right to development as an inalienable human right by virtue of which every human person and all peoples are entitled to participate in development.

7Eleanor Roosevelt is associated with the UDHR because:

  1. She chaired the UN Commission on Human Rights that drafted the UDHR
  2. She drafted the Magna Carta
  3. She was the first UN High Commissioner for Human Rights
  4. She drafted the ICCPR
Show answer and explanation

Answer: A. She chaired the UN Commission on Human Rights that drafted the UDHR

Eleanor Roosevelt chaired the UN Commission on Human Rights 1947-1951 and presided over the drafting of the UDHR. She is often called the 'First Lady of the World' for her pivotal role.

8The phrase 'inherent dignity' in the UDHR Preamble reflects which theory?

  1. Positivist theory
  2. Social Contract theory
  3. Utilitarian theory
  4. Natural rights / Dignity theory
Show answer and explanation

Answer: D. Natural rights / Dignity theory

The UDHR is philosophically grounded in the natural rights/dignity theory - all human beings possess inherent dignity and equal and inalienable rights by virtue of being human, not by virtue of any law or state grant.

9The 'capabilities approach' to human rights is associated with:

  1. John Rawls
  2. Amartya Sen and Martha Nussbaum
  3. H.L.A. Hart
  4. Ronald Dworkin
Show answer and explanation

Answer: B. Amartya Sen and Martha Nussbaum

The Capabilities Approach, developed by Amartya Sen and expanded by Martha Nussbaum, defines human development/rights in terms of what people are able to do and be (functionings and capabilities) - not merely formal rights on paper or utility.

10John Rawls' 'Theory of Justice' (1971) is relevant to human rights because:

  1. It provides a basis for socioeconomic rights through the difference principle
  2. It justifies absolute state power
  3. It rejects all notions of distributive justice
  4. It supports pure utilitarian calculus for rights
Show answer and explanation

Answer: A. It provides a basis for socioeconomic rights through the difference principle

Rawls' difference principle (from behind the 'veil of ignorance'): social and economic inequalities are just only if they benefit the least advantaged members of society. This provides philosophical grounding for second generation (socioeconomic) rights.

11The 'indivisibility' of human rights means:

  1. Civil-political rights and economic-social-cultural rights are equally important and cannot be separated in a hierarchy
  2. Rights cannot be divided between centre and states
  3. Rights apply uniformly without any exception
  4. Individual rights cannot be divided into sub-rights
Show answer and explanation

Answer: A. Civil-political rights and economic-social-cultural rights are equally important and cannot be separated in a hierarchy

Indivisibility means there is no hierarchy among human rights - civil-political rights and economic, social and cultural rights are equally fundamental. The Vienna Declaration 1993 firmly established indivisibility, rejecting the Cold War hierarchical approach.

12The UN High Commissioner for Human Rights (UNHCHR) was established in:

  1. 1948 after UDHR
  2. 1966 after the two Covenants
  3. 2006 after creation of Human Rights Council
  4. 1993 following Vienna World Conference on Human Rights
Show answer and explanation

Answer: D. 1993 following Vienna World Conference on Human Rights

The Office of the UN High Commissioner for Human Rights (OHCHR) was established in 1993 following the Vienna World Conference on Human Rights. The first High Commissioner was Jose Ayala-Lasso of Ecuador (1994). OHCHR is based in Geneva.

13The UN Human Rights Council replaced which earlier body?

  1. UN Trusteeship Council
  2. UN Commission on Human Rights
  3. UN Security Council Sub-Committee on Human Rights
  4. UN Human Rights Treaty Body
Show answer and explanation

Answer: B. UN Commission on Human Rights

The UN Human Rights Council was established by UNGA Resolution 60/251 in 2006, replacing the Commission on Human Rights (which had existed since 1946). The Council meets in Geneva with 47 member states. It introduced the Universal Periodic Review (UPR) mechanism.

14The Universal Periodic Review (UPR) is a mechanism under which:

  1. The UN reviews international treaties periodically
  2. Only developing countries are reviewed for human rights compliance
  3. All UN member states' human rights records are reviewed by peers every 4-5 years
  4. The Security Council reviews human rights situations in conflict zones
Show answer and explanation

Answer: C. All UN member states' human rights records are reviewed by peers every 4-5 years

Universal Periodic Review (UPR) under the Human Rights Council: every UN member state's human rights situation is reviewed by the full Council (through a Working Group) every 4.5 years on a rotating basis. It is a peer-review process.

15The 'social contract' theory of rights as developed by Rousseau differs from Locke's theory in that:

  1. Rousseau rejected all notion of natural rights
  2. Rousseau supported absolute monarchy
  3. Rousseau located sovereignty in the 'general will' of the community rather than in individual natural rights
  4. Rousseau's theory applies only to economic rights
Show answer and explanation

Answer: C. Rousseau located sovereignty in the 'general will' of the community rather than in individual natural rights

Locke's social contract preserves pre-existing natural rights (life, liberty, property) against the state. Rousseau's General Will (volonte generale) locates authority in the collective will of the community - the individual gives up natural freedom for civil freedom within the community.

16Jeremy Bentham's criticism of natural rights was that:

  1. Natural rights are 'nonsense upon stilts' - they are fictional claims that have no objective basis
  2. Rights are too difficult to enforce
  3. Rights should be granted only to wealthy citizens
  4. Natural rights conflict with state sovereignty
Show answer and explanation

Answer: A. Natural rights are 'nonsense upon stilts' - they are fictional claims that have no objective basis

Bentham famously called natural rights 'nonsense upon stilts' - arguing that rights can only exist as positive legal rights created and enforced by the state; natural rights without legal backing are merely rhetorical assertions.

17The 'Rawlsian veil of ignorance' thought experiment is designed to:

  1. Show that people are naturally selfish
  2. Derive principles of justice that would be chosen by rational persons without knowledge of their place in society
  3. Argue for equal distribution of all resources
  4. Justify meritocracy in rights allocation
Show answer and explanation

Answer: B. Derive principles of justice that would be chosen by rational persons without knowledge of their place in society

In Rawls' 'Theory of Justice' (1971): behind the 'veil of ignorance' (not knowing your gender, race, class, talents), rational persons would choose: (1) equal basic liberties for all; (2) inequalities only if they benefit the least advantaged (difference principle).

18Ronald Dworkin's concept of rights as 'trumps' means:

  1. Rights always override each other in a fixed hierarchy
  2. Strong rights belong only to citizens
  3. Individual rights function as constraints on utilitarian calculations - they cannot be overridden merely because doing so would produce greater aggregate welfare
  4. Rights of the majority always prevail
Show answer and explanation

Answer: C. Individual rights function as constraints on utilitarian calculations - they cannot be overridden merely because doing so would produce greater aggregate welfare

Ronald Dworkin (Taking Rights Seriously, 1977): Rights are 'trumps' that individuals can play against the state - even when overriding them would benefit society overall. This is an anti-utilitarian conception of rights setting side-constraints on what majorities or governments can do to individuals.

19The 'Responsibility to Protect' (R2P) doctrine holds that:

  1. When a state fails to protect its population from genocide, war crimes, ethnic cleansing and crimes against humanity, the international community has a responsibility to intervene
  2. States are responsible for protecting their own citizens only
  3. Responsibility to protect applies only to economic rights
  4. Only the UN Security Council bears responsibility to protect citizens worldwide
Show answer and explanation

Answer: A. When a state fails to protect its population from genocide, war crimes, ethnic cleansing and crimes against humanity, the international community has a responsibility to intervene

R2P was developed by the International Commission on Intervention and State Sovereignty (ICISS) in 2001 and endorsed at the 2005 UN World Summit. When states fail to protect their populations, the international community (through the Security Council) has a residual responsibility.

20The 'erga omnes' concept in human rights law means:

  1. Obligations owed by states to the international community as a whole - all states have a legal interest in their fulfillment
  2. Rights enforceable only against the state that violates them
  3. Rights enforceable only by regional human rights courts
  4. Obligations of individuals toward the state
Show answer and explanation

Answer: A. Obligations owed by states to the international community as a whole - all states have a legal interest in their fulfillment

Erga omnes (towards all) obligations - recognized by ICJ in Barcelona Traction case (1970) - are obligations a state owes to the entire international community. Core human rights obligations (prohibition of genocide, slavery, racial discrimination, torture) are erga omnes - any state may invoke responsibility for their breach.

21The concept of 'positive obligations' in human rights law means:

  1. States must take active steps to ensure rights are realized - including legislation, administrative measures, and provision of resources
  2. States only have to refrain from violating rights
  3. Obligations that are positively worded in treaties
  4. Obligations with which states have voluntarily agreed to comply
Show answer and explanation

Answer: A. States must take active steps to ensure rights are realized - including legislation, administrative measures, and provision of resources

Positive obligations go beyond negative duties (not to torture, not to arbitrarily detain) - states must actively ensure rights through legislation, judicial systems, police protection, social services, and resource allocation. Even ICCPR rights impose positive obligations (e.g., right to fair trial requiring courts and legal aid).

22The 'Lotus Principle' in international law states:

  1. States must always seek permission before acting internationally
  2. States must protect their territory's environment
  3. States can only act where explicitly authorized by international law
  4. Everything not prohibited in international law is permitted to sovereign states
Show answer and explanation

Answer: D. Everything not prohibited in international law is permitted to sovereign states

The Lotus Principle (PCIJ, S.S. Lotus case, 1927): States are free to act unless international law expressly prohibits them. This principle has environmental law implications - it was used historically to argue that states could pollute unless specifically prohibited. Modern environmental law has increasingly constrained this through the no-harm rule and precautionary principle.

23The 'capability approach' developed by Amartya Sen focuses on:

  1. Economic capabilities of states to implement human rights
  2. Military capabilities of states to enforce rights
  3. What people are actually able to do and be - their real freedoms and substantive opportunities
  4. Institutional capabilities of national human rights commissions
Show answer and explanation

Answer: C. What people are actually able to do and be - their real freedoms and substantive opportunities

Sen's Capability Approach (Development as Freedom, 1999): Human development should be assessed in terms of what individuals are substantively able to do and be (capabilities/freedoms), not merely formal legal rights or income. Martha Nussbaum extended it with a list of 10 central capabilities.
