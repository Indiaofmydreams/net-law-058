You have 1 month. Here is how I would approach it.

With one month left, I would stop trying to finish reading and start trying to remember.

A month is long enough to change your result and short enough that every day counts. The question is no longer “What else can I read?” but “What do I already know well enough to recall under exam conditions, and what do I only think I know?”

Re-reading feels productive, which is exactly why it is risky. Dunlosky and colleagues rated re-reading and highlighting as low-utility techniques and practice testing as high-utility.1 So I would spend most of this month on questions, and use the notes only to repair what the questions expose.

Revision → MCQs → PYQs → Weak areas → Mocks → Analysis

If a unit is still untouched, don’t ignore it and don’t try to master it. Learn its core from the notes, test yourself on it, and move on. You are not trying to read more. You are trying to make what you know dependable.

1What your priority should be

  • Revise, don’t read anewNotes are for repair. Stop as soon as you can retrieve the key points.
  • Let MCQs find the gapsA wrong answer is useful information. Aim to find gaps, not to score.
  • Close the weak areasReturn to the notes only for what your mistakes point to.
  • Treat mocks as diagnosisThe learning happens in the analysis afterwards.

THE CORE LOOP

Study → Attempt MCQs → Understand mistakes → Revisit the concept → Attempt again

  • ReviseOne unit from the notes, briefly.
  • AttemptMCQs on that unit straight away.
  • AnalyseLog every mistake: didn’t know, confused, or careless.
  • RepairBack to the notes for that concept only.
  • Re-attemptThe same questions after a day or two.
  • Every 4th dayA timed mock, with analysis the same day.

Don’t do this

  • Don’t collect endless study material.
  • Don’t postpone MCQs until you finish the syllabus.
  • Don’t repeatedly read notes without testing recall.
  • Don’t take mocks without analysing your mistakes.
  • Don’t completely change your resources in the final weeks.
  • Don’t start new books or new sources.
  • Don’t skip Paper 1 because Law feels more urgent.

THE EVIDENCEWhy MCQs sit at the centre of this plan

The evidence I lean on here is about re-reading versus testing. Dunlosky and colleagues’ review rated re-reading as low-utility and practice testing as high-utility.1 Rowland’s (2014) meta-analysis of the testing effect found that testing generally produced better retention than restudying, with feedback mattering.6

The idea is old. Gates (1917) found that time spent reciting from memory was more productive than time spent re-reading,10 and Butler and Roediger (2008) found that feedback after multiple-choice questions reduced the risk of remembering wrong answers.5 That is why error analysis is not optional in this plan.

What this does and doesn’t show. These studies are mostly laboratory and classroom experiments on memory. None of them tested a UGC NET strategy, and none can promise a result in the exam. This pathway is informed by evidence on retrieval practice, practice testing and distributed learning. It is guidance, not a guarantee.
