About this question set

This set has 13 multiple-choice questions on Defamation from the UGC NET Law (058) syllabus, unit 5, Law of Torts and Consumer Protection. Try each question first, then tap Show answer to check the key and read the explanation. 13 of the 13 questions carry a written explanation.

For the concepts and case law behind these questions, read the Torts & Consumer Protection notes. Once you are comfortable, test yourself under exam conditions with a full-length mock test.

13 practice questions with answers

1Which of the following is NOT an essential element of defamation?

  1. Defamatory statement
  2. Malicious intention (always required)
  3. Publication
  4. Reference to plaintiff
Show answer and explanation

Answer: B. Malicious intention (always required)

In libel, malice is presumed; malice is not always a required element.

Report this question

2Libel is defamation in:

  1. Spoken form
  2. Permanent/written form
  3. Gestures only
  4. Email only
Show answer and explanation

Answer: B. Permanent/written form

Libel is in a permanent form — written, printed, or broadcast.

Report this question

3Slander is actionable per se in which of the following cases?

  1. Imputation of a contagious disease
  2. Imputation of unchastity to a woman
  3. Imputation of crime punishable with imprisonment
  4. All of the above
Show answer and explanation

Answer: D. All of the above

All four categories of slander are actionable per se without proof of special damage.

Report this question

4Hulton & Co. v. Jones (1910) established:

  1. Defamation requires intent to defame
  2. Strict liability in defamation
  3. Truth is no defence
  4. A statement can be defamatory even if the defendant did not intend to refer to the plaintiff
Show answer and explanation

Answer: D. A statement can be defamatory even if the defendant did not intend to refer to the plaintiff

Innocent publication is no defence if reasonable people would understand it to refer to the plaintiff.

Report this question

5Which of the following is an absolute privilege in defamation?

  1. Parliamentary proceedings
  2. Judicial proceedings
  3. State communications
  4. All of the above
Show answer and explanation

Answer: D. All of the above

All three are absolute privileges — malice cannot defeat them.

Report this question

6The defence of 'fair comment' in defamation applies to:

  1. Private matters
  2. Any comment made honestly
  3. Matters of public interest
  4. Statements made under compulsion
Show answer and explanation

Answer: C. Matters of public interest

Fair comment must be on a matter of public interest and based on true facts.

Report this question

7Sim v. Stretch (1936) laid down the test for a defamatory statement as:

  1. Would the words tend to lower the plaintiff in the estimation of right-thinking members of society generally?
  2. Would the statement cause hurt to feelings?
  3. Any untrue statement
  4. Any published statement
Show answer and explanation

Answer: A. Would the words tend to lower the plaintiff in the estimation of right-thinking members of society generally?

The right-thinking member of society test is the standard for defamation.

Report this question

8Cassidy v. Daily Mirror Newspapers (1929) illustrates:

  1. Qualified privilege
  2. Fair comment
  3. Absolute privilege
  4. Innuendo — statement innocuous on the face but defamatory in context
Show answer and explanation

Answer: D. Innuendo — statement innocuous on the face but defamatory in context

A photograph caption implied the plaintiff's wife was not his lawful wife — defamatory by innuendo.

Report this question

9Monson v. Tussauds (1894) — placing a wax figure in the 'Chamber of Horrors' was held to be:

  1. Slander
  2. Libel — a permanent representation
  3. Not defamation
  4. Nuisance
Show answer and explanation

Answer: B. Libel — a permanent representation

A wax effigy is a permanent representation and therefore libel.

Report this question

10Reynolds v. Times Newspapers (2001) developed:

  1. Reynolds privilege — responsible journalism on matters of public interest as a form of qualified privilege
  2. Absolute privilege for media
  3. Strict liability for media defamation
  4. Fair comment extended to all media
Show answer and explanation

Answer: A. Reynolds privilege — responsible journalism on matters of public interest as a form of qualified privilege

Reynolds privilege protects responsible journalism on matters of public concern.

Report this question

11Under Section 499 IPC, which of the following is NOT an exception to defamation?

  1. Truth for public good
  2. Statements made with malice
  3. Fair comment on public conduct of public servants
  4. Publication of court proceedings
Show answer and explanation

Answer: B. Statements made with malice

Statements made with malice are NOT an exception — all other options are exceptions under S.499 IPC.

Report this question

12The defence of 'justification' (truth) in defamation:

  1. Requires public interest additionally
  2. Is a complete defence in civil defamation
  3. Is not a defence
  4. Is a defence only in criminal defamation
Show answer and explanation

Answer: B. Is a complete defence in civil defamation

Truth is an absolute defence in civil defamation. In criminal defamation (S.499 IPC), truth must be for public good.

Report this question

13'Legal innuendo' in defamation requires:

  1. The defamatory meaning to be apparent on the face of the statement
  2. Malice of defendant
  3. Actual damage
  4. Extrinsic facts known to persons who read the statement that give it a defamatory meaning
Show answer and explanation

Answer: D. Extrinsic facts known to persons who read the statement that give it a defamatory meaning

Legal innuendo requires extrinsic facts to give a seemingly innocent statement a defamatory meaning.
