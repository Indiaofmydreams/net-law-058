Passage: Sowell on Affirmative Action

Thomas Sowell's central contention is that affirmative action is judged by its intentions when it ought to be judged by its consequences. Preferential policies are defended as a means of lifting the disadvantaged. Yet Sowell, drawing on evidence from India, Malaysia, Nigeria, Sri Lanka and the United States in Affirmative Action Around the World, argues that the beneficiaries are seldom the most disadvantaged members of the favoured group. Preferences are claimed by those nearest the threshold, usually the children of the group's more prosperous families, who can use them with the least effort. The poorest remain where they were. The policy therefore redistributes opportunity within the favoured group at least as much as between groups. India's own debate over the "creamy layer", the better-off families within reserved categories who capture most of the benefit, shows that the pattern is recognised even by those who administer the system.

Sowell also insists that preferences carry costs their supporters rarely count. A student admitted to an institution whose standards far exceed his preparation is set up to struggle among peers who are better prepared. The result is often higher attrition and fewer graduates in demanding fields, outcomes that a less elite institution might have avoided. Such a student, who might have thrived elsewhere, is then counted as a statistic of inclusion rather than as a person who has been placed badly. Moreover, the stigma of preference falls on every member of the group, including those who would have succeeded on merit, because employers and colleagues cannot tell them apart.

Preferences also alter incentives on both sides. If admission or a post is assured by category, the beneficiary has less reason to strive for the margin of excellence. Those outside the category, who know that effort will not close the gap, may feel resentment instead of motivation. Sowell adds that progress was under way before preferences arrived: the rise of black Americans out of poverty and into skilled occupations was already marked before the civil rights legislation of the 1960s, and he doubts that later policies deserve the credit often given to them.

Finally, Sowell observes that policies introduced as temporary rarely end. Once one group has obtained a preference, others demand their own, the categories of eligibility multiply, and politics reorganises itself around group identity rather than individual achievement. For Sowell, the question is not whether the past was unjust, but whether this remedy helps the living individuals it claims to serve.

Question: Which of the following best captures Sowell's central argument in the passage?

  1. Affirmative action should be evaluated by its actual results rather than its stated aims
  2. Elite institutions should lower their standards to improve graduation rates
  3. Preferential policies should be extended to all disadvantaged groups equally
  4. Past injustices against groups are exaggerated and need no remedy
Show answer

Answer: A. Affirmative action should be evaluated by its actual results rather than its stated aims

Passage: Sowell on Group Disparities

In the debate over group disparities, Thomas Sowell attacks what he sees as the founding assumption of much modern policy: that, absent discrimination, every group would be represented in every occupation, institution and income bracket in proportion to its numbers. History, he argues in Affirmative Action Around the World and his wider writings, gives no support to this. Outcomes differ among nations, regions, families and even siblings; first-born children, for instance, have often done better than later-born ones in the same household. Groups differ in age distribution, with median ages varying by well over a decade, and a young group cannot be expected to match the income of an older one. They differ in geography, education, family structure and cultural habits towards work and learning. To treat a gap as proof of discrimination is to treat a result with many causes as though it had only one.

Sowell supports the point with a striking observation: in many countries, small minorities have outperformed the majority around them, even when they were disliked or restricted. Chinese in Southeast Asia, Jews in much of Europe, Lebanese in West Africa and Germans in parts of Latin America all rose to prosperity in trades where they held skills that locals lacked. If discrimination alone explained outcomes, he asks, how could the discriminated-against have outperformed their discriminators? The more reasonable explanation, in his view, is that skills, experience and habits differ, and that these differences, built over generations, produce differences in results.

Sowell does not deny that discrimination exists. He asks how much of a given disparity it actually explains, and insists that this be established by evidence, not assumed. The term affirmative action, he notes, originally meant active efforts to ensure that applicants were considered fairly, but it drifted towards quotas and preferences aimed at statistical results. Equal opportunity and equal outcomes are not two stages of one goal but distinct and often rival ones, because producing the second can require unequal treatment of individuals, which violates the first. A law that places the burden on the employer to explain every statistical gap, he adds, treats a numerical imbalance as a presumption of guilt, which reverses the old principle that the accuser must prove the charge.

Question: The central point of the passage is that:

  1. all groups have identical abilities
  2. discrimination no longer exists
  3. group disparities do not, by themselves, prove discrimination
  4. quotas are the only way to reach equality
Show answer

Answer: C. group disparities do not, by themselves, prove discrimination

Passage: Sowell: The International Evidence on Preferences

Sowell's study of preference policies in India, Malaysia, Sri Lanka, Nigeria and the United States rests on a simple method. Instead of asking what such policies are supposed to do, he compares what they did in different countries and looks for what recurs. The pattern, he reports in Affirmative Action Around the World, is striking. Policies introduced for a decade or two are renewed, then widened. In India, reserved places have been extended repeatedly and the list of eligible castes has grown; in Malaysia, preferences for the Malay majority outlived the period they were meant to cover. Once a benefit exists, those who hold it have a stake in its continuation, and politicians who curtail it lose votes.

A point Sowell presses is that preferences are not confined to minorities, as the American experience might suggest. In several of the countries he studies, the favoured group was the majority, which used the machinery of the state to improve its position against a smaller and more successful minority. In such cases the language of remedying past wrongs hides a plain contest for jobs, university seats and public contracts, and the successful minority is penalised for its success.

More dangerous, he argues, is what preferences do to relations among groups. In Sri Lanka, measures favouring the Sinhalese majority in university admissions, introduced to correct an imbalance, left the Tamil minority convinced that the state was against them, and the bitterness fed a long and ruinous civil war. Where preferences make a group's future depend on state allocation rather than on its own skills, energy shifts from acquiring skills to lobbying. Leaders gain by keeping a grievance alive, since their power rests on it, and so the incentive to declare the problem solved is weak.

His conclusion is blunt: policies adopted in the name of unity have repeatedly bred the division they promised to heal.

Passage: Reagan on the Economy

Ronald Reagan's economic argument, set out in his memoir An American Life and in the speeches collected in Speaking My Mind, rests on one conviction: that government, not the citizen, is the usual cause of economic failure. In the 1970s, he argued, Washington taxed work and investment so heavily that productive people had little reason to earn more. Meanwhile inflation, which followed from government printing money to cover government spending, quietly robbed the thrifty of what they had saved. Both, in his view, were taxes imposed without a vote.

Reagan drew on his own experience. He recalled that during his film career, when the top marginal rate on high earners was very steep, there came a point at which taking another picture left him with so little of the extra income that it was not worth doing. What he saw as a private calculation in Hollywood, he reasoned, was being repeated by millions of workers, managers and investors across the country, and the sum of those small decisions was a slower economy.

A tax rate, Reagan held, is a price set on effort; raise the price and people buy less of it. High marginal rates therefore did not merely collect revenue but suppressed the very activity that generated it. Cutting rates, as the 1981 tax act did by reducing the top rate from 70 to 50 per cent, and as the 1986 reform did by bringing it down to 28, would restore incentives, widen the tax base and eventually enlarge revenue. He had no patience for the view that a planner in the capital could allocate resources better than millions of people spending their own money for their own purposes.

Reagan did not deny that government has duties. He wanted it to defend the country, enforce contracts and help those truly unable to help themselves. But he regarded the growth of federal programmes beyond those limits as a transfer of power from the individual to the bureaucrat, and a slow corrosion of the self-reliance on which a free society depends. A government that spends beyond its means, he added, merely postpones the bill and hands it to children who had no say in the matter.

  3. Planners are always less informed than private citizens
  4. Taxation should be abolished to protect individual liberty

Answer: B. Heavy taxation and government spending suppress individual enterprise and economic growth
