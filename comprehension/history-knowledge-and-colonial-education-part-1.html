Passage: Ancient India: Vedic Knowledge, Science and Trade

Ancient India developed traditions of knowledge in which ritual, observation, mathematics, astronomy and practical craftsmanship were closely connected. The Vedic corpus contains early references to numbers, measurement and astronomical observation, while later texts developed these ideas into more systematic forms. The Śulbasūtras, associated with the later Vedic tradition, provide rules for measuring and constructing fire altars. The Baudhāyana, Āpastamba and Kātyāyana Śulbasūtras contain sophisticated geometric procedures, including relationships corresponding to what is now known as the Pythagorean theorem.

The Vedic tradition also developed Jyotiṣa, one of the Vedāṅgas, partly because accurate calculation of time was necessary for ritual purposes. The Vedāṅga Jyotiṣa is associated with calendrical and time calculations, while later works such as the Āryabhaṭīya, Brahmasphuṭasiddhānta and Sūrya Siddhānta developed mathematical astronomy further. The Sanskrit Commission notes that references to astronomy occur in the Ṛgveda and that astronomical observation became increasingly systematised in subsequent traditions.

This development demonstrates an important feature of ancient Indian science: knowledge was often practical before it became abstract. Geometry could serve the construction of ritual spaces; astronomy could regulate calendars and ritual time; mathematics could assist measurement and calculation. This does not mean that ancient Indian thinkers lacked theoretical interests, but it cautions against imposing the modern separation between “science” and “religion” too rigidly on earlier societies.

Scientific knowledge also existed alongside substantial economic and technological activity. The Indus Valley civilisation shows evidence of organised urban life, standardised measurement and long-distance exchange. Later Indian communities participated in extensive Indian Ocean trade, connecting the subcontinent with Arabia, East Africa and Southeast Asia.

The significance of ancient India, therefore, lies not merely in claiming isolated “discoveries”. It lies in the interaction between knowledge, institutions, craftsmanship, urban organisation and commercial networks. At the same time, historical analysis must distinguish genuine achievements from later nationalist exaggeration. The strongest understanding of India's scientific past is neither to deny its contributions nor to exaggerate them, but to examine the surviving evidence critically.

Question: Which of the following best captures the central argument of the passage?

  1. Ancient Indian scientific achievements were primarily borrowed from other civilisations.
  2. Ancient Indian knowledge developed through an interaction of ritual, practical needs, scientific inquiry and wider social activity.
  3. Vedic knowledge was exclusively religious and lacked practical applications.
  4. Ancient Indian science developed independently of social and economic activity.
Show answer

Answer: B. Ancient Indian knowledge developed through an interaction of ritual, practical needs, scientific inquiry and wider social activity.

Passage: Education and the Colonial Mind

Education is often presented as a means of liberation. It teaches people to read, think, question and understand the world around them. Yet education can also become an instrument of power when the knowledge being taught is controlled by a dominant group. Several African, African-American and post-colonial thinkers have therefore examined education not merely as the transmission of knowledge, but as a means through which societies can be shaped.

Frantz Fanon, in The Wretched of the Earth and Black Skin, White Masks, examined the psychological consequences of colonialism. He argued that colonial education, language and culture could lead the educated colonial subject to internalise the values of the coloniser. The educated person could consequently become separated from the culture and experiences of his own people. Ngũgĩ wa Thiong'o developed a related argument in Decolonising the Mind. For him, language was especially important because language does not merely communicate ideas; it also influences how people understand reality. If the language and literature of the coloniser become dominant, the colonised may gradually begin to see their own world through the conceptual framework of another culture.

Amílcar Cabral, in Return to the Source, similarly emphasised the relationship between education, culture and political struggle. Colonialism, in his view, could produce an educated class that was culturally distant from the wider population. Julius Nyerere, in Education for Self-Reliance, criticised an inherited educational system that prepared Africans for European-style administration and employment instead of addressing the actual needs of African societies. Walter Rodney, in How Europe Underdeveloped Africa, connected education with the economic structure of colonialism. Education could produce clerks, administrators and subordinate professionals required by the colonial system rather than people capable of directing an autonomous society.

A similar concern appears in the work of Carter G. Woodson, The Mis-Education of the Negro, and Paulo Freire, Pedagogy of the Oppressed. Woodson argued that education controlled by dominant institutions could teach oppressed people to accept an existing social order rather than question it. Freire described this as the “banking” model of education, in which knowledge is deposited into passive students. Education, therefore, may reproduce existing power rather than challenge it.

This argument can also be connected to Thomas Babington Macaulay's famous Minute on Indian Education of 1835. Macaulay advocated an education system based largely on English knowledge and language and famously envisaged creating a class of persons “Indian in blood and colour, but English in tastes.” The purpose was not simply to educate Indians in an abstract sense. Education was also expected to create an intermediary class capable of assisting British administration. In this respect, colonial education illustrates a larger imperial strategy: control need not always operate through force; it can also operate by influencing what people learn, which language they use, whose history they study and what kind of society they regard as desirable.

The deeper lesson emerging from these thinkers is that imperial power could extend beyond the occupation of territory. By influencing education and knowledge, imperialism could influence the minds through which future societies would understand themselves. Colonialism could therefore leave behind institutions and habits of thought even after formal political control had ended.

Question: According to the passage, why can education become an instrument of power?

  1. Because the group controlling education can influence what knowledge, language and values are considered important.
  2. Because educated people are generally more willing to accept foreign cultures.
  3. Because education is primarily designed to produce government employees.
  4. Because education necessarily prevents people from questioning authority.
Show answer

Answer: A. Because the group controlling education can influence what knowledge, language and values are considered important.

Education could produce clerks, administrators and subordinate professionals required by the colonial system rather than people capable of directing an autonomous society.

A similar concern appears in the work of Carter G. Woodson, The Mis-Education of the Negro, and Paulo Freire, Pedagogy of the Oppressed. Woodson argued that education controlled by dominant institutions could teach oppressed people to accept an existing social order rather than question it. Freire described this as the “banking” model of education, in which knowledge is deposited into passive students. Education, therefore, may reproduce existing power rather than challenge it.

This argument can also be connected to Thomas Babington Macaulay's famous Minute on Indian Education of 1835. Macaulay advocated an education system based largely on English knowledge and language and famously envisaged creating a class of persons “Indian in blood and colour, but English in tastes.” The purpose was not simply to educate Indians in an abstract sense. Education was also expected to create an intermediary class capable of assisting British administration. In this respect, colonial education illustrates a larger imperial strategy: control need not always operate through force; it can also operate by influencing what people learn, which language they use, whose history they study and what kind of society they regard as desirable.

The deeper lesson emerging from these thinkers is that imperial power could extend beyond the occupation of territory. By influencing education and knowledge, imperialism could influence the minds through which future societies would understand themselves. Colonialism could therefore leave behind institutions and habits of thought even after formal political control had ended.

Passage: Education and the Colonial Mind

Education is often presented as a means of liberation. It teaches people to read, think, question and understand the world around them. Yet education can also become an instrument of power when the knowledge being taught is controlled by a dominant group. Several African, African-American and post-colonial thinkers have therefore examined education not merely as the transmission of knowledge, but as a means through which societies can be shaped.

Frantz Fanon, in The Wretched of the Earth and Black Skin, White Masks, examined the psychological consequences of colonialism. He argued that colonial education, language and culture could lead the educated colonial subject to internalise the values of the coloniser. The educated person could consequently become separated from the culture and experiences of his own people. Ngũgĩ wa Thiong'o developed a related argument in Decolonising the Mind. For him, language was especially important because language does not merely communicate ideas; it also influences how people understand reality. If the language and literature of the coloniser become dominant, the colonised may gradually begin to see their own world through the conceptual framework of another culture.

Amílcar Cabral, in Return to the Source, similarly emphasised the relationship between education, culture and political struggle. Passage: Colonial Education and the Making of Modern India

Education is often described as a gift of modernity, but the history of colonial India shows that education can also become an instrument of political power. The British did not introduce education into an empty intellectual landscape. India possessed diverse systems of indigenous schooling, learning and knowledge. Dharampal, in The Beautiful Tree: Indigenous Indian Education in the Eighteenth Century, challenged the assumption that India had been educationally backward before British intervention. Using colonial administrative records, he reconstructed evidence of indigenous schools and educational practices. His work therefore invites us to ask not only what the British introduced, but also what forms of learning already existed and what happened to them under colonial rule.

The purpose of colonial education was not uniform or unchanging. It developed alongside the changing requirements of British rule. Thomas Babington Macaulay's Minute on Indian Education of 1835 is particularly important in this context. Macaulay argued for the promotion of English education and envisaged the creation of a class that could serve as an intermediary between the colonial government and the wider Indian population. Education could therefore serve an administrative purpose: it could produce people familiar with the language, ideas and institutional practices of the rulers.

Gauri Viswanathan, in Masks of Conquest: Literary Study and British Rule in India, develops this argument further. She examines the growth of English literary education in colonial India and argues that literature could acquire a political function within the colonial system. English literature appeared to offer moral improvement and intellectual refinement, yet the educational system in which it was taught could also encourage cultural assimilation and facilitate political control. The significance of colonial education therefore lay not merely in what students were taught, but also in the cultural framework through which they were encouraged to understand knowledge and civilisation.

Frantz Fanon provides a broader psychological perspective in Black Skin, White Masks. He examined how colonial subjects could internalise the cultural hierarchy established by colonial society. Mastery of the coloniser's language and familiarity with the dominant culture could become associated with education, status and social advancement. Education could consequently create not only knowledge but also new ideas about what constituted refinement, success and civilisation.

Yet colonial education produced consequences that could not be completely controlled by the colonial state. The same English education that was intended partly to strengthen administration also exposed Indians to ideas concerning liberty, equality, rights, representation and self-government. Educated Indians could use the intellectual tools provided by colonial institutions to question the legitimacy of colonial rule itself. Thus, education could become an instrument of both domination and resistance.

The history of colonial education in India consequently presents a paradox. A system designed partly to make colonial administration more effective could also create individuals capable of criticising that administration. The attempt to shape the colonial subject intellectually could therefore produce an unintended consequence: the educated subject might begin to question the authority of the very system that educated him. Colonial education did not simply modernise India, nor did it operate only as an instrument of oppression; it became part of a complicated process through which colonial power, cultural change and Indian nationalism interacted.

  3. British education completely replaced India's indigenous educational traditions.
  4. Indigenous education disappeared immediately after the introduction of English education.

Answer: A. Colonial education had both administrative and cultural purposes and could simultaneously become a means of domination and resistance.
