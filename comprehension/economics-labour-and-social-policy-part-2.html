Passage: Reagan on the Economy

Ronald Reagan's economic argument, set out in his memoir An American Life and in the speeches collected in Speaking My Mind, rests on one conviction: that government, not the citizen, is the usual cause of economic failure. In the 1970s, he argued, Washington taxed work and investment so heavily that productive people had little reason to earn more. Meanwhile inflation, which followed from government printing money to cover government spending, quietly robbed the thrifty of what they had saved. Both, in his view, were taxes imposed without a vote.

Reagan drew on his own experience. He recalled that during his film career, when the top marginal rate on high earners was very steep, there came a point at which taking another picture left him with so little of the extra income that it was not worth doing. What he saw as a private calculation in Hollywood, he reasoned, was being repeated by millions of workers, managers and investors across the country, and the sum of those small decisions was a slower economy.

A tax rate, Reagan held, is a price set on effort; raise the price and people buy less of it. High marginal rates therefore did not merely collect revenue but suppressed the very activity that generated it. Cutting rates, as the 1981 tax act did by reducing the top rate from 70 to 50 per cent, and as the 1986 reform did by bringing it down to 28, would restore incentives, widen the tax base and eventually enlarge revenue. He had no patience for the view that a planner in the capital could allocate resources better than millions of people spending their own money for their own purposes.

Reagan did not deny that government has duties. He wanted it to defend the country, enforce contracts and help those truly unable to help themselves. But he regarded the growth of federal programmes beyond those limits as a transfer of power from the individual to the bureaucrat, and a slow corrosion of the self-reliance on which a free society depends. A government that spends beyond its means, he added, merely postpones the bill and hands it to children who had no say in the matter.

Question: Which of the following, if true, would most weaken Reagan's argument for cutting rates?

  1. Inflation reduces the real value of savings
  2. Government must defend the country and enforce contracts
  3. Individuals generally spend their own money prudently
  4. After large rate cuts, work and investment did not rise and revenue fell sharply
Show answer

Answer: D. After large rate cuts, work and investment did not rise and revenue fell sharply

Passage: Labour Arbitrage in IT: The Case For

The offshoring of IT work from the United States to India is, on the strongest reading, an application of the oldest principle in economics: trade makes both parties richer when each does what it does at lower opportunity cost. When a US bank has its software maintained in Bengaluru or Pune, a task that costs a fraction of its American price is performed by engineers fully able to do it, and the saving does not vanish. It returns as lower prices, higher profit, and capital for hiring, research and expansion in higher-value work at home. Companies that refuse to use global talent do not preserve jobs; they lose to rivals that do.

The history of the trade supports this reading. The scramble to repair date-related software defects before the year 2000 first showed American firms how reliable and cheap Indian engineering teams could be, and the relationship, once begun, deepened because it served both sides. Indian firms went on to open offices, hire locally and pay taxes in the United States, so the flow of work was never one-directional. A small American company that could not afford a full engineering department could now build a product at a cost it could bear, and many new firms exist because of it.

The wage gap is not exploitation but a signal. It tells capital where skilled labour is abundant and underpriced, and it tells ambitious young Indians where opportunity lies. Three decades of such work built an IT services industry, a large services-export surplus, and an educated urban middle class that no state development plan had achieved. Wages in these cities have risen sharply, narrowing the very gap that created the trade, which is exactly what arbitrage is expected to do.

Those who demand visa curbs and outsourcing taxes mistake the visible loss of a few jobs for the invisible gain across the whole economy, and ask consumers to subsidise inefficiency. Automation and changing technology have displaced far more work than offshoring ever has, and no one proposes protecting workers from the spreadsheet. Protecting an occupation from competition has never made it more productive, and a nation that tries ends by making its own industries uncompetitive.

Question: Which of the following best expresses the author's position?

  1. Visa restrictions are necessary to protect middle-class jobs
  2. Offshoring IT work benefits both countries, and restricting it is mistaken
  3. Wage gaps between countries should be removed by regulation
  4. Offshoring benefits India but harms the United States
Show answer

Answer: B. Offshoring IT work benefits both countries, and restricting it is mistaken

Passage: Labour Arbitrage in IT: The Case Against

Critics of the offshoring and visa model in the IT industry argue that "labour arbitrage" is a polite name for wage suppression. The gain, they say, is real but badly distributed. Firms capture the saving and shareholders are rewarded, while the American engineer who is asked to train his replacement before the layoff is told that this is the market at work. Temporary work visas, meant to fill genuine shortages, are in this view used to bring in workers at lower pay and with less freedom to leave their employer. That creates a workforce that cannot bargain and depresses the wage of those who can.

Critics point out that the arrangement rewards the firm that finds the cheapest route, not the one that builds the best product. A company that can replace a mid-career engineer with two cheaper ones has no reason to train anyone, so the cost of training is pushed onto the worker and, eventually, onto society. Workers in their forties and fifties, whose pay reflects experience, are the first to be released, and they find that a lifetime of skill has become a liability in a market that prices labour by the cheapest bidder. Their complaint is not that foreigners are unworthy, but that the rules let some employers play one worker against another.

The damage goes beyond wages. Entry-level programming and support work are the rungs by which graduates become senior engineers; when those rungs are exported, the domestic pipeline narrows and the skills base thins within a decade. A country that offshores the apprenticeship cannot later complain that it lacks experts. Critics add that the arbitrage enriches the intermediaries who profit from the difference, while workers on both sides are kept in a competition that holds the wage low everywhere.

To say that consumers gain from lower prices is, they argue, to define a person only as a buyer and never as a worker. A society is more than a marketplace, and a policy that makes its citizens' skills worthless has no claim to efficiency. The remedy, they conclude, is not to ban trade in services but to stop designing visa rules and tax incentives that reward replacement over investment.

Question: Which of the following best states the critics' position?

  1. Lower prices justify the loss of domestic jobs
  2. Visa schemes should be expanded to fill shortages
  3. Labour arbitrage in IT benefits firms at the expense of workers and long-term skills
  4. Offshoring benefits workers in both countries equally
Show answer

Answer: C. Labour arbitrage in IT benefits firms at the expense of workers and long-term skills

Passage: Labour Arbitrage in IT: The Case Against

Critics of the offshoring and visa model in the IT industry argue that "labour arbitrage" is a polite name for wage suppression. The gain, they say, is real but badly distributed. Firms capture the saving and shareholders are rewarded, while the American engineer who is asked to train his replacement before the layoff is told that this is the market at work. Temporary work visas, meant to fill genuine shortages, are in this view used to bring in workers at lower pay and with less freedom to leave their employer. That creates a workforce that cannot bargain and depresses the wage of those who can.

Critics point out that the arrangement rewards the firm that finds the cheapest route, not the one that builds the best product. A company that can replace a mid-career engineer with two cheaper ones has no reason to train anyone, so the cost of training is pushed onto the worker and, eventually, onto society. Workers in their forties and fifties, whose pay reflects experience, are the first to be released, and they find that a lifetime of skill has become a liability in a market that prices labour by the cheapest bidder. Their complaint is not that foreigners are unworthy, but that the rules let some employers play one worker against another.

The damage goes beyond wages. Entry-level programming and support work are the rungs by which graduates become senior engineers; when those rungs are exported, the domestic pipeline narrows and the skills base thins within a decade. A country that offshores the apprenticeship cannot later complain that it lacks experts. Critics add that the arbitrage enriches the intermediaries who profit from the difference, while workers on both sides are kept in a competition that holds the wage low everywhere.

To say that consumers gain from lower prices is, they argue, to define a person only as a buyer and never as a worker. A society is more than a marketplace, and a policy that makes its citizens' skills worthless has no claim to efficiency. The remedy, they conclude, is not to ban trade in services but to stop designing visa rules and tax incentives that reward replacement over investment.

Question: Which of the following is NOT part of the critics' argument?

  1. Temporary visa workers have more freedom than local workers to change employers
  2. Visa workers have limited bargaining power
  3. Experienced mid-career workers are the first to be released
  4. Offshoring thins the domestic skills pipeline
Show answer

Answer: A. Temporary visa workers have more freedom than local workers to change employers

Passage: Artificial Intelligence and the Future of Human Labour

The arrival of artificial intelligence has revived an old question about human labour: what happens when the advantage once associated with intelligence becomes widely available? For much of economic history, differences in productivity were partly differences in access to knowledge, training and cognitive ability. A person who could analyse information faster, write more clearly, calculate more accurately or recognise patterns more readily possessed an advantage over someone who could not. Artificial intelligence unsettles this arrangement because it can increasingly perform many of these intellectual tasks at a fraction of the time and cost.

This does not necessarily mean that human beings become economically irrelevant. It may instead mean that one important source of inequality between workers becomes less significant. An inefficient worker, when assisted by a capable machine, may produce an output approaching that of a more efficient worker. In this sense, AI can be understood not merely as a labour-saving technology but as a technology that reduces the distance between different levels of intellectual performance. The machine can compensate for deficiencies in memory, calculation, drafting, research and even certain forms of reasoning.

Yet this interpretation contains an important limitation. Intelligence, even when it can be automated, does not determine the structure within which it is used. A machine may identify the most efficient way of achieving an objective without determining whether that objective is worth pursuing. It may compare alternatives without deciding which values should govern the comparison. The distinction is important because societies do not merely have problems of knowledge; they also have problems of choice.

This point brings to mind Herbert Simon's distinction between decision-making and the broader structures within which decisions occur. Human beings do not simply solve problems; they define them, select the constraints under which they are to be solved and determine what constitutes a satisfactory outcome. Similarly, Hannah Arendt's reflections on labour and human activity suggest that productive activity cannot be understood entirely through the category of technical efficiency. Human purposes and institutions give meaning and direction to what is produced.

The consequence may be that AI shifts the economic importance of human beings rather than simply diminishing it. If machines increasingly solve the problem of how something can be done, greater importance may attach to deciding what should be done, why it should be done and for whom. These are questions of institutional design, political choice and social priorities. They cannot be settled merely by producing a more intelligent answer.

This also complicates the popular claim that AI will necessarily create a society in which merit becomes irrelevant. It may reduce the premium attached to certain forms of individual intellectual competence while increasing the premium attached to judgment, coordination and the ability to structure complex systems. A person who cannot personally perform every intellectual task may nevertheless be highly effective if that person can formulate the right questions, evaluate competing objectives and organise human and machine capabilities toward a coherent purpose.

The central transformation, therefore, may not be from human labour to machine labour, but from performing intellectual tasks to directing systems capable of performing them. The scarce resource may gradually cease to be intelligence itself and become the capacity to determine where intelligence should be applied. AI can democratise cognitive power; it cannot, by itself, democratise judgment. The former concerns the production of answers. The latter concerns the choice of questions, purposes and consequences.

In such a world, human discretion does not disappear merely because machines become more capable. It becomes visible in a different place.

  3. AI primarily benefits highly skilled workers by increasing the gap between them and inefficient workers.
  4. AI will eventually eliminate the need for most forms of human labour.

Answer: A. AI may reduce differences in intellectual performance while increasing the importance of human judgment and system design.
