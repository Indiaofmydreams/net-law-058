Passage: Tagore on Nationalism

Rabindranath Tagore's lectures on nationalism, delivered in Japan and the United States during the First World War and collected in Nationalism, begin with a distinction that his hearers found uncomfortable: a society and a nation are not the same thing. A society, he argued, is the natural form in which human beings live together, bound by kinship, custom and sympathy, and it grows by its own life. A nation is something different: the political and commercial organisation of a people for the purpose of power. It is a machine, and a machine, however efficient, has no heart.

The danger, in his view, lay in what the machine demanded. The nation, being organised for self-interest, trains its citizens to regard the aggrandisement of the state as a virtue and to treat other peoples as markets, rivals or prey. Greed, he warned, becomes patriotism when it wears a flag. The West, he said, had prospered through this power, but at the price of its own moral life. An individual who would be ashamed to lie for personal profit will lie without hesitation for the national interest, and call it duty. He saw the war then raging as the natural fruit of such a civilisation, and he refused to be cheered by the thought that India might one day be admitted into its company.

Tagore was equally critical of Japan, which he thought had imitated European nationalism swiftly and without reflection, and he doubted that a people could preserve its soul while copying a creed whose success lay in strength. For India he insisted that the central problem was not political but social. Caste, communal division and the treatment of the poor had weakened the country long before any foreign ruler arrived, and replacing a foreign nation-state by an Indian one would not heal these wounds. India's history, he held, had been a patient effort to bring many races and faiths into a common life, an effort of reconciliation rather than conquest, and this offered the world a better lesson than the cult of the nation.

He did not despise love of one's country; what he rejected was the claim that the country's interest outranks the claims of humanity. Freedom, for him, was not the right of a state to dominate but the capacity of a people to be generous and truthful.

Question: Which of the following best states Tagore's central claim in the passage?

  1. Society is an artificial form that the nation improves upon
  2. The nation, as a political organisation of power, endangers moral life and humanity
  3. India should imitate Japan's rapid national organisation
  4. Western nations have failed only because of their wars
Show answer

Answer: B. The nation, as a political organisation of power, endangers moral life and humanity

Passage: Deendayal Upadhyaya and Integral Humanism

Pandit Deendayal Upadhyaya set out his philosophy in four lectures delivered in Bombay in 1965, later published as Integral Humanism, and in his writings in Panchjanya and Organiser. His starting point is that neither of the two dominant ideologies of the century, capitalism and socialism (with communism as its extreme form), grasps the whole of man. Both, he argued, start from matter. One makes the individual an acquisitive animal in a market; the other reduces him to a cog in a collective; each serves only part of his nature and sets the individual against society.

Man, Upadhyaya held, is a four-fold being: body, mind, intellect and soul, with needs corresponding to each. A philosophy that satisfies only bodily wants is a half-truth. The Indian tradition, he said, recognised four ends of human life, dharma, artha, kama and moksha, and placed dharma first, since it is dharma that gives artha and kama their limit and moksha its direction. Economics, therefore, could not be an independent science; it must serve a moral purpose. He wanted a system that guaranteed a minimum livelihood for every person, sought the maximum employment, and limited the concentration of wealth, while avoiding the over-centralisation of both industry and the state.

Society, in his thought, is not a contract between individuals nor a mere aggregate, but a living organism with a collective soul, which he called chiti. Each nation has its own chiti, formed by its history and culture, and the energy that arises from it, which he called virat, is the nation's power to act. The nation must be understood from within, through its culture, rather than by borrowing a ready-made ideology from outside. Imitating the West, he warned, would produce neither Western prosperity nor Indian renewal, but a hollow copy.

His practical creed followed. He championed swadeshi and decentralised production, the dignity of the last person, which he called antyodaya, and an economy measured by the welfare of the poorest rather than the growth of a few. The state, he said, is not the source of dharma; it is bound by it, and a ruler who violates dharma loses his claim to obedience. The individual, the family, society and the state form a series of widening circles in which each supports the others, and the conflict between the individual and society, so central to Western thought, was in his view a false problem.

Question: Which of the following best states Upadhyaya's central claim?

  1. Socialism is the only moral economic system
  2. Both capitalism and socialism neglect the whole man, and India needs an integral, dharma-based alternative
  3. Western ideologies should be adopted with minor changes
  4. Capitalism is correct but needs more state control
Show answer

Answer: B. Both capitalism and socialism neglect the whole man, and India needs an integral, dharma-based alternative

He championed swadeshi and decentralised production, the dignity of the last person, which he called antyodaya, and an economy measured by the welfare of the poorest rather than the growth of a few. The state, he said, is not the source of dharma; it is bound by it, and a ruler who violates dharma loses his claim to obedience. The individual, the family, society and the state form a series of widening circles in which each supports the others, and the conflict between the individual and society, so central to Western thought, was in his view a false problem.

  4. The state is the source of dharma

Passage: J. P. Narayan on the Emergency

Jayaprakash Narayan's judgement on the Emergency of June 1975, set down in his Prison Diary and in the letters he wrote from detention at Chandigarh, was that it was not a defence of the Constitution but a coup carried out through the Constitution. The Emergency was declared within days of the Allahabad High Court's judgment against the Prime Minister's election and of the rally at which he had urged the police and the army not to carry out illegal orders. The "internal disturbance" cited as its ground, he said, was a pretext. The movement he led in Bihar and across the country had been peaceful, and the only danger it posed was to one person's hold on office.

JP argued that democracy is not simply a procedure of periodic voting but a living system whose lifeblood is a free press, an independent judiciary, the freedom of association and the freedom of citizens to dissent. The Emergency, by censoring newspapers, detaining opposition leaders without trial and suspending fundamental rights, had cut at every one of these. A government that cannot be criticised, he argued, ceases to be a government of the people and becomes a tyranny, however regularly it may hold elections. He was particularly bitter that the judiciary, which should have guarded the citizen, had been cowed, and that Parliament had been reduced to a rubber stamp.

He did not confine his criticism to one leader. The Emergency had been possible, he said, because power had been allowed to concentrate and parties had become instruments of a person or a clique rather than of the people. This was why his programme of Total Revolution, sampoorna kranti, sought change at the level of the individual and the village, not only of government: political, economic, social, cultural, educational and moral transformation. Without a citizenry awake to its rights and its duties, he held, any institution would be captured.

Yet JP held that the answer lay in non-violent resistance and in the ballot. He urged the people to prepare for the elections that he believed would come, and in 1977 the defeat of the ruling party vindicated his faith that the Indian voter could not be permanently cowed.
