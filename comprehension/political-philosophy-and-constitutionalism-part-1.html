Passage: Constitutionalism and the “Grammar of Anarchy”

Constitutional democracy depends not only on written rules but also on a political culture that accepts constitutional methods for pursuing political objectives. The existence of elections, legislatures, courts and other representative institutions changes the relationship between political disagreement and political action. Once citizens possess constitutional avenues for expressing grievances and changing governments, the continued use of extra-constitutional methods raises questions about the stability and legitimacy of democratic institutions.

B. R. Ambedkar, in his 1949 Constituent Assembly speech, warned against the continued reliance on civil disobedience, non-cooperation and satyagraha after constitutional methods had become available. He described such practices as the “Grammar of Anarchy”, arguing that methods appropriate under colonial rule could become dangerous when transferred into a constitutional democracy.

This concern has parallels in the writings of other political thinkers. John Stuart Mill defended representative government and warned against concentrating political authority in individuals rather than institutions. His emphasis on representative institutions supports the idea that political power should operate through established constitutional mechanisms. Edmund Burke, in his criticism of the French Revolution, similarly preferred gradual reform to revolutionary destruction of inherited political institutions. For Burke, political change that disregards institutional continuity can produce consequences that reformers cannot control.

Alexis de Tocqueville also warned that revolutionary politics could weaken intermediary institutions and encourage excessive centralisation. Karl Popper, in The Open Society and Its Enemies, criticised attempts to reconstruct society according to comprehensive revolutionary designs and instead advocated piecemeal social engineering. His argument reflects a broader distrust of political projects that seek to transform society through abrupt and total reconstruction.

Max Weber provides another perspective by distinguishing institutional authority from charismatic or revolutionary forms of authority. This distinction helps explain why movements outside established institutions may acquire powerful political legitimacy while simultaneously challenging the authority of constitutional structures.

The argument, therefore, is not that protest or dissent is inherently illegitimate. Rather, the crucial distinction is between constitutional opposition and methods that seek to bypass or undermine constitutional institutions. A democracy must permit disagreement, but its long-term stability depends upon political actors recognising that the pursuit of political ends is itself subject to constitutional limits.

Question: What is the central argument of the passage?

  1. Revolutionary movements are always unsuccessful.
  2. Political protest is incompatible with democracy.
  3. Constitutional democracy requires political actors to pursue change primarily through established constitutional institutions.
  4. Constitutional institutions should prevent all forms of dissent.
Show answer

Answer: C. Constitutional democracy requires political actors to pursue change primarily through established constitutional institutions.

Passage: The Crisis of Liberalism

Liberalism has been one of the most influential political ideas of the modern age. It places considerable importance on individual freedom, autonomy, private choice, equality before the law and, in many versions, free markets. Its historical achievements are substantial: liberal institutions helped challenge inherited privilege and expanded the sphere of individual rights. Yet contemporary thinkers have increasingly asked whether some of liberalism's difficulties arise not from its failure to follow its principles, but from the consequences of following those principles too far.

Patrick J. Deneen makes one of the strongest versions of this argument in Why Liberalism Failed. His claim is not simply that liberal societies have failed to become sufficiently liberal. Rather, he argues that some of the tensions within liberal societies may result from liberalism's own success. The expansion of individual autonomy can weaken the social relationships, traditions and institutions that once shaped individual behaviour. When people are increasingly encouraged to understand themselves primarily as autonomous individuals, the obligations associated with family, community and inherited social practices may become less compelling.

John Gray develops a related but distinct criticism in False Dawn and his later writings. His concern is particularly directed towards an unrestricted form of market liberalism. Markets can generate wealth and freedom, but Gray questions the assumption that market mechanisms can safely replace the social institutions and communal practices on which stable societies depend. If every aspect of social life is treated as a transaction between individuals, institutions that cannot easily be expressed in terms of market value may gradually be weakened.

Samuel Moyn offers a different criticism in Liberalism Against Itself. His argument challenges the assumption that contemporary liberalism simply became too permissive or too individualistic. Moyn examines the transformation of liberal thought during the Cold War and argues that liberalism became increasingly concerned with restricting political transformation. Earlier forms of liberalism had contained more ambitious ideas about emancipation and social change. Cold War liberalism, in his account, increasingly emphasised order, stability and the protection of existing institutions against radical political alternatives. Thus, liberalism's problem may sometimes be that it became too restrictive, rather than simply too expansive.

Adrian Pabst approaches the question from another direction. His criticism concerns the tendency to reduce politics to individual choice and market relationships. A society, he argues, cannot be understood merely as a collection of independent individuals making private choices. Communities, institutions, solidarity and shared obligations also contribute to political and social life. The question therefore becomes whether a political order can preserve individual freedom while also recognising the social structures that make meaningful freedom possible.

These criticisms do not necessarily establish that liberalism has become irrelevant. Rather, they reveal a deeper disagreement about what freedom requires. If freedom is understood only as the absence of restrictions on individual choice, liberalism may appear to demand fewer social constraints. If freedom also depends upon functioning families, communities, institutions and shared purposes, excessive individualisation may itself become a limitation on human flourishing.

The contemporary debate is therefore not simply about whether liberalism should be accepted or rejected. It concerns a more difficult question: how can individual liberty coexist with the institutions, traditions and forms of solidarity upon which a society depends? The answer may determine whether the future involves a reform of liberalism, a movement beyond it, or a renewed interpretation of its original promises.

Question: What is the central criticism of liberalism advanced by Patrick J. Deneen in the passage?

  1. Liberalism has failed because it has not provided sufficient individual freedom.
  2. Liberalism has completely eliminated traditional social institutions.
  3. Liberalism's difficulties may arise partly from the successful application of its own principles, particularly the expansion of individual autonomy.
  4. Liberalism is primarily responsible for the growth of authoritarian governments.
Show answer

Answer: C. Liberalism's difficulties may arise partly from the successful application of its own principles, particularly the expansion of individual autonomy.

If freedom also depends upon functioning families, communities, institutions and shared purposes, excessive individualisation may itself become a limitation on human flourishing.

The contemporary debate is therefore not simply about whether liberalism should be accepted or rejected. It concerns a more difficult question: how can individual liberty coexist with the institutions, traditions and forms of solidarity upon which a society depends? The answer may determine whether the future involves a reform of liberalism, a movement beyond it, or a renewed interpretation of its original promises.

Passage: The Crisis of Liberalism

Liberalism has been one of the most influential political ideas of the modern age. It places considerable importance on individual freedom, autonomy, private choice, equality before the law and, in many versions, free markets. Its historical achievements are substantial: liberal institutions helped challenge inherited privilege and expanded the sphere of individual rights. Yet contemporary thinkers have increasingly asked whether some of liberalism's difficulties arise not from its failure to follow its principles, but from the consequences of following those principles too far.

Patrick J. Deneen makes one of the strongest versions of this argument in Why Liberalism Failed. His claim is not simply that liberal societies have failed to become sufficiently liberal. Rather, he argues that some of the tensions within liberal societies may result from liberalism's own success. The expansion of individual autonomy can weaken the social relationships, traditions and institutions that once shaped individual behaviour. When people are increasingly encouraged to understand themselves primarily as autonomous individuals, the obligations associated with family, community and inherited social practices may become less compelling.

John Gray develops a related but distinct criticism in False Dawn and his later writings. His concern is particularly directed towards an unrestricted form of market liberalism. Markets can generate wealth and freedom, but Gray questions the assumption that market mechanisms can safely replace the social institutions and communal practices on which stable societies depend. If every aspect of social life is treated as a transaction between individuals, institutions that cannot easily be expressed in terms of market value may gradually be weakened.

Samuel Moyn offers a different criticism in Liberalism Against Itself. His argument challenges the assumption that contemporary liberalism simply became too permissive or too individualistic. Moyn examines the transformation of liberal thought during the Cold War and argues that liberalism became increasingly concerned with restricting political transformation. Earlier forms of liberalism had contained more ambitious ideas about emancipation and social change. Cold War liberalism, in his account, increasingly emphasised order, stability and the protection of existing institutions against radical political alternatives. Thus, liberalism's problem may sometimes be that it became too restrictive, rather than simply too expansive.

Adrian Pabst approaches the question from another direction. His criticism concerns the tendency to reduce politics to individual choice and market relationships. A society, he argues, cannot be understood merely as a collection of independent individuals making private choices. Communities, institutions, solidarity and shared obligations also contribute to political and social life. The question therefore becomes whether a political order can preserve individual freedom while also recognising the social structures that make meaningful freedom possible.

These criticisms do not necessarily establish that liberalism has become irrelevant. Rather, they reveal a deeper disagreement about what freedom requires. If freedom is understood only as the absence of restrictions on individual choice, liberalism may appear to demand fewer social constraints. If freedom also depends upon functioning families, communities, institutions and shared purposes, excessive individualisation may itself become a limitation on human flourishing.

The contemporary debate is therefore not simply about whether liberalism should be accepted or rejected. It concerns a more difficult question: how can individual liberty coexist with the institutions, traditions and forms of solidarity upon which a society depends? The answer may determine whether the future involves a reform of liberalism, a movement beyond it, or a renewed interpretation of its original promises.

Passage: How Modern Political Movements Gain Power

Political revolutions are often imagined in traditional terms: an army crosses a border, a ruler is overthrown, and a new political order emerges. Modern political change, however, can follow a less visible path. A government may face a challenge that begins not with an invading force, but with a domestic grievance that gradually develops into an organised political movement. The transformation from dissatisfaction into political power depends upon several conditions, including organisation, communication and the ability to mobilise resources.

A grievance by itself is rarely sufficient to produce political change. Societies may contain widespread poverty, inequality, corruption or political exclusion for long periods without producing a successful challenge to the government. What converts dissatisfaction into political pressure is often organisational capacity. Civil society organisations, professional associations, student groups, religious organisations and informal networks can provide people with methods of coordination. They can transform individuals who are separately dissatisfied into a collective capable of acting towards a common objective.

Information networks can strengthen this process. In earlier periods, governments could exercise considerable control over information by controlling newspapers, radio and other major channels of communication. Modern communication technologies have made it easier for individuals to circulate information rapidly and to discover that dissatisfaction is shared by others. A protest that initially appears local can therefore acquire a national or even international character. Information does not automatically create a successful movement, but it can reduce the difficulty of coordination and increase the visibility of political action.

The relationship between information and political mobilisation is nevertheless complicated. Information can expose abuses of power, but it can also spread rumours, exaggerations and competing narratives. The existence of a communication network therefore does not guarantee that a movement will succeed. What matters is whether information can be converted into sustained organisation, public support and political pressure.

External assistance introduces another dimension. A domestic movement may receive financial resources, technological support, training, diplomatic recognition or other forms of assistance from actors outside the country. Such assistance can alter the balance between a government and its challengers. However, external involvement does not necessarily determine the outcome. A movement that lacks domestic legitimacy or organisational strength may remain weak despite substantial outside support. Conversely, a movement with strong domestic foundations may use external assistance to expand capabilities that already exist.

The modern revolution, therefore, should not be understood simply as a confrontation between two armed forces. It may be better understood as a struggle over organisation, information, legitimacy and resources. The decisive question is not merely whether a population has a grievance, but whether that grievance can be organised into collective action and whether that collective action can acquire sufficient capacity to challenge existing institutions.

This does not mean that governments have become powerless. States continue to possess coercive, administrative and economic resources. They may restrict communication, weaken organisations, control institutions and attempt to discredit challengers. The outcome of political conflict consequently depends upon the interaction between the state's capacity and the challengers' ability to build and sustain an alternative source of political power.
