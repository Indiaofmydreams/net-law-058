Passage: Reagan and the Cold War

Reagan's foreign policy, as he lays it out in An American Life, begins with a moral judgement that his critics found embarrassing and he never retracted: that the Soviet Union was not merely a rival but a system founded on the denial of human freedom. Détente, which accepted the Soviet empire as a permanent fact and sought only to manage it, seemed to him a policy of drift. He held that a regime that jailed its dissenters and walled in its people could not be bargained into virtue; it could only be made to see that continued aggression would not pay.

Hence his doctrine of peace through strength. Weakness invites aggression, he argued, while a nation clearly able to defend itself rarely has to. The military build-up and the Strategic Defense Initiative, which proposed to defend against missiles rather than rely on the threat of mutual destruction, were meant to confront Moscow with a technological and economic race it could not win. At the 1986 Reykjavik summit he refused to bargain away the defence programme even when a sweeping disarmament deal seemed within reach, judging that a bad agreement was worse than none. Reagan believed that talks held from strength would succeed where talks held from anxiety would fail. The 1987 agreement to eliminate an entire class of intermediate-range missiles, he held, vindicated the order of events: arms first, then agreement. Even then he insisted on verification, borrowing a Russian proverb to say that he would trust, but check.

Reagan also believed that the contest was finally one of ideas. Ordinary people under communism, he reasoned, wanted what people everywhere want: to work, worship and speak without fear. When he called on the Soviet leader to open the gate and tear down the Berlin Wall, he was addressing not only Moscow but those who lived behind it. For Reagan, honesty was part of the strategy. To call the Soviet system by its name was to encourage those inside it who already knew it to be a lie.

Question: Which of the following best states Reagan's approach to the Soviet Union?

  1. Accommodation through economic concessions
  2. Firmness and moral clarity, negotiating from strength
  3. Permanent management of the rivalry
  4. Complete military withdrawal from Europe
Show answer

Answer: B. Firmness and moral clarity, negotiating from strength

Passage: “America First” and the Changing International Order

The return of an “America First” foreign policy has raised questions about the future of the international order that the United States itself played a major role in constructing. President Donald Trump's use of tariffs, transactional diplomacy and a more explicitly sovereignty-centred approach has been criticised not merely because tariffs restrict trade, but because such policies may weaken the institutions and expectations that make international relations more predictable.

One important criticism concerns the relationship between national sovereignty and international rules. A state may reasonably seek to protect its own economic interests, industries and workers. However, when powerful states repeatedly use unilateral economic measures to obtain concessions, other states may begin to question whether common rules genuinely constrain the strongest members of the international system. Joel Adu-Quaye and Petros C. Mavroidis distinguish between the immediate economic consequences of tariffs and what they regard as the potentially deeper damage to the institutions responsible for enforcing international trade rules. They suggest that the international system could move away from a rules-based framework towards one in which powerful states increasingly act according to their own interpretations of national interest.

Samir Puri identifies another consequence. He argues that shifting and unpredictable U.S. tariff policies can encourage even American partners to reconsider their dependence on Washington. If access to the American market or other forms of cooperation can suddenly become instruments of pressure, countries may seek greater diversification and alternative partnerships. In this sense, policies intended to strengthen American bargaining power may also encourage other states to develop greater strategic independence.

The argument extends beyond economics. Thomas Carothers contends that the Trump administration has substantially reduced the United States' traditional role in supporting democracy internationally. He points to reduced democracy assistance, weaker support for democratic institutions and a more transactional conception of international partnerships. According to this interpretation, the change is significant because American influence previously operated not only through economic and military power but also through institutions, alliances and the promotion of particular political values.

The resulting situation presents an important paradox. A powerful state may seek greater freedom of action in the name of national sovereignty, yet its unilateral exercise of that freedom can reduce confidence in the international system from which its own influence has benefited. Other states may respond not by accepting greater dependence, but by seeking alternative markets, partnerships and institutions.

The broader issue is therefore not simply whether Trump's policies are beneficial or harmful to the United States. It is whether the international system can remain stable when its most powerful states increasingly prioritise immediate national interests over common rules and institutions. If other countries respond by developing greater autonomy, the result could be a more fragmented international order in which cooperation becomes more transactional and less predictable. The central tension is between sovereignty and interdependence: states want freedom to pursue their interests, but international stability often depends upon accepting constraints that apply beyond any single state's immediate preferences.

Question: What is the central argument of the passage?

  1. National sovereignty is incompatible with international trade.
  2. An increasingly transactional US policy may weaken the rules and institutions underlying the international order.
  3. The United States is withdrawing completely from international affairs.
  4. Tariffs are the only major threat to international stability.
Show answer

Answer: B. An increasingly transactional US policy may weaken the rules and institutions underlying the international order.

Foreign policy is often presented as a choice between friendship with one power and opposition to another. For India, however, the experience of the Russia–Ukraine conflict illustrates a more complicated approach. Since the beginning of the conflict in 2022, India has faced pressure from Western governments to take a stronger position against Russia, while simultaneously having longstanding strategic, defence, energy and economic relationships with Moscow. New Delhi's response can be understood through the idea of strategic autonomy: the capacity of a state to make decisions concerning its vital interests without allowing another power to determine those decisions.

Shyam Saran, in How India Sees the World, explains strategic autonomy not as the rejection of all partnerships, but as the ability to retain meaningful freedom of choice. A strategically autonomous state may cooperate closely with another country when its interests require it, while resisting pressure when its vital interests point in another direction. Autonomy, therefore, does not mean refusing every compromise; it means ensuring that compromises do not eliminate the ability to make independent decisions.

This approach can also be understood through the realist tradition in international relations. Hans J. Morgenthau, in Politics Among Nations, placed national interest at the centre of foreign policy. Kenneth Waltz, in Theory of International Politics, similarly argued that the absence of a central authority in international politics creates strong incentives for states to protect their own security and survival. E. H. Carr, in The Twenty Years' Crisis, criticised attempts to understand international politics entirely through abstract principles while ignoring power, interests and the actual circumstances confronting states.

India's response to the Russia–Ukraine conflict illustrates these ideas. India did not endorse Russia's invasion, and it repeatedly called for dialogue, diplomacy and an end to hostilities. At the same time, it did not join the Western sanctions regime in the manner demanded by some Western governments. India also continued its relationship with Russia, including substantial energy purchases. India's official position repeatedly emphasised the effects of the conflict on food, fuel and fertiliser security, particularly for developing countries.

The energy question became especially important. Following the disruption of global energy markets after 2022, Russian crude became an important source of supplies for Indian refiners. Bilateral trade expanded sharply, with Russian hydrocarbons contributing significantly to the increase. This illustrates an important distinction between moral preference and national policy. A government may disapprove of another state's actions while nevertheless maintaining economic relations with it if those relations are considered important to domestic security and economic stability.

The continuation of India–Russia relations also reflects a longer strategic relationship. Defence cooperation, nuclear energy, space cooperation and diplomatic coordination have developed over decades. The Indian government describes Russia as a longstanding strategic partner and continues to maintain cooperation across several sectors.

The Indian case therefore demonstrates that strategic autonomy does not necessarily require neutrality in every dispute. It involves preserving the ability to make issue-specific choices. India can cooperate with the United States and other Western powers in areas where their interests converge while maintaining relations with Russia where New Delhi considers those relations important. The underlying principle is that partnerships are instruments of national policy, rather than substitutes for it.

From this perspective, India's Russia policy under Prime Minister Narendra Modi can be understood less as choosing Russia over the West and more as an attempt to prevent any external power from determining India's choices. Strategic autonomy seeks room for manoeuvre: the capacity to cooperate, disagree, compromise or resist according to the particular interests at stake. The central question is therefore not “Which bloc does India belong to?”, but “Who makes the final decision when India's vital interests are involved?”
