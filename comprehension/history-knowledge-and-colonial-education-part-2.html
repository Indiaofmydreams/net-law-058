Passage: Colonial Education and the Making of Modern India

Education is often described as a gift of modernity, but the history of colonial India shows that education can also become an instrument of political power. The British did not introduce education into an empty intellectual landscape. India possessed diverse systems of indigenous schooling, learning and knowledge. Dharampal, in The Beautiful Tree: Indigenous Indian Education in the Eighteenth Century, challenged the assumption that India had been educationally backward before British intervention. Using colonial administrative records, he reconstructed evidence of indigenous schools and educational practices. His work therefore invites us to ask not only what the British introduced, but also what forms of learning already existed and what happened to them under colonial rule.

The purpose of colonial education was not uniform or unchanging. It developed alongside the changing requirements of British rule. Thomas Babington Macaulay's Minute on Indian Education of 1835 is particularly important in this context. Macaulay argued for the promotion of English education and envisaged the creation of a class that could serve as an intermediary between the colonial government and the wider Indian population. Education could therefore serve an administrative purpose: it could produce people familiar with the language, ideas and institutional practices of the rulers.

Gauri Viswanathan, in Masks of Conquest: Literary Study and British Rule in India, develops this argument further. She examines the growth of English literary education in colonial India and argues that literature could acquire a political function within the colonial system. English literature appeared to offer moral improvement and intellectual refinement, yet the educational system in which it was taught could also encourage cultural assimilation and facilitate political control. The significance of colonial education therefore lay not merely in what students were taught, but also in the cultural framework through which they were encouraged to understand knowledge and civilisation.

Frantz Fanon provides a broader psychological perspective in Black Skin, White Masks. He examined how colonial subjects could internalise the cultural hierarchy established by colonial society. Mastery of the coloniser's language and familiarity with the dominant culture could become associated with education, status and social advancement. Education could consequently create not only knowledge but also new ideas about what constituted refinement, success and civilisation.

Yet colonial education produced consequences that could not be completely controlled by the colonial state. The same English education that was intended partly to strengthen administration also exposed Indians to ideas concerning liberty, equality, rights, representation and self-government. Educated Indians could use the intellectual tools provided by colonial institutions to question the legitimacy of colonial rule itself. Thus, education could become an instrument of both domination and resistance.

The history of colonial education in India consequently presents a paradox. A system designed partly to make colonial administration more effective could also create individuals capable of criticising that administration. The attempt to shape the colonial subject intellectually could therefore produce an unintended consequence: the educated subject might begin to question the authority of the very system that educated him. Colonial education did not simply modernise India, nor did it operate only as an instrument of oppression; it became part of a complicated process through which colonial power, cultural change and Indian nationalism interacted.

Question: According to Gauri Viswanathan, why could English literary education have a political significance?

  1. British authorities prohibited Indians from reading English literature independently.
  2. English literature directly instructed Indians to support British political rule.
  3. Literature was used exclusively to train Indian soldiers.
  4. Education that appeared to provide moral and intellectual improvement could also facilitate cultural assimilation and political control.
Show answer

Answer: D. Education that appeared to provide moral and intellectual improvement could also facilitate cultural assimilation and political control.

Passage: From Colonial Empire to Informal Empire

The history of the United States is often narrated as a story of liberation from European empire. The American Revolution, in this account, represents the triumph of a people who rejected colonial rule and established an independent republic. Yet historians of empire have increasingly questioned whether independence necessarily marked a complete departure from the structures and ambitions associated with imperial power. The history of the United States can also be examined as a transition from being part of a European imperial world to becoming an important imperial power in its own right.

A. G. Hopkins, in American Empire: A Global History, challenges the idea of American exceptionalism by placing the United States within the longer history of Western imperialism. He argues that American development cannot be separated neatly from European imperial expansion. The new republic remained connected to Britain and Europe through economic, political and cultural relationships even after independence. In the nineteenth century, the United States expanded across the North American continent and later acquired overseas territories. Hopkins therefore presents American history as part of a larger transformation in which nation-building and imperial expansion could occur together.

Stephen Tuffnell's work on Anglo-American inter-imperialism adds another dimension to this argument. In his study of the period from 1865 to 1914, Tuffnell shows that American economic expansion was not developing in complete isolation from the British Empire. American businessmen, migrants, capital and knowledge became connected with networks associated with British imperialism. American expansion could therefore interact with, rather than simply oppose, an existing imperial system.

The meaning of empire, however, changed over time. During the age of European colonialism, imperial power was often visible: territories were conquered, governed and administered directly. The United States itself acquired an overseas territorial empire, particularly in the Caribbean and Pacific. But after the Second World War, the international environment changed. European colonial empires weakened and formal decolonisation accelerated. The decline of territorial empire did not necessarily mean the disappearance of unequal international relationships. The United States increasingly exercised influence through alliances, military bases, financial institutions, trade, political intervention and strategic partnerships. Some scholars therefore describe this as a movement from formal empire towards informal or neo-colonial forms of influence.

The distinction is important. A country need not formally govern another territory in order to possess considerable influence over its political or economic choices. Economic dependence, military relationships and political interventions can create forms of influence that operate without direct colonial administration. Research on the Cold War, for example, has examined how U.S. political interventions could affect trade and economic relationships in countries where such interventions occurred.

The argument, therefore, is not that the United States simply reproduced the British Empire in an identical form. Rather, it suggests a historical continuity alongside institutional change. The methods of power changed-from territorial administration towards wider networks of economic, military and political influence-but the capacity of a powerful state to shape the choices available to weaker states remained significant. The debate over American empire is consequently also a debate about what empire means when control no longer requires a governor, a colony or a formally occupied territory.

Question: What is the principal challenge made by the passage to the conventional narrative of American history?

  1. The United States remained economically dependent upon Britain throughout its history.
  2. European powers continued to govern the United States after the American Revolution.
  3. The American Revolution did not result in political independence.
  4. American independence did not necessarily mark a complete departure from the history and structures of imperial power.
Show answer

Answer: D. American independence did not necessarily mark a complete departure from the history and structures of imperial power.

Passage: Digital Colonialism and the New Architecture of Power

Colonialism is traditionally associated with the occupation of territory, control of populations and extraction of material resources. In the digital age, however, power can operate without direct territorial control. The growing importance of digital infrastructure, platforms, software and data has created new forms of dependence. Some scholars therefore use the concept of digital colonialism or data colonialism to describe situations in which powerful corporations and states acquire influence over societies through control of technological systems and information.

Michael Kwet, in Digital Colonialism: US Empire and the New Imperialism in the Global South, argues that digital technologies can reproduce relationships resembling older forms of colonial domination. Major technology corporations may control platforms, software, infrastructure and data used by people across the world. A country may therefore possess political independence and territorial sovereignty while remaining dependent upon technological systems developed and controlled elsewhere. The important question is consequently not only who controls territory, but also who controls the systems through which contemporary social and economic life takes place.

Shoshana Zuboff develops a related argument in The Age of Surveillance Capitalism. She describes a system in which human experience and behavioural information become valuable economic resources. Digital platforms can collect information about what people search for, watch, purchase and communicate. Such information can then be analysed to predict patterns of behaviour and, potentially, to influence future behaviour. The issue therefore extends beyond privacy.
