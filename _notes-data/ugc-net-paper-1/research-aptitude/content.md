# UGC NET Paper 1 – Research Aptitude: Detailed Study Notes

Concept notes, memory aids and exam-trap analysis for all 29 topics and 157 sub-topics of your Research Aptitude MCQ bank.

## How to use these notes

Every sub-topic is written in the same three-part pattern so that you can revise quickly. **Concept** is a connected explanation that you should read once slowly; it is what lets you answer reasoning and “which statement is correct” questions. **Memorise** is a bullet list of names, dates, terms and one-line facts that UGC NET loves to test as direct recall. **Analyse / traps** points out the distinctions, look-alike terms and wrong-option patterns that cause most errors in analysis-type questions. The notes follow the unit order of your question bank: Topic 1 Research (meaning, types, positivism), Topic 2 Methods, Topic 3 Steps of research, Topic 4 Thesis and article writing, Topic 5 ICT in research, and Topic 6 Research ethics.

**Revision plan.** Read Concept paragraphs once per topic, then revisit only the Memorise bullets and Traps in each later round. In the last week, read only the Traps lines and the comparison tables.

## TOPIC 1A – Meaning and Characteristics of Research

### 1A.1 Meaning, Definitions and Purpose of Research

**Concept.** Research is a systematic, objective and organised search for new knowledge or for a better understanding of existing knowledge. The word comes from the old French *recercher*, “to search again”, which captures the idea that research does not end at one answer; it re-examines, verifies and extends. A study counts as research only when it begins with a clearly defined problem, uses a planned procedure, collects evidence that others can inspect, and draws conclusions that are supported by that evidence. Simply collecting or compiling information, writing a summary of what is already known, or giving a personal opinion is not research, because none of these involves a controlled search for something not yet established. The purposes of research are usually listed as exploration (finding out about a little-known area), description (portraying accurately), explanation (finding why), prediction (forecasting what will happen) and control (using knowledge to influence events).

**Memorise.**

- Research = systematic + objective + empirical + critical investigation; the goal is discovery, verification, relationship-finding, theory-building or problem-solving.

- Redman and Mory: research is a systematised effort to gain new knowledge. Clifford Woody: research involves defining problems, formulating hypotheses, collecting and evaluating data, and testing conclusions. Kerlinger: systematic, controlled, empirical and critical investigation of hypothetical propositions about presumed relations among natural phenomena.

- Kothari’s four objectives: familiarity with a phenomenon (exploratory), accurate portrayal (descriptive), frequency or association (diagnostic), and testing a causal hypothesis (hypothesis-testing).

- Research **methods** are the techniques (sampling, interview, t-test). Research **methodology** is the logic and philosophy behind choosing and justifying those techniques.

**Analyse / traps.** Options that say research “proves” something are almost always wrong; research supports, confirms or rejects. A statement that equates research with mere data collection, or that says it needs no hypothesis in any form, is a trap. Remember that methodology is broader than methods: every question that asks “the study of methods and the reasoning behind them” points to methodology.

### 1A.2 Characteristics of Research

**Concept.** Good research is recognisable by a cluster of features that make it trustworthy. It is *systematic* because it follows ordered steps, *logical* because conclusions follow from evidence by valid reasoning, *empirical* because it rests on observation and data rather than speculation, and *objective* because the personal views of the researcher are kept out of the procedure as far as possible. It is *replicable*, meaning another researcher using the same method on similar subjects should get comparable results, and *cumulative*, since each study builds on earlier ones and adds to a body of knowledge. It is *problem-oriented*, *controlled* (variables are identified and extraneous influences are minimised), *critical* (assumptions are questioned) and *ethical*. Research also aims at generalisation, although action research and many qualitative studies deliberately give that up in return for depth and local relevance.

**Memorise.**

- Core features: systematic, logical, empirical, objective, replicable, verifiable, cumulative, precise, problem-focused, ethical.

- Replicability and objectivity are what separate scientific research from opinion.

- Research is cyclical: findings raise new questions, which start the next cycle.

- Reliable tools and valid measures are part of the character of good research.

**Analyse / traps.** “Research is always completely value-free” is too strong; the realistic position is that objectivity is a goal, and qualitative research openly admits researcher subjectivity. Be careful with an option that says research always aims at universal laws; applied and action research aim at solving specific problems.

### 1A.3 Scientific Method, Attitude and Reasoning

**Concept.** The scientific method is the disciplined way of acquiring knowledge: observe a phenomenon, define a problem, form a hypothesis, test it by collecting evidence under controlled conditions, analyse the results, and then accept, modify or reject the hypothesis, which may feed into a theory. Behind the method lies a *scientific attitude*, which means curiosity, open-mindedness, healthy scepticism, honesty in reporting, objectivity, and readiness to revise beliefs when evidence demands it. Reasoning takes two classical forms. *Deductive reasoning* goes from general premises to a particular conclusion (all humans are mortal; Socrates is human; therefore Socrates is mortal), and the conclusion is certain if the premises are true. *Inductive reasoning* goes from particular observations to a general statement, and the conclusion is only probable. Modern research usually combines both in the hypothetico-deductive cycle: induction suggests a hypothesis and deduction derives testable consequences.

**Memorise.**

- Deduction: general to particular, associated with Aristotle and the syllogism. Induction: particular to general, associated with Francis Bacon.

- Parsimony (Occam’s razor): among competing explanations, prefer the simplest that fits the facts.

- Serendipity: a valuable discovery made by accident but recognised through a prepared mind (the term is from Horace Walpole; penicillin is the usual example).

- Abduction (C. S. Peirce): inference to the best explanation.

**Analyse / traps.** Do not mix up the directions: if the statement moves from many observed cases to a rule, it is induction. A deductive argument can be valid yet false if a premise is false. Scientific attitude includes scepticism, not dogmatic belief.

### 1A.4 Variables

**Concept.** A variable is any characteristic that can take different values across persons, objects or occasions, such as age, income or teaching method. The *independent variable* (IV) is the presumed cause, manipulated or selected by the researcher; the *dependent variable* (DV) is the presumed effect that is measured. An *extraneous variable* is any other factor that might affect the DV and must be controlled; when it actually varies together with the IV and muddies the conclusion it is called a *confounding variable*. An *intervening* (mediating) variable lies between IV and DV and explains how the effect happens, but it is a hypothetical construct that is not directly observed, such as motivation between teaching method and achievement. A *moderator* variable changes the strength or direction of the IV–DV relationship, for example gender changing how strongly study hours affect marks. An *attribute variable* (age, sex, caste) is a characteristic that the researcher can only select, never assign.

**Memorise.**

- IV = cause/predictor/treatment; DV = effect/outcome/criterion.

- Mediator explains **how or why**; moderator explains **for whom or when**.

- Continuous variables take any value in a range (height); discrete take whole counts (number of children).

- Control variables are held constant deliberately.

**Analyse / traps.** The classic confusion is moderator versus mediator versus confounder. Ask: is it in the causal chain (mediator), does it alter the strength of the relationship (moderator), or is it an uncontrolled outside factor spoiling the comparison (confounder)? Also, an attribute variable cannot be experimentally manipulated, which is why such studies are ex post facto.

### 1A.5 Hypothesis, Theory and Operational Definitions

**Concept.** A hypothesis is a tentative, testable statement about the relationship between variables, formed before data collection and expected to be confirmed or rejected by evidence. A good hypothesis is clear, specific, testable, consistent with existing knowledge, and expressed in simple terms. The *null hypothesis* (H0) states that there is no difference or no relationship; the *alternative hypothesis* (H1 or Ha) states that there is. A *theory* is a more developed body of interrelated concepts, definitions and propositions that explains and predicts a wide range of phenomena; it sits above a single hypothesis, and a well-supported theory is not a guess. A *concept* is an abstract idea, a *construct* is a concept deliberately built for research, and an *operational definition* states exactly how that construct will be measured, such as defining “intelligence” as the score on a named test. Operational definitions are the reason studies can be replicated.

**Memorise.**

- Hypothesis → tested; theory → explains; law → describes a regularity that holds without exception in its domain.

- Operational definition: concept turned into measurable procedure (operationalism is linked to P. W. Bridgman).

- Parsimony is a desirable quality of a theory; replicability needs clear operational definitions.

- Hypotheses are never “proved”; we reject or fail to reject H0.

**Analyse / traps.** Watch the wording “accept the null hypothesis”; the correct statistical language is “fail to reject”. A hypothesis that cannot be tested is not a scientific hypothesis. The conceptual definition is dictionary-like; only the operational one tells you how to measure.

### 1A.6 Reliability and Validity of Research Tools

**Concept.** Reliability means consistency: a tool is reliable if it gives similar results under similar conditions. Validity means that the tool measures what it claims to measure. Reliability is checked by *test–retest* (stability over time), *parallel or alternate forms* (equivalence), *split-half* (internal consistency, usually corrected with the Spearman–Brown formula), *Cronbach’s alpha* or Kuder–Richardson (KR-20) for internal consistency, and *inter-rater reliability* when judges score responses. Validity has several types: *content validity* (the items fairly cover the subject; face validity is a surface judgement of this), *criterion-related validity* (agreement with an external standard, either concurrent or predictive), and *construct validity* (the tool measures the theoretical trait, shown by convergent and discriminant evidence).

**Memorise.**

- Reliability ≠ validity; reliability is necessary but not sufficient for validity.

- A valid instrument is necessarily reliable; a reliable instrument may be invalid (a scale that is consistently 2 kg off).

- Alpha of about 0.70 or above is usually treated as acceptable.

- Predictive validity: tool today predicts future performance; concurrent: agrees with a present criterion.

**Analyse / traps.** If an option says a reliable test must be valid, it is wrong. The target analogy helps: tight shots away from the bullseye = reliable but not valid; shots on the bullseye = both.

### 1A.7 Research Process, Design and Data

**Concept.** The research process is a sequence: choosing a problem, reviewing literature, formulating objectives and hypotheses, preparing the research design, deciding the sample, collecting data, analysing and interpreting it, and finally writing the report. A *pilot study* is a small preliminary trial that tests the tools, procedure and feasibility before the main study. The *research design* is the overall plan or blueprint that specifies what data are needed, from whom, by what method and how they will be analysed. Kerlinger’s MAX-MIN-CON principle says a good design maximises systematic (experimental) variance, minimises error variance and controls extraneous variance. Data are *primary* when collected first-hand for the present purpose and *secondary* when collected earlier by someone else for another purpose.

**Memorise.**

- Order: problem → literature review → hypothesis → design → sampling → data collection → analysis → report.

- Pilot study: tests tools and feasibility; not meant to prove hypotheses.

- Primary data: questionnaire, interview, observation. Secondary data: census reports, journals, records.

- Design = blueprint; methodology = reasoning behind the methods.

**Analyse / traps.** Questions on “first step” almost always point to identifying and defining the problem. A pilot study is not the same as a preliminary hypothesis test.

## TOPIC 1B – Types of Research

### 1B.1 Fundamental (Pure or Basic) Research

**Concept.** Fundamental research aims to expand knowledge and develop or test theory for its own sake, without immediate concern for practical use. It asks why and how things are, and its findings are meant to be general. Typical examples are studies on the nature of learning, the structure of matter or how language develops. Its value is judged by its contribution to theory, and although it may look useless at first, it often becomes the base of later applied work.

**Memorise.**

- Also called pure or basic research; theory-oriented; high generalisability.

- Done mostly in universities and laboratories with a long time horizon.

- Example: testing a theory of motivation, formulating a law.

**Analyse / traps.** If the outcome is an immediate practical solution, it is applied or action research, not fundamental.

### 1B.2 Applied Research

**Concept.** Applied research uses existing knowledge to solve a practical problem of a particular group, organisation or society. It tests whether a theory or technique works in real conditions, so it is concerned with usefulness. Evaluating a new teaching method, testing a drug, and market research are standard examples. Its conclusions are valid for the context studied and may be generalised only with care.

**Memorise.**

- Aim: solve a problem; users: managers, planners, teachers, policy makers.

- Example: effect of a remedial programme on learning outcomes.

- Applied research draws on the theories produced by fundamental research.

**Analyse / traps.** Applied research is not “less scientific”; it follows the same rigour. The difference lies in aim and use.

### 1B.3 Action Research

**Concept.** Action research is carried out by practitioners (for example a teacher) in their own working situation to solve an immediate local problem and improve practice. The term was coined by Kurt Lewin in the mid-1940s, who described a spiral of planning, action and fact-finding about the result of the action. In education it was popularised by Stephen Corey, and Kemmis and McTaggart gave the widely used cycle of plan, act, observe and reflect. The researcher is a participant, the sample is usually the researcher’s own class or group, and findings are used at once to revise the next cycle. Generalisation to other settings is limited. *Participatory action research (PAR)* goes further and treats the community as co-researchers for empowerment and change.

**Memorise.**

- Coined: Kurt Lewin. Popularised in education: Stephen Corey.

- Cycle: plan → act → observe → reflect, repeated as a spiral.

- Features: practitioner-led, local, cyclical, collaborative, immediate application.

- First step: identify the problem or area of improvement.

**Analyse / traps.** Options claiming that action research gives universally generalisable results, or is conducted by outside experts only, are wrong.

### 1B.4 Fundamental, Applied and Action Research Compared

**Concept.** The three types sit on a spectrum from theory to practice. Fundamental research targets knowledge and theory, applied research targets the solution of a problem for a wider group, and action research targets improvement within one specific setting and is done by those who work there. Understanding the contrasts lets you answer match-type and “which is correct” questions without memorising each example.

| Feature        | Fundamental          | Applied                          | Action                     |
|----------------|----------------------|----------------------------------|----------------------------|
| Main aim       | Knowledge, theory    | Solve a practical problem        | Improve local practice     |
| Who researches | Scholar or scientist | Researcher for client or society | Practitioner               |
| Setting        | Often laboratory     | Real-world                       | Own workplace or classroom |
| Generalisation | High                 | Moderate                         | Low                        |
| Time to use    | Long term            | Medium                           | Immediate                  |

**Analyse / traps.** The most immediate local application belongs to action research; the greatest generalisation belongs to fundamental research.

### 1B.5 Exploratory, Descriptive and Explanatory Research

**Concept.** These types are classified by purpose. *Exploratory research* is used when little is known; it uses a flexible design, literature survey, expert interviews or case studies to gain insight and to generate hypotheses. *Descriptive research* describes characteristics of a population or phenomenon as they are, answering what, who, where and how many; surveys, fact-finding enquiries and correlational studies fall here, and no variable is manipulated. *Explanatory research* (causal or analytical) asks why, tests hypotheses about cause and effect, and often uses experiments or advanced statistics.

**Memorise.**

- Exploratory: formulates hypotheses; flexible; qualitative leaning.

- Descriptive: what is; surveys, correlational studies; no manipulation.

- Explanatory: why; tests causal hypotheses.

- Correlational research shows association, not cause.

**Analyse / traps.** Correlation does not imply causation. A survey is descriptive even if it uses statistics.

### 1B.6 Longitudinal and Cross-sectional Studies

**Concept.** A *cross-sectional study* collects data from different groups at one point in time, for example children of ages 6, 9 and 12 measured this month; it is quick and inexpensive but cannot follow individual change. A *longitudinal study* collects data repeatedly over a long period. Within it, a *trend study* samples different people from the same population at different times, a *cohort study* follows a group sharing a common experience such as birth year, and a *panel study* follows exactly the same individuals at each wave. Longitudinal designs show true change but suffer from attrition and high cost.

**Memorise.**

- Cross-sectional: one time, many groups. Longitudinal: many times, same or similar units.

- Panel = same individuals every time; trend = different samples; cohort = shared event.

- Weakness: attrition (dropouts) in panel studies.

**Analyse / traps.** Different samples at different times is a trend study, not a panel study.

### 1B.7 Qualitative, Quantitative and Mixed-Methods Research

**Concept.** Quantitative research measures variables numerically, usually follows a deductive logic, uses large samples and statistics, and aims at generalisation. Qualitative research explores meanings, experiences and context in words, uses inductive logic, small purposive samples and interpretive analysis. *Mixed-methods research* integrates both, either by running them together (convergent), by following quantitative with qualitative to explain results (explanatory sequential), or by exploring qualitatively first and then testing quantitatively (exploratory sequential), a framework associated with John Creswell. Among qualitative approaches, *grounded theory* (Glaser and Strauss, 1967) builds theory from data, *ethnography* studies a culture through long field immersion, and *phenomenology* (rooted in Husserl) studies lived experience.

**Memorise.**

- Quantitative: numbers, deductive, objective, generalisable. Qualitative: words, inductive, contextual, rich description.

- Grounded theory: Glaser and Strauss, 1967. Ethnography: culture. Phenomenology: lived experience.

- Triangulation is combining methods or data sources to cross-check findings.

**Analyse / traps.** Do not label grounded theory as quantitative. Mixed methods is not simply using two questionnaires; it needs integration of both strands.

### 1B.8 Conceptual and Empirical Research

**Concept.** *Conceptual research* is based on abstract ideas and reasoning, with no direct data collection; philosophers and theorists develop or reinterpret concepts, and thought experiments such as Einstein’s belong here. *Empirical research* is data-based, resting on observation or experiment and capable of verification by experience; it is sometimes called experimental type research. In law, the matching pair is *doctrinal* (study of statutes and cases) and *non-doctrinal or empirical* research.

**Memorise.**

- Conceptual = theory and reasoning; empirical = observation and data.

- Empirical research tests hypotheses against evidence.

**Analyse / traps.** A literature-only philosophical analysis is conceptual even if it is long and systematic.

### 1B.9 Case Study, Experimental, Historical and Ex Post Facto Research

**Concept.** A *case study* is an intensive investigation of a single unit such as a person, school or community, giving depth rather than breadth. *Experimental research* manipulates the independent variable, uses a control group and random assignment, and so can establish cause and effect. *Historical research* studies past events using primary and secondary sources and criticism of those sources. *Ex post facto* (causal-comparative) research studies a presumed cause after it has already occurred; the researcher cannot manipulate the independent variable, so conclusions about cause are weaker than in experiments.

**Memorise.**

- Case study: depth, single unit; Yin is the best-known method writer.

- Experiment: manipulation + control + randomisation.

- Ex post facto: “after the fact”; no manipulation; Kerlinger’s definition stresses lack of direct control.

**Analyse / traps.** If the researcher cannot assign participants to groups (gender, caste, past illness), the design is ex post facto, not experimental.

### 1B.10 Evaluation and Interdisciplinary Research

**Concept.** *Evaluation research* judges the worth or effectiveness of a programme, policy or product. *Formative evaluation* is done during the programme to improve it, and *summative evaluation* at the end to judge outcomes (the distinction is credited to Michael Scriven). Other forms include process and impact evaluation. *Interdisciplinary research* integrates concepts and methods from two or more disciplines to address a problem that no single discipline can handle; it differs from multidisciplinary work, where disciplines work side by side without real integration, and transdisciplinary work, which also involves non-academic stakeholders.

**Memorise.**

- Formative = during, to improve. Summative = at the end, to judge.

- Multi = side by side; inter = integrated; trans = crosses boundaries including society.

**Analyse / traps.** Do not reverse formative and summative.

### 1B.11 Content Analysis, Census, Field, Laboratory Research and Meta-analysis

**Concept.** *Content analysis* systematically codes and quantifies the content of communication such as texts, speeches or media (Berelson is the classic source). A *census* is complete enumeration of every unit of the population, whereas a sample survey studies a part. *Field research* is done in the natural setting and has high realism but less control; *laboratory research* is done in an artificial controlled setting and has high control but lower realism. *Meta-analysis* statistically combines the results of many independent studies to estimate an overall effect (the term was coined by Gene Glass in 1976). The *Delphi technique* collects expert opinion through several anonymous rounds with feedback until consensus emerges.

**Memorise.**

- Meta-analysis: Gene Glass, 1976. Delphi: expert panel, anonymity, iterative rounds.

- Census = every unit; no sampling error but costly.

**Analyse / traps.** A systematic review is not necessarily a meta-analysis; only the latter pools effect sizes statistically.

## TOPIC 1C – Positivism and Post-positivism

### 1C.1 Positivism: Origins and Comte

**Concept.** Positivism is the view that genuine knowledge comes from sensory experience and scientific method, and that social phenomena can be studied like natural ones. It was named and systematised by Auguste Comte (1798–1857) in his *Course of Positive Philosophy*; he also coined the word “sociology”. Comte’s *law of three stages* says that human thought passes from the theological stage (explanations by gods and spirits), through the metaphysical stage (abstract forces and essences), to the positive or scientific stage (observation and laws). Earlier roots lie in the empiricism of Bacon and Hume and in Saint-Simon’s ideas; Durkheim later applied positivist method to sociology.

**Memorise.**

- Founder: Auguste Comte; coined “positivism” and “sociology”.

- Three stages: theological → metaphysical → positive.

- Sociology as the “queen” of sciences in his hierarchy.

**Analyse / traps.** The stage order is a favourite match item; keep it in historical sequence, theological first.

### 1C.2 Positivism: Assumptions and Methodology

**Concept.** Positivism assumes a single, objective, external reality (realist ontology), that the researcher can stand apart from what is studied (objectivist epistemology), and that research should be free of values (value-free axiology). Methodologically it favours experiments and surveys, quantitative measurement, hypothesis testing through the hypothetico-deductive model, and the search for law-like generalisations. Observation is supposed to be neutral, causes are sought through control, and knowledge grows by verification.

**Memorise.**

- Ontology: one objective reality. Epistemology: objective, detached researcher. Axiology: value-free.

- Methods: experiment, survey, statistics; deductive; generalisation.

- Related ideas: empiricism, determinism, reductionism, parsimony.

**Analyse / traps.** Statements such as “reality is socially constructed” or “multiple realities” are not positivist; they belong to constructivism.

### 1C.3 Logical Positivism and the Verification Principle

**Concept.** Logical positivism emerged in the 1920s–30s in the Vienna Circle (Moritz Schlick, Rudolf Carnap, Otto Neurath) and was popularised in English by A. J. Ayer in *Language, Truth and Logic* (1936). Its central claim, the *verification principle*, holds that a statement is meaningful only if it is either analytically true (true by definition, as in logic and mathematics) or empirically verifiable. Metaphysical, ethical and theological claims were therefore dismissed as meaningless. The doctrine was criticised because the verification principle itself is neither analytic nor empirically verifiable.

**Memorise.**

- Vienna Circle: Schlick, Carnap, Neurath; Ayer in Britain.

- Verification principle: meaning = verifiability.

- Criticised by Popper (falsification) and Quine.

**Analyse / traps.** Verification (logical positivism) and falsification (Popper) are opposites in emphasis; do not interchange them.

### 1C.4 Popper and Falsification

**Concept.** Karl Popper rejected verification and induction. In *The Logic of Scientific Discovery* (German 1934, English 1959) he argued that what separates science from non-science is *falsifiability*: a scientific theory must make claims that could, in principle, be refuted by observation. No amount of confirming instances proves a universal law, but a single contrary instance (one black swan) can refute it. Science advances by bold conjectures and severe attempts at refutation, a position called *critical rationalism*. Popper cited Marxist history and Freudian psychoanalysis as theories so flexible that nothing could count against them, contrasting them with Einstein’s risky predictions. His ideas are the starting point of post-positivism.

**Memorise.**

- Key concept: falsifiability as the demarcation criterion.

- Books: *Logic of Scientific Discovery*; *Conjectures and Refutations*; *The Open Society and Its Enemies*.

- Theories are corroborated, never finally proved.

**Analyse / traps.** If an option says science proves theories true by repeated confirmation, it contradicts Popper.

### 1C.5 Kuhn, Lakatos, Feyerabend and Paradigm Shift

**Concept.** Thomas Kuhn’s *The Structure of Scientific Revolutions* (1962) argued that science does not grow only by steady accumulation. Mature science works in *normal science* within a shared paradigm; accumulating anomalies create a *crisis*, followed by a *scientific revolution* and a *paradigm shift* to a new framework, such as the move from Newtonian to Einsteinian physics. Rival paradigms are partly *incommensurable*. Imre Lakatos offered a middle path with *research programmes* made of a hard core protected by a belt of auxiliary hypotheses, judged as progressive or degenerating. Paul Feyerabend, in *Against Method*, defended epistemological anarchism with the slogan “anything goes”.

**Memorise.**

- Kuhn: paradigm, normal science, anomaly, crisis, revolution.

- Lakatos: research programmes. Feyerabend: epistemological anarchism. Popper: falsification.

- Sequence: pre-science → normal science → anomalies → crisis → revolution → new normal science.

**Analyse / traps.** Keep the pairing of thinker and concept firm; match questions test exactly this.

### 1C.6 Post-positivism

**Concept.** Post-positivism keeps the aim of studying reality scientifically but admits that humans can know it only imperfectly. It is often described as *critical realism*: a real world exists, yet our observations are theory-laden, fallible and shaped by the observer’s background. Objectivity is therefore a regulative ideal rather than an achievable state. Researchers use multiple methods and triangulation to approximate truth, accept probabilistic rather than certain conclusions, and hold that hypotheses are not proved but only not yet rejected. Quantitative methods remain common, though qualitative ones are accepted. Influential discussions come from Popper, Kuhn, Guba and Lincoln, Phillips and Burbules, and Roy Bhaskar’s critical realism.

**Memorise.**

- Ontology: critical realism (reality exists but is imperfectly apprehended).

- Epistemology: modified objectivist; knowledge is fallible.

- Triangulation and falsification are central.

- Observation is theory-laden.

**Analyse / traps.** Post-positivism is a modification of positivism, not a rejection of science; it should not be confused with constructivism.

### 1C.7 Ontology, Epistemology and Axiology

**Concept.** These three terms define a research paradigm. *Ontology* asks what exists and what reality is. *Epistemology* asks how we can know it and what relationship the knower has with the known. *Axiology* asks about the role of values and ethics in research. The combination determines the methodology, meaning the practical way of studying the problem.

| Paradigm                        | Ontology                                | Epistemology               | Axiology                  | Typical methods         |
|---------------------------------|-----------------------------------------|----------------------------|---------------------------|-------------------------|
| Positivism                      | One objective reality                   | Objective, detached        | Value-free                | Experiments, surveys    |
| Post-positivism                 | Critical realism                        | Modified objectivity       | Mostly value-aware        | Mixed, triangulation    |
| Interpretivism / constructivism | Multiple socially constructed realities | Subjective, co-constructed | Value-bound               | Interviews, ethnography |
| Critical theory                 | Reality shaped by power                 | Transactional              | Value-laden, emancipatory | Participatory, dialogic |
| Pragmatism                      | Reality is what works                   | Practical                  | Both                      | Mixed methods           |

**Analyse / traps.** Map each statement to its question word: “what is real” is ontology, “how do we know” is epistemology, “what role do values play” is axiology.

### 1C.8 Interpretivism, Constructivism and Critical Paradigms

**Concept.** Interpretivism holds that social life must be understood from the meanings people give to it; Max Weber’s *Verstehen* (interpretive understanding) and the hermeneutic tradition of Dilthey and Gadamer are key sources. Constructivism adds that reality is socially constructed (Berger and Luckmann), so there are multiple realities and the researcher is part of the inquiry. Phenomenology (Husserl, Schutz) studies lived experience. These paradigms prefer qualitative methods and rich description. *Critical theory*, from the Frankfurt School (Adorno, Horkheimer, Habermas), goes further by examining power and inequality and aiming at emancipation. *Hermeneutics* is the theory and method of interpretation, originally of texts.

**Memorise.**

- Weber: Verstehen. Dilthey and Gadamer: hermeneutics. Husserl: phenomenology.

- Critical theory: Frankfurt School; emancipation.

- Constructivism: multiple, socially constructed realities.

**Analyse / traps.** A statement about the goal of explanation and prediction through laws is positivist; a statement about understanding meaning is interpretivist.

### 1C.9 Research Paradigms and Triangulation

**Concept.** A paradigm, in the sense used by Kuhn and later by Guba and Lincoln, is a worldview that shapes what questions are asked and how they are answered. *Pragmatism*, drawn from Peirce, James and Dewey, rejects the fight between paradigms and chooses whatever method best answers the question; it is often called the philosophy of mixed methods. *Triangulation* means using more than one source, method, investigator or theory to cross-check findings. Norman Denzin listed four types: data, investigator, theory and methodological triangulation.

**Memorise.**

- Pragmatism: what works; philosophical base of mixed methods.

- Denzin’s triangulation types: data, investigator, theory, methodological.

- Triangulation improves credibility and validity.

**Analyse / traps.** Triangulation is about convergence of evidence, not about increasing sample size.

## TOPIC 2A – Experimental Method

### 2A.1 Basic Concepts of Experimental Research

**Concept.** The experimental method is the research strategy in which the investigator deliberately manipulates one or more independent variables, controls other influences, and observes the effect on a dependent variable. Because the researcher decides who receives the treatment, it is the strongest method for establishing cause and effect. A true experiment has three hallmarks: manipulation of the independent variable, control (usually through a control group and constancy of conditions) and randomisation. R. A. Fisher laid the statistical foundations in the 1920s–30s with the principles of randomisation, replication and local control (blocking), and developed analysis of variance. J. S. Mill’s canons (agreement, difference, concomitant variation and residues) are the older logical ancestors of experimental reasoning. Experiments may run in a laboratory (high control) or in the field (high realism).

**Memorise.**

- Three hallmarks: manipulation, control, randomisation.

- Fisher: randomisation, replication, local control; ANOVA and the F-ratio.

- Experimental group receives the treatment; control group does not, or receives the standard treatment.

- Laboratory experiment = more control; field experiment = more realism.

**Analyse / traps.** If the researcher cannot manipulate the independent variable, the study is not a true experiment, however carefully it is analysed.

### 2A.2 Treatment, Control, Randomisation and Matching

**Concept.** The control group gives the baseline against which the effect of the treatment is judged. For that comparison to be fair, the groups must be equivalent at the start. *Random assignment* gives every participant an equal chance of being placed in any group, so that known and unknown differences are spread out evenly on average; this is the main safeguard of internal validity. Do not confuse it with *random selection*, which is the way a sample is drawn from the population and supports external validity. When randomisation is impossible or the sample is small, *matching* pairs participants who are alike on a key variable such as IQ or age and then splits each pair between groups. Matching controls only the variables that were matched, which is its weakness. *Counterbalancing* varies the order of conditions across participants to control order effects in repeated-measures designs.

**Memorise.**

- Random assignment → internal validity. Random selection → external validity.

- Matching controls only measured variables; randomisation controls unmeasured ones too.

- Blocking: group units into homogeneous blocks and randomise within each block.

**Analyse / traps.** An option that says randomisation guarantees identical groups is wrong; it makes them equivalent only in probability.

### 2A.3 Placebo and Blinding

**Concept.** The *placebo effect* is an improvement caused by a participant’s expectation rather than by the actual treatment, so many medical and psychological experiments give the control group an inert substance or sham procedure. Blinding prevents expectations from distorting results. In a *single-blind* design the participants do not know which group they are in; in a *double-blind* design neither the participants nor the experimenters who deal with them know; in a *triple-blind* design the data analysts are also unaware. Double-blinding guards against *demand characteristics* (participants guessing what is wanted) and *experimenter bias* (the Rosenthal or Pygmalion effect, in which the researcher’s expectations unintentionally influence outcomes).

**Memorise.**

- Single blind: participants unaware. Double blind: participants and experimenter unaware.

- Placebo group controls for expectation, not for the treatment itself.

- Rosenthal effect: experimenter expectancy.

**Analyse / traps.** If an option says neither the participants nor the experimenter knows the assignment, that is double-blind, whatever else is added.

### 2A.4 Internal and External Validity

**Concept.** Following Campbell and Stanley (1963), *internal validity* is the extent to which the observed change in the dependent variable can really be attributed to the independent variable rather than to something else. *External validity* is the extent to which the results can be generalised to other persons (population validity), places (ecological validity) and times. Cook and Campbell (1979) added *construct validity* (are the concepts correctly operationalised) and *statistical conclusion validity* (are the statistical inferences sound). There is a trade-off: tight laboratory control raises internal validity but may lower external validity, whereas field studies do the reverse.

**Memorise.**

- Internal = causal certainty. External = generalisability.

- Campbell and Stanley (1963) classic list of threats; Cook and Campbell (1979) extension.

- A study that is not internally valid cannot be externally valid in any useful sense.

**Analyse / traps.** Do not treat reliability and internal validity as the same thing; reliability is consistency of measurement, internal validity is soundness of causal inference.

### 2A.5 Threats to Validity

**Concept.** Threats to internal validity are rival explanations for the observed effect. *History* is an outside event occurring between pretest and posttest. *Maturation* is natural change in participants with time (growing older, tired or wiser). *Testing* is the effect of taking a pretest on the later score. *Instrumentation* is a change in the measuring tool or the observers. *Statistical regression* is the tendency of extreme scorers to move toward the mean on retesting. *Selection* means groups differ from the start. *Mortality* (attrition) is the loss of participants, especially when dropouts differ between groups. Threats to external validity include pretest sensitisation, interaction of selection and treatment, reactive arrangements such as the *Hawthorne effect* (behaviour changes because people know they are being studied, from the Western Electric studies associated with Elton Mayo) and multiple-treatment interference.

**Memorise.**

- Internal threats: History, Maturation, Testing, Instrumentation, Regression, Selection, Mortality (memory cue: HMTIRSM).

- Hawthorne effect = awareness of being observed; John Henry effect = control group works harder to compete.

- Regression to the mean affects groups chosen for extreme scores.

**Analyse / traps.** Distinguish history (outside event) from maturation (internal change over time) and testing (effect of the pretest itself).

### 2A.6 Experimental Designs

**Concept.** *Pre-experimental* designs lack control, for instance the one-shot case study (X then O), the one-group pretest–posttest (O X O) and the static-group comparison; they cannot rule out many threats. *True experimental* designs use random assignment: the posttest-only control group design (R X O / R O), the pretest–posttest control group design (R O X O / R O O) and the *Solomon four-group design* (Solomon, 1949), which combines both to detect the effect of pretesting. *Quasi-experimental* designs, such as the non-equivalent control group design or the time-series design, compare groups without random assignment. *Factorial designs* study two or more independent variables at once and reveal *interaction effects*; a 2 × 3 design has two factors with 2 and 3 levels. A *randomised block design* controls one nuisance variable, and a *Latin square* controls two by arranging treatments so each appears once in every row and column. Within-subjects (repeated-measures) designs expose the same people to all conditions, while between-subjects designs use different people for each condition.

**Memorise.**

- Solomon four-group: four groups, controls pretest sensitisation.

- Factorial design: main effects + interaction; analysed by ANOVA.

- Latin square: controls two extraneous variables (rows and columns).

- Quasi-experiment: no random assignment.

**Analyse / traps.** A one-group pretest–posttest design looks experimental but is pre-experimental, because it has no control group.

## TOPIC 2B – Descriptive Method

### 2B.1 Descriptive and Survey Methods

**Concept.** Descriptive research describes things as they are: the present status of a population, the opinions people hold, the conditions that exist, the processes going on or the trends developing. It does not manipulate variables. The main forms are surveys, case studies, developmental or growth studies, correlational studies, comparative studies and observational studies. A *survey* collects information from a sample or the whole population, usually by questionnaire, interview or schedule; it may be cross-sectional (once) or longitudinal (repeatedly). In a *panel study* the same respondents are surveyed again and again, which makes change at the individual level visible. Surveys give breadth and are efficient for large populations, but they establish association rather than cause and are open to response bias.

**Memorise.**

- Descriptive = what is; no manipulation; includes survey, case study, correlational, developmental studies.

- Panel study: same respondents repeatedly; trend study: new samples each time.

- Opinion polls and census-type enquiries are surveys.

**Analyse / traps.** A statement that a survey can prove causation is wrong; it can only show association.

### 2B.2 Questionnaire, Interview and Observation

**Concept.** A *questionnaire* is a set of written questions answered by the respondent without an interviewer. It reaches a scattered sample cheaply and removes interviewer bias, but it needs literate respondents and a mailed questionnaire often suffers from a low return rate. A *schedule* contains similar questions but is filled in by an enumerator, so it can be used with illiterate respondents and gives higher response, at higher cost. Questions can be *closed* (fixed options) or *open-ended*; they should avoid leading, loaded and double-barrelled wording, and the instrument should be pilot-tested. An *interview* may be structured (fixed questions), semi-structured or unstructured, and uses probing and rapport. *Observation* may be participant (the observer joins the group, as in ethnographies) or non-participant, structured or unstructured, and is open to observer bias and the Hawthorne effect.

**Memorise.**

- Questionnaire: self-administered; schedule: filled by the investigator.

- Mailed questionnaire: wide coverage, low response, no interviewer bias.

- Leading question: suggests the expected answer, so it biases responses.

- Participant observation gives depth but risks losing objectivity.

**Analyse / traps.** If respondents are illiterate, the correct tool is a schedule or interview, not a mailed questionnaire.

### 2B.3 Attitude Scales and Sociometry

**Concept.** Attitude scales convert opinions into scores. The *Likert scale* (1932) is a summated rating scale in which respondents mark agreement on a five-point range from strongly agree to strongly disagree and the item scores are added. The *Thurstone scale* (1929) uses statements pre-rated by judges to be equal-appearing intervals, and respondents agree with those they accept. The *Guttman scale* (scalogram, 1944) uses cumulative, unidimensional items so that agreement with a stronger item implies agreement with the weaker ones. Osgood’s *semantic differential* (1957) uses bipolar adjective pairs (good–bad) on a seven-point range to measure the meaning of a concept along evaluation, potency and activity. The *Bogardus social distance scale* (1925) measures willingness to accept groups at different degrees of closeness. *Sociometry*, developed by J. L. Moreno (1934), studies social acceptance and rejection within a group and displays them in a sociogram.

**Memorise.**

- Likert: summated, five-point agreement. Thurstone: equal-appearing intervals, judges. Guttman: cumulative scalogram.

- Semantic differential: Osgood, bipolar adjectives. Bogardus: social distance.

- Sociometry: Moreno; sociogram; stars and isolates.

**Analyse / traps.** Likert is easy to build because no judges are needed, unlike Thurstone. Sociometry measures group relations, not individual attitudes toward objects.

### 2B.4 Sampling Techniques

**Concept.** A *population* is the entire group of interest and a *sample* is the part actually studied; the list from which the sample is drawn is the *sampling frame*. In *probability sampling* every unit has a known, non-zero chance of selection, which allows estimation of sampling error. The main kinds are simple random, systematic (every k-th unit after a random start, where k = N/n), stratified (random sampling within strata, proportionate or disproportionate), cluster (whole groups are randomly selected) and multistage. In *non-probability sampling* selection is not random, so sampling error cannot be calculated: convenience (accidental), purposive (judgemental), quota (fixed numbers from categories but chosen non-randomly) and snowball (participants recruit others). Fuller treatment appears in Topic 3E.

**Memorise.**

- Probability: simple random, systematic, stratified, cluster, multistage.

- Non-probability: convenience, purposive, quota, snowball.

- Systematic interval k = N/n; quota is non-random even though it resembles stratified sampling.

**Analyse / traps.** Quota sampling is a non-probability method, a favourite wrong-option trap in lists of probability techniques.

## TOPIC 2C – Historical Method

### 2C.1 Meaning and Process of Historical Research

**Concept.** Historical research is the systematic collection and objective evaluation of evidence about past events in order to test hypotheses, explain causes and trends, and so help us understand the present and anticipate the future. Unlike experiments, the past cannot be repeated or manipulated, so the researcher works only with surviving traces and must judge how far they can be trusted. The usual sequence is: choose a problem and form a hypothesis; collect sources (called *heuristics*); subject them to *criticism* (external, then internal); *synthesise and interpret* the verified facts; and finally present the findings in an organised narrative, often chronological. The writing of history and the study of how it has been written is *historiography*. R. G. Collingwood is associated with the idea that the historian must re-enact past thought in the mind.

**Memorise.**

- Steps: problem → heuristics (collection) → criticism → synthesis/interpretation → exposition.

- Heuristics = locating and collecting sources; chronology = arranging events in time order.

- Historical research is non-reactive: the researcher cannot influence the past.

**Analyse / traps.** A question on “collection of sources” points to heuristics, while “testing authenticity” points to criticism. Do not confuse historical method (a research method) with historiography (the study of historical writing).

### 2C.2 Primary and Secondary Sources

**Concept.** A *primary source* comes directly from the time and person under study: eyewitness accounts, diaries, letters, official records, archives, inscriptions, coins, photographs, speeches and artefacts. A *secondary source* is an account written by someone who did not witness the event, such as a textbook, encyclopaedia, biography or later history. Sources are also divided into *records* (intentionally preserved evidence, such as minutes and chronicles) and *relics or remains* (unintentional evidence, such as tools, buildings and skeletons). *Oral history* collects first-hand testimony through interviews and is treated as primary evidence for recent events. Whether a source is primary or secondary depends on how close the author was to the event and on the research question.

**Memorise.**

- Primary: original, contemporary, first-hand. Secondary: interpretation by later writers.

- Relics/remains give unintended evidence; records are intended to inform.

- Oral history: interviews with participants; subject to memory distortion.

**Analyse / traps.** A textbook on the Mughal period is secondary even if it quotes primary documents; a diary of a participant is primary.

### 2C.3 External and Internal Criticism

**Concept.** Historical criticism asks two questions in a fixed order. *External criticism* (lower criticism) checks authenticity: is the document genuine, who wrote it, when and where? It examines handwriting, paper, ink, language, style, signatures and seals, and exposes forgeries. *Internal criticism* (higher criticism) assumes the document is genuine and tests credibility: what does the text mean (positive internal criticism) and is the author reliable, competent, close to the events, honest and free from bias (negative internal criticism)? *Corroboration*, the confirmation of a fact by independent sources, strengthens credibility.

**Memorise.**

- External = genuineness. Internal = trustworthiness of content.

- Order: external first, then internal.

- Corroboration: agreement of independent witnesses.

**Analyse / traps.** A genuine document can still be false; that is why internal criticism follows external criticism.

### 2C.4 Historiography and Historians

**Concept.** Historiography traces how historians have understood and written about the past. Herodotus is called the Father of History for his *Histories*, while Thucydides is admired for his critical, evidence-based approach. Leopold von Ranke, the father of modern scientific history, insisted on archival documents and on showing the past as it actually happened. Ibn Khaldun’s *Muqaddimah* is a landmark of the philosophy of history, and Kalhana’s *Rajatarangini* (twelfth century) is a celebrated chronicle of Kashmir. Marc Bloch and Lucien Febvre founded the Annales school with its emphasis on long-term social and economic structures. E. H. Carr, in *What Is History?*, described history as a continuous dialogue between the historian and the facts, and between the present and the past; Arnold Toynbee wrote *A Study of History* on the rise and fall of civilisations by challenge and response; Karl Marx’s historical materialism interprets history through economic relations.

**Memorise.**

- Herodotus: Father of History. Ranke: scientific history. Carr: *What Is History?* Toynbee: challenge and response.

- Annales school: Bloch and Febvre. Kalhana: *Rajatarangini*. Ibn Khaldun: *Muqaddimah*.

- Indian schools: nationalist, Marxist (D. D. Kosambi), subaltern (Ranajit Guha).

**Analyse / traps.** Match each historian with the central idea; options usually swap Carr, Toynbee and Collingwood.

### 2C.5 Limitations and Errors in Historical Research

**Concept.** The historian cannot observe events, repeat them or control variables, and the surviving record is partial, often written by the powerful and sometimes forged or destroyed. Interpretation is therefore open to subjectivity. Typical errors include *presentism* or *anachronism* (judging the past by present values or concepts), selective use of evidence to support a favourite view, hindsight bias, the single-cause fallacy and the *post hoc* fallacy (assuming that because B followed A, A caused B). Generalisations from unique events are limited, which is why historical conclusions are expressed as probabilities.

**Memorise.**

- Presentism = reading present standards into the past.

- Main limits: incomplete sources, bias, no replication, no experimentation.

**Analyse / traps.** An option that says historical research can be repeated under controlled conditions is wrong by definition.

## TOPIC 2D – Qualitative Method

### 2D.1 Nature and Aims of Qualitative Research

**Concept.** Qualitative research aims at understanding meanings, experiences and processes in their natural setting rather than measuring and counting. The researcher is the main instrument, the design is flexible and emergent, reasoning is inductive, and data are words, images and observations gathered through interviews, observation and documents. It produces *thick description*, a term that Clifford Geertz borrowed from Gilbert Ryle for rich, contextual accounts of the meaning of behaviour. Samples are small and purposive, so results are not statistically generalisable; instead Lincoln and Guba speak of *transferability* to similar contexts.

**Memorise.**

- Features: natural setting, inductive, holistic, researcher as instrument, emergent design.

- Thick description: Geertz. Verstehen: Weber.

- Not typical of qualitative work: large random samples and statistical hypothesis testing.

**Analyse / traps.** “Qualitative” does not mean unsystematic; it has its own standards of rigour (see 2D.5).

### 2D.2 Sampling and Data Saturation

**Concept.** Qualitative sampling is purposive: participants are chosen because they can give rich information. Forms include criterion sampling, maximum-variation sampling, typical-case and extreme or deviant-case sampling, snowball sampling and *theoretical sampling* in grounded theory, where the next participants are chosen according to the emerging theory. There is no fixed sample size; sampling continues until *data saturation*, when additional interviews yield no new themes or properties. In grounded theory the equivalent is *theoretical saturation*. Key informants and gatekeepers help access.

**Memorise.**

- Purposive, snowball and theoretical sampling are typical.

- Saturation = no new information emerging; it decides when to stop.

**Analyse / traps.** Do not apply a sample-size formula to qualitative studies; adequacy is judged by richness and saturation.

### 2D.3 Interviews, Focus Groups and Field Notes

**Concept.** The in-depth interview, unstructured or semi-structured, explores a participant’s perspective through open questions and probing. A *focus group* brings together a small group, commonly six to ten people, under a moderator to discuss a topic; the group interaction produces data that individual interviews cannot, and the technique grew from Robert Merton’s focused interview. *Field notes* record what is observed, with descriptive notes (what happened) kept separate from reflective notes (the researcher’s thoughts); interviews are usually audio-recorded and transcribed. Rapport, probing and a reflexive journal improve quality.

**Memorise.**

- Focus group: 6–10 participants, moderator, interaction.

- Field notes: descriptive + reflective. Probing: follow-up questions for depth.

- Emic = insider’s view; etic = outsider’s view.

**Analyse / traps.** Focus-group data are group-level; individual opinions may be shaped by dominant members.

### 2D.4 Qualitative Data Analysis

**Concept.** Analysis begins during data collection and moves from raw text to themes. In *thematic analysis*, Braun and Clarke (2006) describe six phases: familiarisation with data, generating initial codes, searching for themes, reviewing themes, defining and naming themes, and writing up. In grounded theory, coding proceeds from *open coding* (breaking data into labelled parts) to *axial coding* (relating categories) to *selective coding* (integrating around a core category), using the *constant comparative method* and *memo writing*. Other forms are content, narrative and discourse analysis. Software such as NVivo, ATLAS.ti and MAXQDA (CAQDAS) helps organise data but does not do the interpretation.

**Memorise.**

- Braun and Clarke: six-phase thematic analysis.

- Open → axial → selective coding (Strauss and Corbin); constant comparison (Glaser and Strauss).

- CAQDAS: NVivo, ATLAS.ti, MAXQDA.

**Analyse / traps.** Software supports analysis; the researcher still codes and interprets.

### 2D.5 Rigour and Trustworthiness

**Concept.** Lincoln and Guba (1985) replaced the quantitative criteria with four parallel ones. *Credibility* parallels internal validity, *transferability* parallels external validity, *dependability* parallels reliability and *confirmability* parallels objectivity. Credibility is strengthened by prolonged engagement, persistent observation, triangulation, peer debriefing, negative-case analysis and *member checking* (asking participants to verify the interpretation). Dependability and confirmability rest on an *audit trail*, a documented record of decisions. *Reflexivity* means the researcher critically examines their own influence on the study, and thick description supports transferability.

**Memorise.**

- Credibility ↔ internal validity; transferability ↔ external validity; dependability ↔ reliability; confirmability ↔ objectivity.

- Member checking: participant verification. Audit trail: documented decisions.

**Analyse / traps.** If an option pairs transferability with reliability, it is wrong; reliability’s counterpart is dependability.

### 2D.6 Approaches and Perspectives

**Concept.** *Ethnography* studies a culture through prolonged participation (Malinowski, Geertz). *Grounded theory* (Glaser and Strauss, 1967; Strauss and Corbin; Charmaz’s constructivist version) generates theory from data. *Phenomenology* (Husserl) describes lived experience and uses *bracketing* (epoché) to set aside presuppositions. *Narrative inquiry* studies stories, and *case study* gives an in-depth account of a bounded case. The emic–etic distinction (Kenneth Pike) separates the insider’s categories from the outsider’s analytic categories.

**Memorise.**

- Ethnography → culture; grounded theory → theory from data; phenomenology → lived experience; bracketing → Husserl.

- Thick description → Geertz.

**Analyse / traps.** Phenomenology describes experience; grounded theory builds theory. Do not interchange them.

## TOPIC 2E – Quantitative Method

### 2E.1 Characteristics of Quantitative Research

**Concept.** Quantitative research expresses phenomena in numbers and analyses them statistically. It is deductive (it starts with theory and hypotheses), uses structured instruments such as tests and questionnaires, draws large and preferably random samples, aims at objectivity and generalisation, and can be replicated. Its designs include descriptive surveys, correlational studies and experimental or quasi-experimental studies. Its strength is precision and the ability to test hypotheses; its weakness is limited attention to context and meaning.

**Memorise.**

- Numbers, structured tools, large samples, statistical tests, generalisation.

- Philosophical base: positivism and post-positivism.

**Analyse / traps.** Quantitative studies still need valid and reliable tools; numbers do not guarantee objectivity.

### 2E.2 Scales of Measurement

**Concept.** S. S. Stevens (1946) classified measurement into four levels, remembered as NOIR. A *nominal* scale only classifies into categories (gender, religion); the mode and chi-square are suitable. An *ordinal* scale ranks (first, second, third) without equal gaps; the median, percentiles and rank correlation fit. An *interval* scale has equal intervals but an arbitrary zero (Celsius temperature, IQ, calendar year), so differences are meaningful but ratios are not; the mean, standard deviation and Pearson’s r are suitable. A *ratio* scale has equal intervals and a true zero (height, weight, income, age), so ratios are meaningful and every statistic can be used.

| Scale    | Property                        | Example               | Suitable average     |
|----------|---------------------------------|-----------------------|----------------------|
| Nominal  | Classification                  | Religion, blood group | Mode                 |
| Ordinal  | Order                           | Rank, grade           | Median               |
| Interval | Equal intervals, arbitrary zero | Temperature in °C, IQ | Mean                 |
| Ratio    | True zero                       | Weight, income        | Mean, geometric mean |

**Analyse / traps.** 40 °C is not twice as hot as 20 °C because Celsius is interval; Kelvin is ratio. Likert items are ordinal in strict terms though often treated as interval.

### 2E.3 Descriptive Statistics and Correlation

**Concept.** Measures of central tendency are the mean, median and mode; measures of dispersion are the range, quartile deviation, mean deviation, variance and standard deviation. In a perfectly *normal distribution* the curve is bell-shaped and symmetrical, mean = median = mode, and about 68%, 95% and 99.7% of cases lie within one, two and three standard deviations of the mean. In a positively skewed distribution mean \> median \> mode, and in a negatively skewed one mean \< median \< mode. Karl Pearson’s empirical relation for moderate skew is mode = 3 median − 2 mean. The correlation coefficient r ranges from −1 to +1; its square, r², is the proportion of variance shared. Spearman’s rho is used for ranks.

**Memorise.**

- Normal curve: 68–95–99.7 rule; mean = median = mode.

- Positive skew: tail to the right; mean is pulled highest.

- r = −1 perfect negative; 0 no linear relation; +1 perfect positive.

**Analyse / traps.** Correlation is not causation, and r = 0 means no linear relation, not necessarily no relation.

### 2E.4 Errors and Significance in Hypothesis Testing

**Concept.** A *Type I error* (alpha) is rejecting a null hypothesis that is true, a false positive. A *Type II error* (beta) is failing to reject a null hypothesis that is false, a false negative. *Power* (1 − beta) is the probability of correctly rejecting a false null. The *significance level* (commonly 0.05) is the maximum Type I risk the researcher accepts. The *p-value* is the probability of obtaining a result at least as extreme as the one observed if the null hypothesis were true; if p is less than alpha, H0 is rejected. For a fixed sample size, lowering alpha raises beta.

**Memorise.**

- Type I = false alarm (alpha). Type II = missed effect (beta). Power = 1 − beta.

- p \< alpha → reject H0; a smaller p means stronger evidence against H0.

**Analyse / traps.** The p-value is not the probability that the null hypothesis is true.

### 2E.5 Statistical Tests: t-test, ANOVA and Chi-square

**Concept.** Parametric tests assume interval or ratio data, approximate normality and equal variances. The *t-test* (William Gosset, writing as “Student”, 1908) compares means of one sample, two independent groups or paired observations, especially with small samples; the *z-test* is used with large samples. *ANOVA* (Fisher) compares three or more means through the F-ratio of between-group to within-group variance. The *chi-square test* (Pearson) analyses frequencies in categories, for goodness of fit or independence. Non-parametric alternatives include the Mann–Whitney U test (for the independent t-test), the Wilcoxon signed-rank test (for the paired t-test) and the Kruskal–Wallis test (for one-way ANOVA).

**Memorise.**

- t-test: two means; ANOVA: three or more means; chi-square: categorical frequencies.

- Mann–Whitney U → independent t; Wilcoxon → paired t; Kruskal–Wallis → ANOVA.

- F = between-group variance ÷ within-group variance.

**Analyse / traps.** Running many t-tests instead of one ANOVA inflates Type I error.

## TOPIC 3A – Identifying and Selecting a Research Problem

### 3A.1 Sources and Criteria for Selecting a Problem

**Concept.** Identifying the problem is the first and most important step because every later decision depends on it; a problem well stated is half solved. Problems arise from personal experience and observation, gaps and recommendations in the literature, theories that need testing, practical or policy needs, discussions with supervisors and experts, and new technologies. A suitable problem is researchable (it can be investigated with data), significant and original, feasible in time, money, access and skill, interesting to the researcher, ethical and clearly delimited. Topics that are too broad cannot be completed; topics that are too narrow lack significance. The usual movement is from a general area to a narrowed, specific problem.

**Memorise.**

- Sources: experience, literature gaps, theory, previous recommendations, experts, social needs.

- Criteria: researchable, significant, original, feasible, ethical, interesting.

- First step of research = selecting and defining the problem.

**Analyse / traps.** “Suggestions for further research” at the end of earlier studies are a rich source of problems.

### 3A.2 Statement of the Problem, Objectives and Variables

**Concept.** The problem statement says what is to be studied in clear, specific and researchable terms, naming the variables, the population and the relationship of interest. It usually moves from background to gap to purpose and may be written as a question or a declarative sentence. *Objectives* convert the problem into achievable aims, begin with action verbs (to examine, to compare, to determine) and are often written to be specific, measurable and realistic. Research questions or hypotheses then follow from the objectives. Concepts in the statement must be operationalised so that they can be measured.

**Memorise.**

- Good statement: clear, specific, researchable, variables named.

- Objectives: action verbs, measurable, linked to the problem.

**Analyse / traps.** Objectives describe what the study will do; hypotheses predict what the study will find.

### 3A.3 Delimitations and Limitations

**Concept.** *Delimitations* are the boundaries the researcher deliberately sets on the study, such as the geographical area, population, time period and variables included; they are choices within the researcher’s control. *Limitations* are weaknesses or constraints that the researcher cannot fully control, such as small sample size, limited time, imperfect tools, self-report bias and restricted generalisability. *Assumptions* are things taken to be true without proof. Stating all three honestly lets readers judge how far the findings apply.

**Memorise.**

- Delimitations = chosen scope. Limitations = unavoidable weaknesses.

- Assumptions = accepted without testing.

**Analyse / traps.** If a restriction was a deliberate choice (only urban schools), it is a delimitation; if it was forced (low response rate), it is a limitation.

## TOPIC 3B – Review of Literature

### 3B.1 Purpose and Qualities of a Literature Review

**Concept.** A literature review surveys and critically evaluates what is already known about the topic. It helps the researcher avoid duplication, discover the research gap, sharpen the problem and hypotheses, build the theoretical framework, learn which methods and tools worked, and compare later findings with earlier ones. A good review is relevant, current, organised thematically or chronologically, critical rather than merely descriptive, and ends by showing what is missing. It begins early but continues throughout the study. A *research gap* is a question that earlier studies left unanswered or answered inadequately.

**Memorise.**

- Purposes: avoid duplication, find gap, refine problem, frame theory, choose methods.

- Good review: critical synthesis, not a list of summaries.

**Analyse / traps.** Citing many studies is not a review; synthesis and critique are what count.

### 3B.2 Primary and Secondary Sources of Literature

**Concept.** *Primary literature* reports original research or ideas for the first time: research articles, theses, conference papers, patents and technical reports. *Secondary literature* interprets or summarises primary work: review articles, textbooks, encyclopaedias, handbooks and yearbooks. *Tertiary sources* help you find the others: indexes, abstracts, bibliographies, directories and databases. *Grey literature* includes reports, working papers and theses that are not commercially published.

**Memorise.**

- Primary: original research. Secondary: reviews, textbooks. Tertiary: indexes, bibliographies.

- Grey literature: outside commercial publishing.

**Analyse / traps.** A review article is secondary even if it appears in a journal.

### 3B.3 Search Strategies and Boolean Operators

**Concept.** Effective searching starts with keywords and their synonyms. Boolean operators combine terms: *AND* narrows the search (both terms must appear), *OR* broadens it (either term, useful for synonyms), and *NOT* excludes a term. *Truncation* (for example educat\*) finds word endings, wildcards replace single characters, and quotation marks search an exact phrase. Field searching, filters for date and type, and controlled vocabulary or thesaurus terms (such as MeSH in medicine) refine results. *Citation chaining* follows references backward and citations forward from a good paper.

**Memorise.**

- AND narrows; OR widens; NOT excludes.

- Truncation (\*) for word stems; quotes for exact phrases.

- Snowballing = following references and citing papers.

**Analyse / traps.** If you want more results, use OR; if you want fewer and more precise results, use AND.

### 3B.4 Repositories: Shodhganga and INFLIBNET

**Concept.** *Shodhganga* is the national repository of Indian electronic theses and dissertations (ETDs), maintained by the INFLIBNET Centre, Gandhinagar, an inter-university centre of the UGC. UGC regulations require universities to deposit the electronic copy of every approved doctoral thesis there. *Shodhgangotri* holds synopses of research topics from registered scholars. Other INFLIBNET services include *e-ShodhSindhu*, the consortium for electronic resources, and *Vidwan*, the database of experts. Searching Shodhganga shows what Indian doctoral work already exists on a topic.

**Memorise.**

- Shodhganga = theses; Shodhgangotri = synopses; INFLIBNET Centre, Gandhinagar (UGC).

- e-ShodhSindhu = e-resources consortium; Vidwan = experts database.

**Analyse / traps.** Shodhganga holds completed theses, whereas Shodhgangotri holds synopses of ongoing research.

### 3B.5 Types of Reviews and Journal Metrics

**Concept.** A *narrative* review summarises a field selectively. A *systematic review* follows an explicit protocol, searches comprehensively, applies stated inclusion criteria and appraises quality, often reported by PRISMA guidelines. A *meta-analysis* statistically pools effect sizes across studies. A *scoping review* maps the extent of literature. Journal quality is often judged by metrics. The *impact factor* (Clarivate’s Journal Citation Reports) is the number of citations in a year to items published in the previous two years divided by the number of citable items in those years. *CiteScore* (Scopus) uses a four-year window. The *h-index* (Hirsch, 2005) is the largest number h such that the author has h papers each cited at least h times; the *i10-index* counts papers with at least ten citations.

**Memorise.**

- Impact factor: two-year window, Clarivate. CiteScore: four-year window, Scopus.

- h-index: Hirsch, 2005. Systematic review: protocol; meta-analysis: statistical pooling.

**Analyse / traps.** Impact factor measures a journal, h-index measures an author; mixing them is a typical trap.

## TOPIC 3C – Formulating Hypotheses

### 3C.1 Types of Hypotheses

**Concept.** Hypotheses can be classified in several ways. A *research (scientific) hypothesis* states the expected relationship in substantive terms; a *statistical hypothesis* restates it in terms of population parameters, such as μ1 = μ2. A *null hypothesis* denies any difference or relationship, and an *alternative hypothesis* asserts one. A *directional (one-tailed) hypothesis* predicts the direction of the difference (group A will score higher than group B) and is tested with a one-tailed test; a *non-directional (two-tailed) hypothesis* predicts only that a difference exists. A *simple hypothesis* links one independent and one dependent variable, whereas a *complex hypothesis* involves several variables. A *working hypothesis* is a provisional guess used to guide early investigation.

**Memorise.**

- Directional → one-tailed test; non-directional → two-tailed test.

- Simple = one IV and one DV; complex = more than one of either.

- Null (H0) and alternative (H1/Ha) are statistical forms.

**Analyse / traps.** The wording “is greater than” or “is lower than” signals a directional hypothesis; “is different from” signals a non-directional one.

### 3C.2 Null and Alternative Hypotheses

**Concept.** The null hypothesis states that there is no significant difference or relationship, for example “there is no significant difference in the achievement of boys and girls”. It is the starting assumption of statistical testing, because it is easier to find evidence against a precise statement than to prove a vague one. The researcher computes a test statistic and its p-value; if p is smaller than the chosen significance level, H0 is rejected in favour of H1, otherwise we *fail to reject* it. Failing to reject is not proof that H0 is true, only that the data did not provide enough evidence against it. Typical forms are H0: μ1 = μ2 against H1: μ1 ≠ μ2 (two-tailed) or H1: μ1 \> μ2 (one-tailed).

**Memorise.**

- “No significant difference/relationship” = null hypothesis.

- Decision words: reject H0 or fail to reject H0 (not “prove”, not “accept”).

- p \< alpha → reject H0.

**Analyse / traps.** An option that says the null hypothesis is proved true by a non-significant result is wrong.

### 3C.3 Sources and Formulation of Hypotheses

**Concept.** Hypotheses come from theory (deduced consequences), earlier research findings, everyday observation and experience, analogy with other fields, cultural beliefs and insight. Goode and Hatt list sources such as science itself, analogies, culture and personal experience. Formulating a hypothesis involves reviewing the literature, identifying the variables, stating the expected relationship, checking that it can be tested with available methods, and operationalising the terms. A good hypothesis is clear, specific, testable, consistent with known facts, simple, and stated before data are collected.

**Memorise.**

- Sources: theory, past research, observation, analogy, culture, experience.

- Qualities: testable, specific, simple, consistent with existing knowledge.

**Analyse / traps.** A hypothesis built after looking at the data and then “tested” on the same data is poor practice.

## TOPIC 3D – Research Design

### 3D.1 Purpose and Principles of Research Design

**Concept.** The research design is the plan that connects the research questions to the data and the analysis; it specifies the type of study, the sample, the tools, the timing and the analytical procedures. Its purpose is to obtain valid answers economically and to control variance. Kothari groups designs as exploratory (flexible, to formulate a problem), descriptive and diagnostic (accurate description, little flexibility) and experimental (hypothesis testing). Fisher’s three principles of experimental design are *replication*, *randomisation* and *local control*, and Kerlinger’s MAX-MIN-CON principle guides variance control. A research proposal or synopsis sets out the design in advance: title, background, review, objectives, hypotheses, methodology, sampling, tools, analysis plan, schedule and references.

**Memorise.**

- Design = blueprint. Fisher: replication, randomisation, local control.

- Exploratory = flexible; descriptive = structured; experimental = tests causal hypotheses.

**Analyse / traps.** Design is decided before data collection; it is not the same as the report structure.

### 3D.2 Exploratory and Mixed-Methods Designs

**Concept.** Exploratory designs are used to formulate a problem or hypotheses when knowledge is limited. Selltiz and colleagues named three common methods: a literature survey, an experience survey of knowledgeable people, and the analysis of insight-stimulating examples (case studies). Mixed-methods designs, as classified by Creswell, include the *convergent parallel design* (both strands collected together and merged), the *explanatory sequential design* (quantitative first, qualitative to explain), and the *exploratory sequential design* (qualitative first, then a quantitative test). Notation such as QUAN → qual shows the order and the priority of the strands.

**Memorise.**

- Exploratory methods: literature survey, experience survey, insight-stimulating cases.

- Explanatory sequential = QUAN then qual; exploratory sequential = qual then QUAN.

**Analyse / traps.** In “explanatory” sequential, the qualitative strand explains numbers, which is the reverse of the exploratory sequence.

### 3D.3 Quasi-experimental and Experimental Designs

**Concept.** In quasi-experimental designs the researcher manipulates a treatment but cannot randomly assign participants, as in the non-equivalent control group design, the interrupted time-series design and the regression-discontinuity design. True experimental designs use random assignment. Fisher’s formal designs include the *completely randomised design* (units assigned wholly at random), the *randomised block design* (units grouped into homogeneous blocks and randomised within blocks), the *Latin square* (two blockings) and *factorial designs* (several factors studied together with their interactions). Notation: R for random assignment, X for the treatment and O for an observation.

**Memorise.**

- Quasi-experiment: treatment but no randomisation.

- CRD, RBD, Latin square, factorial: formal experimental designs.

**Analyse / traps.** If groups were formed by existing classrooms rather than random assignment, the design is quasi-experimental.

### 3D.4 Construct Validity and Types of Variables

**Concept.** *Construct validity* asks whether the study’s measures and treatments really represent the theoretical constructs intended; it is threatened by vague definitions of the construct, relying on a single measure (mono-operation bias) or a single method (mono-method bias). Variables can be further classified: *latent variables* are not directly observable (intelligence, attitude) and are inferred from *manifest* or observed indicators such as test scores; *dichotomous variables* have two categories (pass/fail); *dummy variables* code categories as 0 and 1 for regression; *composite variables* combine several items into one score.

**Memorise.**

- Latent = unobserved construct; manifest = observed indicator.

- Dummy variable: 0/1 coding of categories.

**Analyse / traps.** Construct validity concerns meaning of measures, not the size of the sample.

## TOPIC 3E – Sampling

### 3E.1 Basic Concepts: Sample, Statistic and Errors

**Concept.** The *population* (universe) is the complete set of units that the researcher wants to describe; the *sampling frame* is the actual list from which the sample is drawn; a *sample* is the subset studied. A *parameter* describes the population (written with Greek letters, such as μ and σ) and a *statistic* describes the sample (x̄ and s). Sampling is used because it saves time and cost and is the only possible option when testing is destructive. *Sampling error* is the difference between a sample statistic and the population parameter that arises by chance because only a part is studied; it falls as the sample grows and can be estimated in probability samples through the *standard error* (σ/√n). *Non-sampling errors*, such as non-response, measurement and coverage errors, occur in censuses as well and do not shrink with a larger sample.

**Memorise.**

- Parameter = population; statistic = sample.

- Standard error = σ/√n; to halve it, the sample must be four times larger.

- Census has no sampling error but may have large non-sampling error.

**Analyse / traps.** A very large but biased sample is still biased; size cannot cure bad selection.

### 3E.2 Sample Size

**Concept.** Sample size depends on the variability of the population, the precision (margin of error) required, the confidence level, the number of subgroups to be analysed, the expected response rate and the resources available. For a proportion, a common formula is n = Z²pq / e²; with p = 0.5, 95% confidence (Z = 1.96) and a 5% margin of error it gives about 384. Yamane’s simple formula is n = N / (1 + Ne²). Beyond a moderately large population, the population size matters little; what matters is the absolute sample size. About 30 observations are often cited as the minimum for the central limit theorem to begin to apply.

**Memorise.**

- 95% confidence → Z = 1.96; 99% → Z = 2.58.

- Greater variability or tighter precision → larger sample.

- Increasing n reduces sampling error, not bias.

**Analyse / traps.** Sample size does not need to be a fixed percentage of the population.

### 3E.3 Probability and Non-probability Sampling Methods

**Concept.** In *simple random sampling* each unit has an equal and independent chance, drawn by lottery or random number tables. *Systematic sampling* picks every k-th unit after a random start, where k = N/n; it is convenient but risky if the list has a hidden pattern. *Stratified sampling* divides the population into internally homogeneous strata and draws randomly from each, proportionately or disproportionately; it improves precision. *Cluster sampling* randomly selects whole groups (schools, villages) that are internally heterogeneous, saving cost but usually with higher error than simple random sampling of the same size. *Multistage sampling* selects in successive stages (state, district, village, household), as national surveys do. Non-probability methods include *convenience*, *purposive (judgemental)*, *quota* and *snowball* sampling; their selection probabilities are unknown, so sampling error cannot be calculated.

**Memorise.**

- Stratified: homogeneous within strata. Cluster: heterogeneous within clusters.

- Quota = non-random equivalent of stratified; snowball = chain referral for hidden populations.

- Sampling with replacement: a unit may be chosen more than once.

**Analyse / traps.** Stratified draws from every stratum; cluster draws only some clusters and studies all or many units in them.

## TOPIC 3F – Data Collection

### 3F.1 Questionnaire, Schedule and Question Types

**Concept.** A good questionnaire is short, clear, logically ordered (easy and non-sensitive items first) and free from jargon, ambiguity, leading, loaded and double-barrelled questions. *Closed-ended* items (dichotomous, multiple-choice, rating, ranking) are easy to score; *open-ended* items give rich detail but are harder to code. *Contingency or filter questions* direct only some respondents to follow-up items. Pre-testing and a cover letter explaining the purpose improve quality and response. Online surveys are fast and cheap but suffer from self-selection, coverage bias (people without internet are excluded) and low control over who answers.

**Memorise.**

- Avoid double-barrelled questions (two ideas in one item).

- Closed = easy to analyse; open = depth.

- Online survey limits: coverage and self-selection bias.

**Analyse / traps.** A question such as “Is the teacher knowledgeable and friendly?” is double-barrelled.

### 3F.2 Reliability of Tools

**Concept.** *Test–retest* reliability correlates scores from the same people on two occasions; practice and memory effects can distort it. *Parallel-forms* reliability correlates two equivalent versions. *Split-half* reliability correlates two halves of one test (often odd and even items); because the halves are shorter, the coefficient is stepped up with the Spearman–Brown formula, r(full) = 2r / (1 + r). *KR-20* suits dichotomous items and *Cronbach’s alpha* (Cronbach, 1951) suits multi-point items such as Likert scales. *Inter-rater* agreement is shown by percentage agreement or Cohen’s kappa. Longer tests, clearer items and uniform administration raise reliability.

**Memorise.**

- Test–retest → stability; parallel forms → equivalence; split-half, KR-20, alpha → internal consistency.

- Spearman–Brown: corrects split-half; Cohen’s kappa: agreement between raters.

- Alpha ≥ 0.70 acceptable.

**Analyse / traps.** Matching each method to the type of reliability is the usual question pattern.

### 3F.3 Validity of Tools

**Concept.** *Content validity* is judged by experts who check that items represent the whole domain, often guided by a table of specifications; *face validity* is only the surface impression. *Criterion-related validity* compares scores with an external criterion, either measured at the same time (concurrent) or later (predictive). *Construct validity* is shown by convergent evidence (correlation with measures of the same construct), discriminant evidence (low correlation with unrelated constructs), known-groups comparison and factor analysis; Campbell and Fiske (1959) proposed the multitrait–multimethod matrix. A test’s validity cannot exceed the square root of its reliability.

**Memorise.**

- Concurrent = now; predictive = future. Convergent and discriminant belong to construct validity.

- Maximum validity = √ reliability.

**Analyse / traps.** Content validity is a matter of judgement, not a correlation coefficient.

### 3F.4 Observation, Rating and Projective Techniques

**Concept.** Observation may be participant or non-participant, structured or unstructured, overt or covert, and uses tools such as checklists, rating scales, anecdotal records, time sampling and event sampling. Rating errors include the *halo effect* (a general impression colours all ratings), *leniency* or severity error, *central tendency error* and the *logical error*. Interviewer bias can also distort answers. *Projective techniques* present ambiguous stimuli so that people project hidden feelings: the Rorschach inkblot test (1921), the Thematic Apperception Test (Murray and Morgan, 1935), word association, sentence completion and draw-a-person tests. They are used mainly for personality assessment and are open to subjective scoring.

**Memorise.**

- Halo effect: one trait influences ratings of others.

- Rorschach = inkblots; TAT = pictures and stories.

- Projective tests: disguised purpose, ambiguous stimuli.

**Analyse / traps.** Halo effect is a rating error, not a sampling error.

### 3F.5 Standardised Tests

**Concept.** A standardised test has fixed content, uniform administration and scoring procedures, and norms obtained from a representative sample, so that an individual’s score can be interpreted against others. Norm-referenced tests compare a person with the norm group using percentiles, stanines or T-scores; criterion-referenced tests compare performance with a set standard. Examples are intelligence tests (Binet–Simon 1905, Stanford–Binet, Wechsler scales), aptitude and achievement tests, personality inventories (MMPI, Cattell’s 16PF) and interest inventories (Strong, Kuder). Speed tests stress time, whereas power tests stress difficulty.

**Memorise.**

- Norms → interpretation against a reference group.

- Binet–Simon: first intelligence test (1905).

**Analyse / traps.** A teacher-made test is not standardised, even if it is reliable.

## TOPIC 3G – Data Analysis and Interpretation

### 3G.1 Processing of Data: Editing, Coding and Tabulation

**Concept.** Processing prepares raw data for analysis, and Kothari lists the order as editing, coding, classification and tabulation. *Editing* checks completed instruments for errors, omissions and inconsistencies, either in the field or centrally. *Coding* assigns numerical or symbolic labels to responses, guided by a codebook. *Classification* groups data by attributes or by class intervals. *Tabulation* arranges the classified data in rows and columns; the parts of a table are the title, caption (column headings), stub (row labels), body, footnotes and source note. Data cleaning also deals with missing values and outliers.

**Memorise.**

- Order: editing → coding → classification → tabulation.

- Table parts: title, stub, caption, body, footnote, source.

**Analyse / traps.** Stub refers to the row headings; caption refers to the column headings.

### 3G.2 Skewness and Central Tendency

**Concept.** The mean uses all values but is pulled by extremes; the median is the middle value and is best for skewed data or open-ended classes; the mode is the most frequent value and is the only average for nominal data. The geometric mean suits growth rates and the harmonic mean suits rates such as speed. In a symmetrical distribution all three coincide; in positive skew (long right tail) mean \> median \> mode; in negative skew mean \< median \< mode. Karl Pearson’s coefficient of skewness is (mean − mode) / SD. *Kurtosis* describes peakedness: leptokurtic (peaked), mesokurtic (normal), platykurtic (flat). The *coefficient of variation* (SD / mean × 100) compares variability between series with different units.

**Memorise.**

- Positive skew: mean is highest. Negative skew: mean is lowest.

- Median for skewed data; mode for nominal data; SD is the most reliable measure of dispersion.

**Analyse / traps.** In skewed income data the median represents the typical person better than the mean.

### 3G.3 Inferential Statistics and Non-parametric Tests

**Concept.** Inferential statistics draw conclusions about a population from a sample, through *estimation* (point and interval estimates, with confidence intervals) and *hypothesis testing*. Parametric tests (t, z, F) assume interval or ratio data and approximately normal distributions; non-parametric or distribution-free tests (chi-square, Mann–Whitney U, Wilcoxon, Kruskal–Wallis, sign test, Kolmogorov–Smirnov, Spearman) suit nominal or ordinal data, small samples and non-normal data. Degrees of freedom: for a chi-square test of independence df = (rows − 1)(columns − 1), so a 3 × 4 table has 6; for goodness of fit df = k − 1; for an independent-samples t-test df = n1 + n2 − 2.

**Memorise.**

- Chi-square works on frequencies, not percentages.

- df (independence) = (r − 1)(c − 1); df (goodness of fit) = k − 1.

- Non-parametric tests: fewer assumptions but usually less power.

**Analyse / traps.** Chi-square is non-parametric even though it is widely used with large samples.

### 3G.4 Correlation and Regression

**Concept.** Pearson’s product-moment r measures the strength and direction of a linear relationship between interval or ratio variables; Spearman’s rank correlation and Kendall’s tau serve ordinal data; point-biserial and phi coefficients handle a dichotomy. Partial and multiple correlation add control for or combine variables. *Regression* predicts one variable from another by the least-squares line y = a + bx, where b is the slope. The two regression lines intersect at the means, r² equals the product of the two regression coefficients (so r is their geometric mean, with the common sign), and r² is the coefficient of determination. Correlation, however high, does not prove cause, and spurious correlations arise from a third variable.

**Memorise.**

- b(yx) × b(xy) = r²; both coefficients carry the sign of r.

- Regression lines meet at (x̄, ȳ); r² = variance explained.

**Analyse / traps.** Correlation measures association; regression is used for prediction.

### 3G.5 Interpretation of Data

**Concept.** Interpretation draws meaning from analysed results by linking them to the hypotheses, earlier findings and theory, and by stating what they imply. It is the bridge between analysis and the report. Kothari’s precautions are to avoid generalising beyond the population studied, to check that the sample and tools were sound, not to infer cause from correlation alone, to keep extraneous factors and limitations in view, and to avoid selective reporting. Statistical significance is not the same as practical importance, so effect sizes (for example Cohen’s d) and confidence intervals should accompany p-values.

**Memorise.**

- Statistical significance ≠ practical significance.

- Do not generalise beyond the data; do not infer cause from correlation.

**Analyse / traps.** A result can be significant with a huge sample and still be trivial in size.

## TOPIC 3H – Report Writing

### 3H.1 Structure of a Research Report

**Concept.** A research report has three parts. The *preliminary pages* include the title page, certificate, declaration, acknowledgements, abstract, table of contents and lists of tables, figures and abbreviations. The *main body* contains the introduction, review of literature, methodology, results and analysis, discussion, conclusions, recommendations and limitations with suggestions for further research. The *end matter* contains the bibliography or references, appendices (questionnaires, raw data, tools, supplementary tables) and, where used, a glossary or index. Technical reports serve scholars and funders, while popular reports serve the general public with simpler language.

**Memorise.**

- Preliminary pages → body → end matter.

- Appendix: supporting material kept out of the body.

- Abstract: short summary, typically 150–300 words.

**Analyse / traps.** Tools and raw data belong in appendices, not in the main chapters.

### 3H.2 References, Footnotes and Latin Abbreviations

**Concept.** A *footnote* appears at the bottom of the page and an *endnote* at the end of a chapter or document. A *bibliography* lists all works consulted, whereas a *reference list* lists only works cited, usually in alphabetical order by author’s surname. Latin abbreviations save space: *ibid.* (ibidem, in the same place) refers to the immediately preceding source; *op. cit.* (opere citato) refers to a work already cited; *loc. cit.* (loco citato) refers to the same passage; *et al.* means and others; *cf.* means compare; *viz.* means namely; *passim* means here and there; *sic* means thus, as in the original.

**Memorise.**

- Ibid. = same as the immediately preceding reference.

- Op. cit. = work cited earlier; loc. cit. = same place cited earlier.

- Bibliography = all consulted; references = only cited.

**Analyse / traps.** Ibid. cannot be used if another source has intervened since the one it refers to.

### 3H.3 Language, Conclusions and Recommendations

**Concept.** Research writing should be clear, precise, objective and formal, usually impersonal and in the past tense for methods and results, and free of jargon, emotive words and padding. Conclusions must follow from the findings, answer the objectives and state whether hypotheses were supported, without claiming more than the data allow and without introducing new data. Recommendations should be practical and linked to specific findings, and the report should also state limitations and suggest directions for further research. Careful revision and proofreading complete the work.

**Memorise.**

- Conclusions: derived from results, not beyond them.

- Recommendations: practical, specific, evidence-based.

**Analyse / traps.** A conclusion that introduces new results is a flaw; new results belong in the results section.

## TOPIC 4A – Format and Structure of a Thesis

### 4A.1 Meaning and Organisation of a Thesis

**Concept.** A thesis is a long, formally written document submitted for a degree, presenting the candidate’s original research, the method used and the conclusions reached. In India and the United Kingdom “thesis” usually denotes the doctoral work and “dissertation” the shorter master’s work, while in the United States the usage is often reversed. Before the thesis comes the *synopsis*, a concise research plan submitted at registration. The work is carried out under a supervisor, monitored by a research advisory or doctoral committee, and finally examined by external evaluators and defended in an open *viva voce*. A thesis must show an original contribution to knowledge, a mastery of the literature, and a sound method. Its parts follow a standard order: preliminary pages, main chapters and end matter.

**Memorise.**

- Synopsis → research plan; thesis → completed doctoral document; viva voce → oral defence.

- Standard order: preliminary pages → chapters → end matter.

- Original contribution is the central requirement for a doctoral thesis.

**Analyse / traps.** A thesis differs from a term paper or project report by its originality and depth; a synopsis is a plan, not a summary of finished work.

### 4A.2 Preliminary Pages

**Concept.** The preliminary (front) pages come before the first chapter. They normally include the *title page* (title, candidate’s name, degree, supervisor, institution, month and year), the supervisor’s *certificate* stating that the work is original and was done under guidance, the candidate’s *declaration* of originality, the *acknowledgements*, the *abstract*, the *table of contents*, and the *lists of tables, figures and abbreviations*. A *preface* is written by the author about the purpose and scope of the work, whereas a *foreword* is written by someone else. These pages are numbered in lower-case Roman numerals (i, ii, iii), and the main text restarts with Arabic numeral 1.

**Memorise.**

- Certificate: by the supervisor. Declaration: by the candidate.

- Preface: by the author; foreword: by another person.

- Front matter in Roman numerals; text in Arabic numerals.

**Analyse / traps.** If the question asks who certifies originality of guidance, the answer is the supervisor, not the candidate.

### 4A.3 Main Chapters of a Thesis

**Concept.** The typical chapters are: *Introduction* (background, statement of the problem, objectives, hypotheses, significance, scope and delimitations, definitions of terms); *Review of Related Literature* (what is known, the gap and the theoretical framework); *Methodology* (design, population, sample, tools, procedure and the plan for analysis); *Results or Data Analysis* (findings in tables and figures, with minimal interpretation); *Discussion* (meaning of the findings, comparison with earlier studies); and *Summary, Conclusions and Recommendations* (what was found, what follows, limitations and suggestions for further research). Some theses merge results and discussion; qualitative theses may arrange chapters by theme.

**Memorise.**

- Chapter order: introduction → literature → methodology → results → discussion → conclusions.

- Results report; discussion interprets.

- Delimitations and objectives belong in the introduction.

**Analyse / traps.** Putting interpretation into the results chapter, or new data into the conclusion chapter, are standard faults.

### 4A.4 Appendices, References, Index and Glossary

**Concept.** The end matter follows the last chapter. The *bibliography or reference list* gives full details of sources, alphabetically by author. *Appendices* hold supporting material that would interrupt the text: questionnaires, tests, raw data, permission letters and extra tables, each labelled Appendix A, B and so on and mentioned in the text. A *glossary* is an alphabetical list of specialised terms with meanings. An *index* is an alphabetical list of subjects and names with page numbers, mostly used in books. In Indian practice the term *annexure* is used for official documents or material from other sources attached to the thesis, although appendix and annexure are often used loosely.

**Memorise.**

- References before appendices in most styles (APA).

- Appendix = supporting material; glossary = terms; index = subjects with page numbers.

**Analyse / traps.** An index is not a table of contents: the table of contents lists the thesis’s own headings in order of appearance, while an index is alphabetical.

### 4A.5 UGC Regulations and Thesis Submission

**Concept.** The UGC regulates the doctoral degree through its Ph.D. regulations (a 2016 version, later revised in 2022), which cover admission, course work, supervisors, research advisory committees, duration, evaluation and submission. The thesis is evaluated by external examiners, followed by an open viva voce. Earlier regulations required scholars to make presentations and publish a paper before submission, so check the current rules. A soft copy of the approved thesis must be deposited with INFLIBNET for hosting on Shodhganga, and under the UGC (Promotion of Academic Integrity and Prevention of Plagiarism in Higher Educational Institutions) Regulations, 2018 the thesis must carry an undertaking of originality and be checked with plagiarism-detection software.

**Memorise.**

- Thesis soft copy → INFLIBNET → Shodhganga.

- Plagiarism regulations: UGC 2018; undertaking of originality is mandatory.

- Evaluation: external examiner(s) and open viva.

**Analyse / traps.** Do not mix up the Ph.D. regulations (degree process) with the 2018 plagiarism regulations (academic integrity).

## TOPIC 4B – Format and Structure of a Research Article

### 4B.1 IMRaD and Sections of an Article

**Concept.** Most scientific articles follow the IMRaD structure: Introduction, Methods, Results and Discussion, preceded by the title, abstract and keywords and followed by the conclusion, acknowledgements and references. The *Introduction* tells why the study matters (background, gap, objective); the *Methods* section tells how it was done in enough detail for replication; the *Results* section reports what was found without interpretation; the *Discussion* explains what the results mean, compares them with earlier work, admits limitations and states implications. The overall shape is like an hourglass, moving from the general field to the specific study and back to general significance. Abstracts and conclusions are usually written last.

**Memorise.**

- IMRaD: Introduction (why), Methods (how), Results (what), Discussion (so what).

- Methods must allow replication.

- Results state findings; discussion interprets them.

**Analyse / traps.** Interpretation inside the results section, or new data in the discussion, indicates a structural error.

### 4B.2 Title, Abstract and Keywords

**Concept.** A good *title* is concise, specific and informative, contains the key variables, and avoids unexplained abbreviations. The *abstract* is a stand-alone summary of the purpose, method, main results and conclusion, usually 150 to 300 words, containing no citations and no new information. It may be *informative* (structured under headings) or *descriptive* (indicating what the paper covers). *Keywords*, usually three to eight, are the terms by which indexing services and search engines retrieve the paper, so they should be standard terms rather than the title’s words alone. Some journals also ask for a graphical abstract or highlights.

**Memorise.**

- Abstract: self-contained, no citations, written last.

- Keywords: for indexing and retrieval.

- Structured abstract: Background, Methods, Results, Conclusions.

**Analyse / traps.** An abstract that cites references or introduces material absent from the paper is faulty.

### 4B.3 Types of Articles

**Concept.** An *original research article* reports new data and is the main type. A *short communication* or *brief report* publishes preliminary or limited findings quickly. A *review article* summarises and critiques existing literature, and may be narrative or systematic; a *meta-analysis* pools results statistically. A *case report* describes a single case in detail. Other items include letters to the editor, commentaries and editorials (opinion pieces), perspectives, technical notes, conference papers and book reviews.

**Memorise.**

- Original article = new data; review article = synthesis of existing work.

- Short communication = brief, preliminary.

- Editorial and commentary = opinion, not primary research.

**Analyse / traps.** A review article does not report new empirical data.

### 4B.4 Peer Review

**Concept.** Peer review is the evaluation of a manuscript by independent experts before publication, to check validity, originality and significance. The usual path is submission, editorial screening (a weak paper may be “desk rejected”), assessment by reviewers, an editorial decision (accept, minor revision, major revision or reject), revision with a response letter, and proofs. In *single-blind* review the reviewers know the authors but the authors do not know the reviewers; in *double-blind* review both are anonymous; in *open* review identities (and sometimes reports) are public. Its weaknesses are delay, reviewer bias and inconsistency, and the Committee on Publication Ethics (COPE) provides guidelines for editors and reviewers.

**Memorise.**

- Single-blind: authors unaware of reviewers. Double-blind: both anonymous. Open: identities disclosed.

- Decisions: accept, minor revision, major revision, reject.

- COPE: ethics guidance for publishers and editors.

**Analyse / traps.** Double-blind protects against bias toward author identity, but it is not a guarantee of quality.

### 4B.5 Journal Metrics and Identifiers

**Concept.** Identifiers make scholarly output traceable. The *ISSN* (International Standard Serial Number) is an eight-digit number identifying a periodical, with separate numbers for print and electronic forms; the *ISBN* identifies a book and has thirteen digits since 2007; the *DOI* (Digital Object Identifier) is a permanent link to a particular article; and *ORCID* is a persistent sixteen-digit identifier for an individual researcher. Metrics rate journals and authors: the *impact factor* (Clarivate, two-year window), *CiteScore* (Scopus, four-year window), *SJR* (weights citations by the prestige of the citing journal), *SNIP* (normalises for field differences), the *h-index* and *i10-index*. In India the UGC-CARE list (Consortium for Academic and Research Ethics) identifies quality journals.

**Memorise.**

- ISSN = 8 digits, serials. ISBN = 13 digits, books. ORCID = 16 digits, researchers. DOI = permanent article link.

- Impact factor: Clarivate; CiteScore, SJR, SNIP: Scopus-based.

- UGC-CARE: quality-journal list.

**Analyse / traps.** ISSN is for journals; ISBN is for books. DOI points to an item; ORCID points to a person.

### 4B.6 Predatory Journals, Duplicate Submission and Authorship

**Concept.** *Predatory journals* exploit the author-pays model: they charge fees, provide little or no genuine peer review, make false claims about indexing or impact factor, spam researchers with invitations and promise very quick acceptance; the term is linked with Jeffrey Beall’s lists. Safeguards include checking indexing in databases, membership of DOAJ and COPE, and the “Think. Check. Submit.” checklist. *Duplicate submission* means sending the same manuscript to several journals at once; *redundant publication* means publishing the same findings twice; *salami slicing* splits one study into several thin papers. Authorship should reflect substantial contribution: the ICMJE criteria require substantial contribution to the work, drafting or critical revision, final approval and accountability. *Ghost authors* contribute but are not named; *guest or gift authors* are named without contributing.

**Memorise.**

- Predatory journal: fees without real review; false indexing claims.

- Salami slicing: splitting one study into many papers.

- Ghost author: unnamed contributor; gift author: named non-contributor.

**Analyse / traps.** Submitting one manuscript to two journals simultaneously is unethical even if the journals are reputable.

## TOPIC 4C – Styles of Referencing and Citation

### 4C.1 APA Style: Reference List and Editions

**Concept.** APA style, from the American Psychological Association, is used in psychology, education and the social sciences. It uses author–date citation and a *References* list that is alphabetical, double-spaced and has a hanging indent. The current edition is the *seventh* (published in 2019); the sixth appeared in 2009. In a journal reference the article title is in sentence case, the journal title and volume are italicised and the DOI is given as a URL. Book titles are in sentence case and italic, and the publisher’s location is no longer included. The seventh edition lists up to twenty authors and cites works by three or more authors with *et al.* from the first citation.

**Memorise.**

- APA 7th edition: 2019; 6th: 2009.

- Author–date; hanging indent; alphabetical references list.

- Sentence case for article and book titles; italic for journal and book titles.

**Analyse / traps.** APA uses “References”, whereas MLA uses “Works Cited”.

### 4C.2 APA Style: In-text Citations and Quotations

**Concept.** In-text citations give the author and year: (Smith, 2020) or Smith (2020). With two authors both names are given with an ampersand inside brackets: (Smith & Jones, 2020); with three or more, use (Smith et al., 2020). A direct quotation needs a page number, (Smith, 2020, p. 15), and a quotation of fewer than forty words is put in quotation marks within the text, while one of forty words or more is set as an indented *block quotation* without quotation marks. A source cited through another is shown as (Smith, 2000, as cited in Jones, 2015). A work without a date uses n.d.; personal communications are cited only in the text.

**Memorise.**

- Under 40 words: inline quotation marks. 40 words or more: block quote, no marks.

- Three or more authors: et al. from first citation.

- Secondary source: “as cited in”.

**Analyse / traps.** The forty-word threshold for block quotations is APA’s; MLA uses a different rule (more than four lines of prose).

### 4C.3 MLA Style

**Concept.** The Modern Language Association style is used mainly in literature, languages and the humanities. It uses *author–page* in-text citation with no comma and no “p.”, for example (Smith 45), and a *Works Cited* list in alphabetical order with a hanging indent. The ninth edition appeared in 2021. Entries follow a template of core elements: author, title of source, title of container, other contributors, version, number, publisher, publication date and location. Titles of independent works such as books are italicised, and titles of shorter works such as articles are put in quotation marks. A block quotation is used for prose of more than four lines and is indented from the left margin.

**Memorise.**

- MLA: author–page, e.g. (Smith 45); Works Cited; 9th edition (2021).

- Containers: the larger work holding a source (a journal, a website).

- Block quotation: more than four lines of prose.

**Analyse / traps.** No year appears in MLA in-text citations, unlike APA.

### 4C.4 Other Styles: Chicago, Harvard, Vancouver, IEEE, AMA, OSCOLA, Turabian, ACS

**Concept.** Different disciplines prefer different styles. *Chicago* (the *Chicago Manual of Style*; the 17th edition of 2017 is widely cited and a newer edition appeared in 2024) offers a notes-and-bibliography system with footnotes or endnotes, used in history and the humanities, and an author–date system. *Harvard* is a generic author–date style with no single official manual, common in the UK and Australia. *Vancouver* uses numbers in order of first citation and is used in medicine. *IEEE* uses numbers in square brackets, such as \[1\], for engineering and computing. *AMA* uses superscript numbers for medical writing (11th edition, 2020). *OSCOLA* is the Oxford style for legal citation with footnotes. *Turabian* is a student adaptation of Chicago (9th edition, 2018). *ACS* serves chemistry.

| Style              | Discipline                             | Citation form            |
|--------------------|----------------------------------------|--------------------------|
| APA                | Psychology, education, social sciences | Author–date              |
| MLA                | Literature, humanities                 | Author–page              |
| Chicago / Turabian | History, humanities                    | Footnotes or author–date |
| Harvard            | General                                | Author–date              |
| Vancouver, AMA     | Medicine                               | Numbered                 |
| IEEE               | Engineering, computing                 | Numbered in brackets     |
| OSCOLA             | Law                                    | Footnotes                |

**Analyse / traps.** Numbered styles list references in order of citation, not alphabetically.

### 4C.5 Latin Abbreviations: Ibid., Op. cit., Loc. cit.

**Concept.** Footnote conventions use Latin shorthand. *Ibid.* (ibidem) refers to the work cited in the immediately preceding note; *op. cit.* (opere citato) refers to a work already fully cited earlier, with the author’s name and page; *loc. cit.* (loco citato) refers to the same place (same page or passage) in a work already cited; *idem* means the same author; *et al.* means and others; *cf.* (confer) means compare; *q.v.* (quod vide) means which see; *viz.* means namely; *e.g.* (exempli gratia) means for example; *i.e.* (id est) means that is; *passim* means throughout; *ff.* means and the following pages. Modern manuals, including recent Chicago editions, discourage ibid. and op. cit. in favour of shortened citations.

**Memorise.**

- Ibid. = immediately preceding source. Op. cit. = earlier cited work. Loc. cit. = same passage.

- e.g. = for example; i.e. = that is.

**Analyse / traps.** Do not swap e.g. and i.e.: e.g. gives examples, while i.e. restates or clarifies.

### 4C.6 Citation Concepts and Tools

**Concept.** A *citation* acknowledges a source inside the text and is matched by an entry in the reference list. A *primary citation* gives the original source, and a *secondary citation* cites a source through another. Citation indexing, pioneered by Eugene Garfield through the Science Citation Index, allows tracing who cites whom and led to the impact factor. Bibliometrics (the term is credited to Alan Pritchard, 1969) measures publications and citations, with classic laws: *Lotka’s law* on author productivity, *Bradford’s law* on scattering of articles across journals, and *Zipf’s law* on word frequency. *Altmetrics* measure online attention. Reference managers such as Zotero, Mendeley and EndNote store sources and format citations in many styles.

**Memorise.**

- Garfield: citation indexing and impact factor. Lotka: authors. Bradford: journals. Zipf: words.

- Citation Style Language (CSL) underlies many reference-manager styles.

**Analyse / traps.** Citing a source you have not read, through another author, should be shown as a secondary citation.

## TOPIC 4D – Research Paper, Article, Workshop, Seminar, Conference and Symposium

### 4D.1 Seminar, Workshop, Symposium and Colloquium

**Concept.** These academic events differ in purpose and format. A *seminar* is a gathering, usually small, where participants present papers or prepared views on a topic under expert guidance and discuss them. A *workshop* is practice-oriented: participants learn skills or methods by doing, with hands-on exercises. A *symposium* is a formal meeting in which several experts deliver short papers on different aspects of one theme. A *conference* is a larger formal gathering, often periodic, with many papers in parallel sessions and opportunities for networking. A *colloquium* is a more informal academic discussion, often a departmental talk. *Faculty development programmes* and *refresher courses* train teachers; a *webinar* is a web-based seminar.

**Memorise.**

- Seminar = presentation + discussion. Workshop = hands-on skills. Symposium = several experts, one theme. Conference = large, many sessions.

- Colloquium = informal academic talk and discussion.

**Analyse / traps.** If the stem stresses learning by doing, it is a workshop; if it stresses several speakers on aspects of one theme, it is a symposium.

### 4D.2 Conference: Sessions, Proceedings and Presentations

**Concept.** A conference starts with a *call for papers*; authors submit abstracts, which are reviewed and accepted, after which full papers are prepared in a final (camera-ready) form. The *inaugural session* opens the event, the *keynote address* by an invited expert sets the theme, a *plenary session* is attended by all participants with no parallel sessions, *technical sessions* are parallel sessions where papers are presented, and a *poster session* displays work visually with authors standing by to answer questions. The accepted papers are often published as *conference proceedings*, sometimes with an ISBN or ISSN. The event ends with a *valedictory session*.

**Memorise.**

- Keynote: sets theme. Plenary: everyone together. Technical sessions: parallel paper presentations.

- Poster session: visual presentation, authors present at the poster.

- Proceedings: published collection of conference papers.

**Analyse / traps.** Plenary sessions have no parallel sessions, while technical sessions do.

### 4D.3 Roles in Academic Meetings

**Concept.** The *chairperson* presides over a session, introduces speakers, manages time and keeps discussion orderly. The *convener* or organising secretary arranges the event. The *rapporteur* records the proceedings of a session and prepares a summary report with recommendations. A *discussant* is a prepared respondent who comments critically on papers. A *moderator* guides the discussion, particularly in panels and webinars. A *resource person* is an expert invited to deliver a talk or conduct a session, and the *keynote speaker* delivers the principal address.

**Memorise.**

- Chair presides; rapporteur records; discussant critiques; moderator facilitates; convener organises.

**Analyse / traps.** The person who prepares the session report is the rapporteur, not the chair.

### 4D.4 Panel Discussion and Webinar

**Concept.** A *panel discussion* brings a small group of experts, commonly three to six, before an audience to discuss a topic under a moderator; it presents different viewpoints and usually includes audience questions, and it is spontaneous rather than based on read papers. A *round table* gives equal status to all participants around an issue. A *webinar* is a seminar delivered over the internet using video platforms, with interactive features such as chat and polls, and it can be recorded for later viewing. MOOCs, such as those on SWAYAM in India, are open online courses and differ from a single-event webinar.

**Memorise.**

- Panel: experts + moderator + audience interaction.

- Webinar: online seminar; interactive and often recorded.

**Analyse / traps.** A webcast is mainly one-way, while a webinar is interactive.

### 4D.5 Research Papers, Monographs and Book Reviews

**Concept.** A *research paper* presents original findings in a journal or conference. A *monograph* is a detailed scholarly work on a single, specialised subject, usually by one or a few authors. A *treatise* is a systematic, comprehensive discussion of a subject. A *book review* is a critical evaluation of a book, combining a summary with an assessment. An *edited volume* collects chapters by different authors under an editor, and a *festschrift* is a collection of essays in honour of a scholar. Handbooks and encyclopaedias are reference works, and an *annotated bibliography* lists sources with short evaluative notes.

**Memorise.**

- Monograph: one specialised subject in depth. Edited volume: many authors, one editor.

- Book review: summary plus critical assessment.

- Festschrift: essays honouring a scholar.

**Analyse / traps.** A monograph is not a collection of papers by many authors; that is an edited volume.

## TOPIC 5A – Digital Tools for Literature Search

### 5A.1 ICT Basics and Search Techniques

**Concept.** ICT stands for information and communication technology: the computers, networks, software and media used to create, store, retrieve and share information. In research, ICT lets a scholar locate literature, collect and analyse data, manage references and publish. A *search engine* crawls and indexes the open web (Google, Bing), whereas a *database* contains curated, indexed records (Scopus, PubMed), so a database search is usually more precise for scholarly work. Boolean logic, named after George Boole, combines terms: AND narrows, OR broadens, NOT excludes. Truncation (educat\*) retrieves word endings, quotation marks fix an exact phrase, and filters limit by date, language, subject or document type. In Google, a minus sign excludes a word and operators such as site: and filetype: restrict the search.

**Memorise.**

- ICT = information and communication technology.

- Boolean: AND (narrow), OR (broaden), NOT (exclude); quotes = phrase; \* = truncation.

- Google: -word excludes; site: restricts domain; filetype: restricts format.

**Analyse / traps.** Adding more AND terms reduces results; adding more OR terms increases them.

### 5A.2 Scholarly Search Engines and Citation Tracking

**Concept.** *Google Scholar* (launched 2004) is a free search engine for scholarly articles, books, theses and patents; its “Cited by” link lets you follow later work, and it offers alerts, a personal library and author profiles with h-index and i10-index. It is broad but not curated, so quality control is the user’s job. *Semantic Scholar* (Allen Institute for AI) uses AI to rank and summarise papers. *BASE* (Bielefeld) and *CORE* aggregate open-access content. *Dimensions* and *OpenAlex* provide open or freemium bibliographic data, and visual tools such as Connected Papers and ResearchRabbit map citation networks. Microsoft Academic was shut down in 2021. Scopus and Web of Science provide curated citation tracking by subscription.

**Memorise.**

- Google Scholar: free, broad, “Cited by”, alerts, profile metrics.

- Semantic Scholar: AI-based. BASE and CORE: open-access aggregators.

- Scopus and Web of Science: curated, subscription citation databases.

**Analyse / traps.** Wide coverage is not the same as quality: Google Scholar includes non-peer-reviewed items.

### 5A.3 Indian Repositories and Consortia

**Concept.** The *INFLIBNET Centre* at Gandhinagar, an inter-university centre of the UGC, runs most national scholarly infrastructure. *Shodhganga* hosts doctoral theses; *Shodhgangotri* hosts synopses; *Vidwan* is the database of experts; *IRINS* is the Indian Research Information Network System for institutional research profiles; and *ShodhShuddhi* provides plagiarism-detection access to institutions. E-resource consortia included the *UGC-INFONET Digital Library Consortium* (universities), *N-LIST* (colleges) and *INDEST-AICTE* (technical institutions), later brought together as *e-ShodhSindhu*. The *National Digital Library of India (NDLI)*, run from IIT Kharagpur under the Ministry of Education, is a single-window learning repository, and *e-PG Pathshala* offers postgraduate content. From 2025 the *One Nation One Subscription (ONOS)* scheme aims to give government-funded institutions access to thousands of international journals through a single national arrangement.

**Memorise.**

- Shodhganga = theses; Shodhgangotri = synopses; Vidwan = experts; IRINS = research profiles.

- N-LIST = colleges; INFONET = universities; INDEST = technical institutions; e-ShodhSindhu = merged consortium.

- ONOS = One Nation One Subscription (2025 onward).

**Analyse / traps.** NDLI belongs to the Ministry of Education (IIT Kharagpur), not to the UGC’s INFLIBNET.

### 5A.4 Open Access and Preprints

**Concept.** *Open access (OA)* means free online access to scholarly work with permission to reuse under open licences such as Creative Commons. The *gold* route publishes in an OA journal, often funded by an article processing charge; the *green* route deposits a version in a repository, sometimes after an embargo; *hybrid* journals make selected articles OA in a subscription journal; *diamond* or *platinum* OA charges neither author nor reader. The movement rests on the Budapest (2002), Bethesda and Berlin (2003) declarations. The *DOAJ* lists vetted OA journals, *OpenDOAR* lists repositories, and *SHERPA/RoMEO* records publishers’ self-archiving policies. *Preprints* are manuscripts shared before peer review on servers such as arXiv (1991, Paul Ginsparg), bioRxiv, medRxiv, SSRN and ChemRxiv. *Plan S* (cOAlition S, 2018) requires publicly funded research to be published in OA form.

**Memorise.**

- Gold = OA journal; green = repository self-archiving; diamond = no fees for anyone.

- DOAJ = OA journals; OpenDOAR = repositories.

- Preprints are not peer reviewed.

**Analyse / traps.** Free to read is not necessarily free to reuse; licences define reuse rights.

### 5A.5 Alerts and Current Awareness

**Concept.** Current awareness services keep researchers informed of new publications without repeated searching. *Table-of-contents (TOC) alerts* email the contents of new journal issues, *saved-search alerts* notify when new results match a query, *citation alerts* tell you when your paper is cited, and *RSS feeds* deliver headlines to a reader. A library-based version is *Selective Dissemination of Information (SDI)*, which matches new documents to a user’s interest profile. Google Scholar, Scopus, PubMed and publishers’ sites all offer alerts.

**Memorise.**

- SDI = profile-based delivery of new information.

- Alerts: TOC, search, citation; RSS = feed.

**Analyse / traps.** An alert informs you about new items; it does not evaluate their quality.

## TOPIC 5B – Digital Tools for Data Collection

### 5B.1 Online Survey Tools

**Concept.** Online survey platforms let researchers build questionnaires, distribute links and collect responses automatically. *Google Forms* is free and sends results to Google Sheets; *Microsoft Forms* is similar within Microsoft 365; *SurveyMonkey* and *Qualtrics* offer advanced features and academic licences; *LimeSurvey* is open source; *KoboToolbox*, *ODK* and *SurveyCTO* support offline mobile collection in the field; *REDCap* serves clinical and academic research with strong data-security features. Useful functions include skip logic (branching), randomisation of items, validation rules, multilingual forms and export to CSV, Excel or SPSS. Limitations are self-selection bias, coverage bias, low response and duplicate or bot responses.

**Memorise.**

- Google Forms: free, links to Sheets. Qualtrics: advanced. KoboToolbox/ODK: offline field use. REDCap: clinical research.

- Skip logic shows questions according to earlier answers.

**Analyse / traps.** An online survey cannot represent people without internet access.

### 5B.2 Computer-assisted Interviewing and Mobile Data Collection

**Concept.** Computer-assisted methods replace the paper questionnaire. *CAPI* (computer-assisted personal interviewing) means an interviewer asks questions face to face and enters answers on a tablet or laptop; *CATI* is the telephone version; *CAWI* is web-based self-completion; *CASI* is computer-assisted self-interviewing; *IVR* is interactive voice response; *PAPI* is the traditional pen-and-paper personal interview. Built-in skip patterns, range checks, GPS and time stamps reduce entry errors and speed up processing. Large national surveys in India, such as the recent rounds of the National Family Health Survey, have used tablet-based CAPI.

**Memorise.**

- CAPI = in person; CATI = telephone; CAWI = web; PAPI = paper.

- Advantages: fewer errors, faster data, built-in checks, geo-tagging.

**Analyse / traps.** CAPI is interviewer-administered, whereas CAWI is respondent self-administered.

### 5B.3 Remote Interviews, Transcription and Crowdsourcing

**Concept.** Video platforms such as Zoom, Google Meet and Microsoft Teams allow interviews and focus groups across distance, with recording only after informed consent. Transcription may be manual or automatic (Otter, Trint, Whisper-based tools and similar), but automatic transcripts must be checked against the audio for errors, names and dialect. *Crowdsourcing* platforms such as Amazon Mechanical Turk, Prolific and CloudResearch recruit participants quickly for surveys and experiments; researchers must pay fairly and use attention checks to guard against careless or automated responses. Citizen-science sites such as Zooniverse crowdsource data classification by volunteers.

**Memorise.**

- Consent is required before recording.

- MTurk, Prolific: online participant pools. Zooniverse: citizen science.

- Always verify automatic transcripts.

**Analyse / traps.** Crowdsourced samples are convenience samples and may not represent the general population.

### 5B.4 Web Data, APIs and Big Data

**Concept.** Researchers can gather data from the web by *scraping* (extracting content from pages with tools such as BeautifulSoup, Scrapy or Selenium) or, more cleanly, through an *API* (application programming interface) offered by a platform. Other sources are Google Trends (relative search interest), web analytics, social-media analytics, sensors and Internet-of-Things devices, satellite imagery and open government data such as data.gov.in. *Big data* is classically defined by three Vs: volume, velocity and variety, with veracity and value often added, and it is handled with tools such as Hadoop and Spark. Problems include representativeness, platform rules and API limits, and privacy and consent.

**Memorise.**

- Three Vs: volume, velocity, variety.

- API = structured access permitted by the platform; scraping = extracting pages directly.

- Respect robots.txt, terms of service and privacy law.

**Analyse / traps.** Social media users are not a random sample of the population, so conclusions need caution.

## TOPIC 5C – Digital Tools for Data Analysis

### 5C.1 Statistical Software

**Concept.** *SPSS* (Statistical Package for the Social Sciences, now IBM SPSS) is menu-driven and popular in social science and education. *SAS* is strong in business and pharmaceutical analytics; *Stata* in economics and epidemiology; *Minitab* in quality control; *EViews* in time-series econometrics; *AMOS* and *LISREL* in structural equation modelling; *SmartPLS* in PLS-SEM. *R* is a free, open-source language for statistics with thousands of packages on CRAN and the RStudio environment, and *Python* (pandas, NumPy, SciPy, statsmodels, scikit-learn) serves data science. *PSPP* is a free SPSS-like program, and *JASP* and *jamovi* are free graphical programs built on R. *Excel* handles basic statistics through its Data Analysis ToolPak.

**Memorise.**

- SPSS = social sciences; SAS = business/pharma; Stata = economics; R = free and open source.

- PSPP, JASP and jamovi = free alternatives with menus.

- AMOS/LISREL = SEM.

**Analyse / traps.** R and Python need code; SPSS, JASP and jamovi can be used through menus.

### 5C.2 Qualitative Analysis Software and Text Tools

**Concept.** *CAQDAS* (computer-assisted qualitative data analysis software) helps organise, code and retrieve text, audio, video and images. Leading packages are *NVivo*, *ATLAS.ti*, *MAXQDA*, *Dedoose* (web-based and suited to mixed methods), *QDA Miner* and the free *Taguette*. Typical functions are coding (called nodes in NVivo), queries, word-frequency counts, word clouds and matrix comparisons. *Voyant Tools* is a web-based text-analysis tool, *AntConc* supports corpus linguistics, and sentiment analysis and topic modelling (such as LDA) apply computational text mining. The software stores and retrieves codes; the researcher still decides what the codes and themes mean.

**Memorise.**

- CAQDAS: NVivo, ATLAS.ti, MAXQDA, Dedoose, Taguette.

- Voyant Tools: text analysis in the browser; word cloud shows word frequency.

**Analyse / traps.** A word cloud displays frequency, not meaning or themes.

### 5C.3 Visualisation, Data Mining and Computational Tools

**Concept.** *Tableau*, *Power BI* and *Looker Studio* build interactive dashboards; *Datawrapper* and *Flourish* produce web charts; *Gephi* analyses networks; *VOSviewer* (from Leiden University) and *CiteSpace* map bibliometric networks. For mining and machine learning, *WEKA* (University of Waikato), *Orange*, *KNIME* and *RapidMiner* offer visual workflows. *Jupyter Notebook* is an interactive document that combines code, output and text for Python, R and Julia, and *Google Colab* provides Jupyter-style notebooks in the cloud. Python’s Matplotlib, Seaborn and Plotly and R’s ggplot2 are common plotting libraries.

**Memorise.**

- VOSviewer: bibliometric maps. Gephi: networks. WEKA: data mining. Jupyter: code + text notebooks.

- Colab: cloud notebooks. ggplot2: R graphics.

**Analyse / traps.** Tableau and Power BI are for visualisation, not for storing or collecting data.

### 5C.4 Collaboration and Writing Tools: Git, Overleaf and LaTeX

**Concept.** *Git*, created by Linus Torvalds in 2005, is a distributed version-control system that tracks changes to files, and *GitHub* and *GitLab* host Git repositories for collaboration. *LaTeX* (based on TeX by Donald Knuth and developed by Leslie Lamport) is a typesetting system favoured for mathematical and technical documents, with *BibTeX* handling references, and *Overleaf* is an online collaborative LaTeX editor. Google Docs and Microsoft 365 allow real-time co-editing. The *Open Science Framework (OSF)* supports project management and pre-registration, *Zenodo* (from CERN) and *Figshare* issue DOIs for data and code, and *protocols.io* shares methods.

**Memorise.**

- Git: version control (Torvalds, 2005). LaTeX: typesetting. Overleaf: online LaTeX. OSF: open-science projects and pre-registration.

- Zenodo and Figshare: DOIs for datasets.

**Analyse / traps.** Git tracks versions of files; it is not a word processor.

### 5C.5 GIS and Data Cleaning

**Concept.** A *Geographic Information System (GIS)* captures, stores, analyses and displays spatial data in layers. *ArcGIS* (Esri) is commercial, *QGIS* is free and open source, and *Google Earth Engine* handles large satellite archives; in India *Bhuvan* is ISRO’s geoportal. Data cleaning detects and corrects errors, duplicates, inconsistent labels, missing values and outliers before analysis. *OpenRefine* (formerly Google Refine) clusters and standardises messy data, and Excel Power Query and R’s tidyverse also support data wrangling. Cleaning decisions must be documented so the analysis stays reproducible.

**Memorise.**

- QGIS = free GIS; ArcGIS = commercial; Bhuvan = ISRO.

- OpenRefine = data cleaning.

**Analyse / traps.** Cleaning is a step before analysis, not after interpretation.

## TOPIC 5D – Online Databases and Repositories

### 5D.1 Citation and Indexing Databases

**Concept.** *Scopus* (Elsevier, 2004) is a large abstract and citation database that supplies CiteScore, SJR, SNIP and author profiles. *Web of Science* (Clarivate, formerly the Institute for Scientific Information) has the Core Collection of SCIE, SSCI, AHCI and the Emerging Sources Citation Index (ESCI), and publishes the *Journal Citation Reports* with the impact factor. *Google Scholar* is free but not curated. *Dimensions* links publications with grants and patents. In India, INFLIBNET’s IndCat is a union catalogue of library holdings, and the UGC-CARE list (Consortium for Academic and Research Ethics, introduced around 2018–19) was created to identify quality journals, though UGC’s journal-quality policy has been revised, so check the current position.

**Memorise.**

- Scopus = Elsevier; Web of Science = Clarivate; impact factor = JCR.

- WoS Core Collection: SCIE, SSCI, AHCI, ESCI.

- Google Scholar = free, not curated.

**Analyse / traps.** CiteScore belongs to Scopus; impact factor belongs to Journal Citation Reports.

### 5D.2 Subject-specific Databases and Preprint Servers

**Concept.** Subject databases give deeper coverage in a field. *PubMed/MEDLINE* (National Library of Medicine) covers biomedicine; *ERIC* covers education; *PsycINFO* (APA) covers psychology; *EconLit* covers economics; *IEEE Xplore* covers engineering and electronics; the *ACM Digital Library* covers computing; *MathSciNet* covers mathematics; *SciFinder* covers chemistry; and the *Cochrane Library* holds systematic reviews in health. *JSTOR* is an archive of humanities and social science journals with older issues. *ProQuest Dissertations and Theses* collects theses, and *EBSCOhost* and *ProQuest* are platforms hosting many databases. *arXiv* (physics, mathematics, computer science), *SSRN* (social sciences) and *RePEc* (economics) provide preprints and working papers.

**Memorise.**

- PubMed = medicine; ERIC = education; PsycINFO = psychology; EconLit = economics; JSTOR = archive of journals.

- IEEE Xplore = engineering; ACM DL = computing; arXiv = physics, maths, CS preprints.

**Analyse / traps.** EBSCOhost is a platform that hosts databases, not itself a subject database.

### 5D.3 Identifiers, Quality Lists and Access Initiatives

**Concept.** *ORCID* gives each researcher a persistent identifier that distinguishes them from others with the same name and links to their works. Other profiles are Web of Science ResearcherID, Scopus Author ID, Google Scholar profile and INFLIBNET’s Vidwan. *Crossref* is the main registration agency for scholarly DOIs and *DataCite* registers DOIs for datasets. The *Directory of Open Access Journals* vets OA journals, and the UGC-CARE list was India’s quality list. Access initiatives include *e-ShodhSindhu*, *NDLI*, *IRINS* and, from 2025, *ONOS* (One Nation One Subscription), which seeks a single national subscription to major international journals for public institutions.

**Memorise.**

- ORCID = researcher ID. Crossref = DOI registration for scholarly content. DataCite = DOIs for data.

- ONOS = national subscription scheme; Vidwan = Indian experts database.

**Analyse / traps.** ORCID identifies a person; DOI identifies a work.

### 5D.4 Data Repositories and FAIR Principles

**Concept.** Research data repositories store datasets and make them citable. Examples are *Zenodo*, *Figshare*, *Dryad*, *Harvard Dataverse*, *OSF*, *Mendeley Data* and *ICPSR* for social science data; in India the Open Government Data platform is *data.gov.in*, working under the National Data Sharing and Accessibility Policy of 2012. The *FAIR principles* (Wilkinson and colleagues, 2016) say that data should be Findable, Accessible, Interoperable and Reusable. Good practice includes a data management plan, rich metadata (for example Dublin Core), a persistent identifier such as a DOI, and a clear licence (CC0 or CC BY).

**Memorise.**

- FAIR = Findable, Accessible, Interoperable, Reusable (2016).

- Data repositories: Zenodo, Figshare, Dryad, Dataverse, ICPSR.

- India: data.gov.in; NDSAP 2012.

**Analyse / traps.** FAIR does not always mean open; data can be FAIR yet access-controlled.

### 5D.5 Research Metrics

**Concept.** Metrics operate at three levels. *Journal-level* measures are the impact factor, CiteScore, SJR, SNIP and Eigenfactor. *Author-level* measures include the h-index (Hirsch, 2005), the g-index (Leo Egghe) and the i10-index (Google Scholar). *Article-level* measures include citation counts, field-weighted citation impact and *altmetrics* (online attention such as news, social media and policy mentions, shown by Altmetric and PlumX). Misuse of metrics is criticised in the San Francisco Declaration on Research Assessment (DORA, 2012) and the Leiden Manifesto (2015), which urge that research be judged on its content and not on journal metrics alone.

**Memorise.**

- h-index: Hirsch, 2005. i10: Google Scholar. g-index: Egghe.

- DORA (2012) and Leiden Manifesto (2015): responsible use of metrics.

- Altmetrics: online attention.

**Analyse / traps.** A high journal impact factor does not guarantee that a given article is good.

## TOPIC 5E – Reference Managers

### 5E.1 Reference Manager Software

**Concept.** A reference manager collects bibliographic records and PDFs, organises them in a library and formats citations and bibliographies automatically. *Zotero* is free and open source, developed at George Mason University’s Center for History and New Media (2006), and captures records through a browser connector. *Mendeley* (owned by Elsevier) combines reference management with PDF reading and annotation. *EndNote* (Clarivate) is a commercial desktop-based manager. *JabRef* works natively with BibTeX, *Paperpile* integrates with Google Docs, *RefWorks* is from ProQuest and *Citavi* is popular in Europe. They save time and reduce citation errors, but the captured metadata must still be checked.

**Memorise.**

- Zotero: free, open source. Mendeley: Elsevier. EndNote: Clarivate, commercial. JabRef: BibTeX.

- Functions: capture, store, organise, cite, format bibliography.

**Analyse / traps.** A reference manager does not guarantee correct citations; auto-captured data can contain errors.

### 5E.2 Features, Formats and Plug-ins

**Concept.** The *cite while you write* feature inserts citations into Word, LibreOffice or Google Docs through a plug-in and builds the bibliography in a chosen style. Styles are stored in *Citation Style Language (CSL)* files, which makes it possible to switch between APA, MLA, Chicago, Vancouver and others in one click. Common exchange formats are *RIS*, *BibTeX (.bib)* and *EndNote XML*. Further features include duplicate detection, full-text search, annotation of PDFs, tagging, group libraries for collaboration and synchronisation across devices.

**Memorise.**

- Cite While You Write (CWYW) = plug-in citing.

- Formats: RIS, BibTeX, EndNote XML; styles: CSL.

- Group libraries enable shared reading lists.

**Analyse / traps.** Changing the style in a reference manager changes only the formatting, not the accuracy of the underlying data.

## TOPIC 5F – Plagiarism-checking Tools

### 5F.1 Plagiarism Detection Software

**Concept.** Detection software compares a submitted text with large databases of web pages, journals and previously submitted papers, then highlights matching passages and produces a similarity report. *Turnitin* is widely used for student work; *iThenticate*, also from Turnitin, is used by researchers and publishers and powers *Crossref Similarity Check*. *Urkund* (now part of Turnitin as Ouriginal) was supplied to Indian institutions through INFLIBNET’s *ShodhShuddhi* initiative, and India’s *DrillBit* has also been adopted, so check the current vendor. *Grammarly*, *Copyscape*, *Unicheck* and *PlagScan* offer other checks. The software finds matching text; it does not decide whether plagiarism has occurred, which needs human judgement.

**Memorise.**

- Turnitin: students. iThenticate: researchers and publishers. ShodhShuddhi: INFLIBNET’s service for Indian institutions.

- Output: similarity report highlighting matches.

**Analyse / traps.** A similarity report is evidence for review, not a verdict.

### 5F.2 Similarity Index and Limitations of Checkers

**Concept.** The *similarity index* is the percentage of the text that matches other sources; it is not the same as a plagiarism percentage, because matches can be properly quoted or cited. Under the UGC 2018 regulations, matches in quoted material with attribution, references, bibliography, table of contents, preface, acknowledgements, and generic terms, laws, standard symbols and common knowledge are excluded from the similarity count. Checkers give *false positives* (common phrases, correctly cited text) and *false negatives*: paraphrased plagiarism, translated plagiarism, copied ideas and figures, and sources absent from their database are missed. Detectors for AI-generated text are unreliable and should not be used as sole proof.

**Memorise.**

- Similarity index ≠ plagiarism percentage.

- Excluded in UGC 2018 counting: quotes with attribution, references, bibliography, preface, acknowledgements, common knowledge.

- Software misses paraphrase, translation and idea plagiarism.

**Analyse / traps.** A zero similarity index does not prove originality, and a high one does not prove plagiarism.

### 5F.3 Avoiding Plagiarism and Use of Generative AI

**Concept.** To avoid plagiarism, acknowledge every source for words, data, figures and ideas; put exact words in quotation marks; paraphrase by restating in your own structure and still cite; keep careful notes that separate your ideas from sources; use a reference manager; run a self-check before submission; and obtain permission for reused material, including your own earlier published text. Generative AI tools can produce fluent text but may invent references and reproduce source material, so the researcher remains responsible for accuracy and originality. Journals and bodies such as COPE and ICMJE hold that an AI tool cannot be an author, and its use should be disclosed as the journal or institution requires. Presenting AI-written text as your own work is treated as misconduct in many policies.

**Memorise.**

- AI cannot be listed as an author; disclose AI assistance.

- Verify every AI-supplied reference against the source.

- Paraphrasing still needs citation.

**Analyse / traps.** Changing a few words of a source is patchwriting, not paraphrasing, and still counts as plagiarism.

## TOPIC 6A – Ethical Principles in Research

### 6A.1 Meaning and Purpose of Research Ethics

**Concept.** Ethics is the study of moral principles that distinguish right from wrong conduct, and research ethics applies them to the design, conduct and reporting of research. It protects the dignity, rights, safety and privacy of participants, protects animals and the environment, and safeguards the integrity of knowledge so that the public can trust science. David Resnik lists its purposes as promoting the aims of research (truth and avoidance of error), supporting values needed for collaboration (trust, fairness, accountability), keeping researchers answerable to the public, and upholding wider moral and social values such as human rights, animal welfare and legality. Research ethics applies to all fields, not just medicine, and it is wider than the law: an act may be legal and still be unethical. *Responsible conduct of research* rests on honesty, objectivity, carefulness, openness, confidentiality and respect for colleagues and intellectual property.

**Memorise.**

- Research ethics covers participants, animals, data, authorship and the public interest.

- Core integrity values: honesty, objectivity, carefulness, openness, accountability.

- Ethics ≠ law; ethics applies in social sciences and humanities as well.

**Analyse / traps.** A statement that ethics applies only to medical or clinical research is wrong.

### 6A.2 Historical Codes: Nuremberg, Helsinki and Belmont

**Concept.** Modern research ethics grew from abuses. The *Nuremberg Code* (1947) came from the trial of Nazi doctors; its first principle is that the voluntary consent of the human subject is absolutely essential. The *Declaration of Helsinki*, adopted by the World Medical Association in 1964 and revised several times since, is the central statement for medical research and requires ethics committee review, informed consent and a favourable risk–benefit balance. The *Tuskegee Syphilis Study* (1932–1972), in which poor Black men in Alabama were left untreated to observe the disease, led to the US National Research Act (1974) and then to the *Belmont Report* (1979) with its three principles: respect for persons, beneficence and justice. The *CIOMS guidelines* (from 1982, revised in 2016) apply these principles internationally, and ICH-GCP governs clinical trials.

**Memorise.**

- Nuremberg 1947 (voluntary consent); Helsinki 1964 (WMA); Belmont 1979 (three principles).

- Tuskegee: 1932–72; led to the National Research Act and Belmont Report.

- CIOMS: international ethical guidelines.

**Analyse / traps.** Keep the order: Nuremberg (1947) → Helsinki (1964) → Belmont (1979).

### 6A.3 Beneficence, Non-maleficence and Justice

**Concept.** The Belmont Report’s principles are *respect for persons* (autonomy and protection of those with diminished autonomy, giving rise to informed consent), *beneficence* (maximise benefits and minimise possible harm) and *justice* (fair distribution of the burdens and benefits of research and fair selection of participants). Beauchamp and Childress, in *Principles of Biomedical Ethics*, name four principles: autonomy, beneficence, *non-maleficence* (do no harm, *primum non nocere*) and justice. A risk–benefit assessment must show that risks are justified by the expected benefit. The Tuskegee study violated all three Belmont principles, notably justice, because a vulnerable group bore the burden.

**Memorise.**

- Belmont: respect for persons, beneficence, justice.

- Beauchamp and Childress: autonomy, beneficence, non-maleficence, justice.

- Justice = fair selection and fair sharing of burdens and benefits.

**Analyse / traps.** Belmont has three principles and Beauchamp–Childress has four; non-maleficence is the extra one.

### 6A.4 Ethics Committees and ICMR Guidelines

**Concept.** Before a study involving people starts, an independent committee reviews the protocol, the consent documents and the safeguards: an *Institutional Ethics Committee (IEC)* in India, an *Institutional Review Board (IRB)* in the United States and a *Research Ethics Committee (REC)* in the UK. Such committees are multidisciplinary and include scientists, a clinician, a legal expert, a social scientist and a lay member, and they monitor studies after approval. In India the *ICMR National Ethical Guidelines for Biomedical and Health Research Involving Human Participants (2017)* set the standards, with general principles such as essentiality, voluntariness, non-exploitation, social responsibility, privacy and confidentiality, risk minimisation, professional competence, maximisation of benefit, transparency and accountability, and totality of responsibility. Clinical trials are governed by the New Drugs and Clinical Trials Rules, 2019, and registered in the Clinical Trials Registry of India (CTRI).

**Memorise.**

- IEC (India), IRB (US), REC (UK): independent protocol review.

- ICMR National Ethical Guidelines: 2017. CTRI: trial registry.

- Committee includes a lay person and a legal expert.

**Analyse / traps.** Ethics approval must come before data collection, not after.

### 6A.5 Vulnerable Participants, Risk and Deception

**Concept.** Vulnerable groups include children, prisoners, pregnant women, people with mental illness or cognitive impairment, the elderly, the economically or educationally disadvantaged, refugees, and people in dependent relationships such as students or employees. They need extra safeguards, such as parental consent plus the child’s assent, or a legally authorised representative. *Minimal risk* means harm no greater than in ordinary daily life. *Deception* is acceptable only when the study has real value, no non-deceptive alternative exists, the risk is minimal and participants are *debriefed* afterwards. Classic cases are Milgram’s obedience experiments (deception and distress), Zimbardo’s Stanford prison experiment (psychological harm) and Humphreys’ covert observation study, *Tearoom Trade*.

**Memorise.**

- Minimal risk = no greater than daily life.

- Deception needs justification, minimal risk and debriefing.

- Right to withdraw at any time without penalty.

**Analyse / traps.** Consent from a parent does not replace the child’s own assent, where the child is able to give it.

### 6A.6 Conflict of Interest

**Concept.** A conflict of interest arises when a researcher’s personal, financial or professional interest could, or could appear to, compromise judgement or objectivity. Financial examples are sponsor funding, consultancy fees, shares and patents; non-financial examples include rivalry, close personal relationships and academic competition. Conflicts are not always wrong, but they must be managed: disclose them to the institution, ethics committee, funder and journal; step aside from decisions where they apply (recusal); and arrange independent oversight. Reviewers and editors with a conflict should decline to handle the paper.

**Memorise.**

- Manage conflict by disclosure, recusal and oversight.

- Journals require a conflict-of-interest statement from authors.

**Analyse / traps.** The perceived conflict matters as well as the actual one; hiding a conflict is the real offence.

### 6A.7 Research Misconduct: Fabrication, Falsification and Plagiarism

**Concept.** Research misconduct is conventionally summed up as FFP. *Fabrication* is making up data or results; *falsification* is manipulating materials, equipment, processes or data, or changing or omitting results so that the record is not accurate; *plagiarism* is taking another’s ideas, words or results without credit. Honest error and genuine differences of opinion are not misconduct. Related *questionable research practices* are p-hacking, HARKing (hypothesising after results are known), selective reporting and cherry-picking. Terms sometimes used for data abuse are *cooking* (selecting data that fit), *trimming* (smoothing outliers) and *forging*. Consequences range from retraction to dismissal.

**Memorise.**

- FFP = fabrication, falsification, plagiarism.

- HARKing = presenting a post-hoc hypothesis as if it were pre-planned.

- Honest error ≠ misconduct.

**Analyse / traps.** Fabrication invents data that never existed; falsification alters data that did exist.

### 6A.8 Authorship and Publication Ethics

**Concept.** Authorship credit should follow contribution and responsibility. The ICMJE (Vancouver) criteria require all four: substantial contribution to conception, design, data or analysis; drafting or critically revising the work; final approval of the version published; and agreement to be accountable for the work. Contributors who do not qualify are thanked in the acknowledgements. *Ghost authorship* hides a real contributor; *guest or gift (honorary) authorship* credits someone who did not contribute. The *corresponding author* handles communication with the journal. Publication ethics also forbids duplicate and redundant publication, salami slicing, image manipulation and breach of confidentiality in peer review, and the *CRediT* taxonomy describes fourteen contributor roles. COPE guides editors on dealing with misconduct.

**Memorise.**

- ICMJE: four criteria, all required.

- Ghost = unnamed contributor; guest or gift = named without contributing.

- CRediT: 14 contributor roles; COPE: publication ethics body.

**Analyse / traps.** Holding a senior position or providing funding does not by itself qualify for authorship.

### 6A.9 Whistleblowing and Animal Research Ethics

**Concept.** A *whistleblower* reports suspected misconduct in good faith through proper channels; institutions must protect them from retaliation and keep their identity confidential as far as possible. In India the Whistle Blowers Protection Act, 2014 covers disclosures about public servants. For animal research the guiding framework is the *3Rs* of Russell and Burch (1959): *Replacement* (use non-animal methods where possible), *Reduction* (use the fewest animals) and *Refinement* (minimise suffering). The *Five Freedoms* come from the Brambell Report (1965). In India the *CPCSEA* (Committee for the Purpose of Control and Supervision of Experiments on Animals), set up under the Prevention of Cruelty to Animals Act, 1960, regulates animal experiments through Institutional Animal Ethics Committees, and the *ARRIVE* guidelines govern reporting of animal studies.

**Memorise.**

- 3Rs: Replacement, Reduction, Refinement (Russell and Burch, 1959).

- CPCSEA and IAEC: India’s animal-experiment oversight.

- Whistleblower must act in good faith and be protected.

**Analyse / traps.** Reduction means fewer animals, not fewer experiments of poor design; poor design wastes animals.

### 6A.10 Research Integrity, Open Science and Questionable Practices

**Concept.** Research integrity statements set shared standards. The *Singapore Statement* (2010) names four principles: honesty, accountability, professional courtesy and fairness, and good stewardship; the *Montreal Statement* (2013) addresses collaboration across borders; the *European Code of Conduct for Research Integrity* lists reliability, honesty, respect and accountability. Open-science practices support integrity: sharing data and materials, pre-registration, registered reports and replication. *Publication bias* (the *file drawer problem*, a term from Robert Rosenthal) is the tendency to publish only significant results. The *reproducibility crisis* refers to the low rate at which many published findings can be replicated. *Replicability* means getting similar results with new data; *reproducibility* means getting the same results from the same data and code.

**Memorise.**

- Singapore Statement 2010: honesty, accountability, courtesy and fairness, stewardship.

- File drawer problem = unpublished negative results.

- Reproducibility = same data and code; replicability = new data.

**Analyse / traps.** Do not interchange reproducibility and replicability; the data source is what differs.

## TOPIC 6B – Plagiarism and Academic Integrity

### 6B.1 Meaning and Types of Plagiarism

**Concept.** The word comes from the Latin *plagiarius*, a kidnapper. Plagiarism is presenting another person’s words, ideas, data or creative work as one’s own, without proper acknowledgement. The UGC regulations of 2018 describe it as taking someone else’s work or ideas and passing them off as one’s own. Forms include *verbatim* copying, *mosaic plagiarism* or *patchwriting* (stitching together phrases from sources with small changes), *paraphrasing without credit*, *self-plagiarism* (reusing one’s own published text without disclosure), *translation plagiarism*, *idea plagiarism*, *plagiarism of data or images*, *secondary-source plagiarism* (citing a source one has not read), and *contract cheating or ghostwriting*. It can be intentional or accidental, but carelessness is not a defence. Presenting a junior colleague’s results as one’s own is also plagiarism. Common knowledge need not be cited.

**Memorise.**

- Plagiarism = using others’ work without credit; applies to words, ideas, data, images.

- Mosaic/patchwriting: stitched phrases with minor changes.

- Self-plagiarism: reusing your own published text without disclosure.

**Analyse / traps.** Changing a few words in a copied sentence is still plagiarism; accidental plagiarism is still plagiarism.

### 6B.2 Paraphrasing, Quotation and Citation Practice

**Concept.** There are three legitimate ways to use a source: *quote* (exact words in quotation marks, with citation and page), *paraphrase* (restate the idea in your own words and sentence structure, still with citation) and *summarise* (condense the main points, with citation). Direct quotations should be rare and short; long ones are set as block quotes. Statistics, theories, findings, figures and unusual ideas must be cited, while widely known facts do not need citation. Keep notes that clearly separate your words from source words, cite as you write, and match every in-text citation to a reference-list entry. A paraphrase that keeps the original structure and merely swaps synonyms is inadequate.

**Memorise.**

- Quote: exact words + quotation marks + citation. Paraphrase: new wording + citation.

- Even a perfect paraphrase needs a citation.

**Analyse / traps.** Removing quotation marks while keeping the wording is plagiarism even if a citation is given.

### 6B.3 Copyright and Licences

**Concept.** In India copyright is governed by the Copyright Act, 1957 (amended in 2012). It arises automatically when an original literary, dramatic, musical or artistic work, film or sound recording is created; registration is optional. It protects the expression of ideas, not the ideas themselves. For literary works the term is the author’s life plus sixty years. Under *fair dealing* (Section 52) limited use for private study, research, criticism, review, reporting and teaching is allowed, a narrower idea than the US doctrine of fair use. Authors also hold moral rights of paternity and integrity. *Creative Commons* licences (CC BY, BY-SA, BY-NC, BY-ND, CC0) state what reuse is allowed. Plagiarism is an ethical offence against academic norms, whereas infringement is a legal offence; one can plagiarise public-domain work, which is free from copyright, without breaking copyright law.

**Memorise.**

- Copyright Act, 1957; term: life + 60 years for literary works; arises without registration.

- Fair dealing (Section 52): research, criticism, review, teaching.

- CC BY requires attribution; CC0 waives rights.

**Analyse / traps.** Copyright protects expression, not ideas; plagiarism and copyright infringement are different wrongs.

### 6B.4 UGC 2018 Plagiarism Regulations

**Concept.** The *UGC (Promotion of Academic Integrity and Prevention of Plagiarism in Higher Educational Institutions) Regulations, 2018* apply to students, researchers, faculty and staff. They require an undertaking of originality, use of plagiarism-detection software, and institutional bodies for academic integrity (a Departmental Academic Integrity Panel and an Institutional Academic Integrity Panel). They exclude from similarity counting quotations with attribution, references and bibliography, table of contents, preface and acknowledgements, and generic terms, laws, standard symbols and standards. Penalties depend on the similarity level. For students: *Level 0* up to 10% (no penalty); *Level 1* above 10% to 40% (a revised script must be submitted within six months); *Level 2* above 40% to 60% (debarred from submitting a revised script for one year); *Level 3* above 60% (registration for that programme cancelled). For faculty and staff the levels lead to withdrawal of the manuscript and, at higher levels, denial of increments and exclusion from supervision, and repeat offences attract severe action.

| Level | Similarity | Student consequence                                 |
|-------|------------|-----------------------------------------------------|
| 0     | Up to 10%  | None                                                |
| 1     | 10–40%     | Revise within six months                            |
| 2     | 40–60%     | Barred from submitting revised version for one year |
| 3     | Above 60%  | Registration cancelled                              |

**Memorise.**

- Levels 0–3 at 10%, 40%, 60% thresholds.

- Exclusions: quotes with attribution, references, preface, acknowledgements, common knowledge.

- Bodies: DAIP and IAIP.

**Analyse / traps.** The thresholds define where each level begins; check whether the question says “above” or “up to”.

### 6B.5 Academic Integrity Values and Misconduct

**Concept.** The International Center for Academic Integrity names the fundamental values of academic integrity as honesty, trust, fairness, respect, responsibility and, added later, courage. Breaches include *cheating* (unauthorised aid), *collusion* (unauthorised collaboration), *contract cheating* (paying someone to produce work, as through essay mills), *ghostwriting*, impersonation, fabrication of data or citations, *facilitating* others’ dishonesty and *multiple submission* of the same work for credit more than once. Institutions respond with honour codes, education, clear policies and fair procedures.

**Memorise.**

- ICAI values: honesty, trust, fairness, respect, responsibility, courage.

- Contract cheating = paying for work; collusion = improper collaboration.

**Analyse / traps.** Collaboration that the instructor allows is not collusion; unauthorised collaboration is.

### 6B.6 Questionable Research and Publishing Practices

**Concept.** Beyond plain plagiarism, several practices damage integrity. *Image manipulation* (splicing, duplicating or selectively adjusting blots and micrographs) distorts evidence. *Padding the reference list* adds irrelevant sources to look scholarly. *Coercive citation* is when editors or reviewers pressure authors to cite their journal or their own work to raise metrics, and *citation cartels* are groups citing each other to inflate counts; *excessive self-citation* has a similar effect. *Paper mills* are commercial services that sell fabricated papers or authorship. *Peer-review rings* use fake reviewers, and *predatory journals* accept papers for a fee without genuine review. Such journals typically promise very quick acceptance and make false claims about indexing.

**Memorise.**

- Coercive citation: demanded by editors or reviewers. Paper mill: sells papers or authorship.

- Predatory journal: fee, no real review, quick acceptance, false claims.

- Image manipulation is misconduct, not mere “enhancement”.

**Analyse / traps.** Quick acceptance guaranteed within days is a red flag, not a feature.

### 6B.7 Authorship and Acknowledgement Issues

**Concept.** Authorship disputes are best prevented by agreeing contributions and author order at the start of the project against the ICMJE criteria and institutional rules. When a dispute arises, the first step is a discussion among the authors using those criteria, then referral to the institution or the journal, whose editor may consult COPE guidance. People who contributed but do not meet authorship criteria (technical help, language editing, general supervision, funding access) are named in *acknowledgements* with their permission, and funding sources are stated. The *CRediT* roles are conceptualisation, data curation, formal analysis, funding acquisition, investigation, methodology, project administration, resources, software, supervision, validation, visualisation, writing the original draft, and writing (review and editing).

**Memorise.**

- First step in an authorship dispute: discuss within the team against agreed criteria.

- Acknowledge non-author contributors and funders.

- CRediT: 14 roles.

**Analyse / traps.** Honorary authorship damages credibility; acknowledgement is the correct recognition for minor help.

### 6B.8 Consequences and Notable Cases

**Concept.** Consequences of misconduct include retraction of papers, rejection or withdrawal of a thesis, cancellation of registration or degree, loss of funding and employment, debarment, reputational damage and sometimes legal action. Well-known cases include Hwang Woo-suk (fabricated stem-cell papers in *Science*, 2005), Diederik Stapel (fabricated data in many social-psychology papers, 2011), Jan Hendrik Schön (fabricated physics results at Bell Labs, 2002), Andrew Wakefield (the retracted 1998 *Lancet* paper linking MMR vaccine to autism), Haruko Obokata (retracted STAP cell papers, 2014) and the Piltdown Man hoax (exposed in 1953). *Retraction Watch*, founded in 2010, tracks retractions, and COPE gives retraction guidelines.

**Memorise.**

- Hwang: stem cells. Stapel: social psychology. Schön: physics. Wakefield: MMR and autism. Obokata: STAP.

- Retraction Watch: database of retractions.

**Analyse / traps.** A retraction does not always mean fraud; it can follow honest error, but the reason must be stated.

### 6B.9 Plagiarism Detection and AI-related Issues

**Concept.** Detection combines software (see 5F) with human judgement: sudden shifts in style, anachronisms, inconsistent terminology and unusual fluency can signal copying. Software cannot catch everything, such as translated text, paraphrase and unavailable sources. Generative AI adds new problems: tools can invent references, reproduce source text and produce paraphrase that evades checkers, and “tortured phrases” from paraphrasing tools can reveal machine-spun text. Journals and COPE advise that AI cannot be an author, that its use must be disclosed, and that the human authors remain responsible for accuracy. AI-text detectors are unreliable and should not be used alone to accuse anyone.

**Memorise.**

- AI cannot be an author; disclose use; humans are accountable.

- Check every AI-generated citation against a real source.

**Analyse / traps.** An AI-detection score is not proof of misconduct.

## TOPIC 6C – Informed Consent, Confidentiality and Honesty in Reporting Data

### 6C.1 Informed Consent: Meaning, Elements and Rights

**Concept.** Informed consent is a participant’s voluntary agreement to take part after being told what the research involves. It is a *process*, not just a signature on a form. Its elements are *disclosure* (purpose, procedures, duration, risks, benefits, alternatives, confidentiality arrangements and whom to contact), *comprehension* (language and wording the person understands, in their own language), *voluntariness* (no coercion or undue influence), *competence* (capacity to decide) and *authorisation* (signature, thumbprint or witnessed agreement). The information is given in a participant information sheet and recorded in a consent form. Participants may withdraw at any time without penalty or loss of benefits, and the form must not ask them to give up legal rights. In India the consent process in regulatory clinical trials is audio-visually recorded, and participants who suffer trial-related injury are entitled to free medical care and compensation under the New Drugs and Clinical Trials Rules, 2019.

**Memorise.**

- Elements: disclosure, comprehension, voluntariness, competence, authorisation.

- Consent is a continuing process; withdrawal is allowed at any time without penalty.

- Nuremberg Code: voluntary consent is essential.

**Analyse / traps.** A signed form obtained without real understanding is not valid informed consent.

### 6C.2 Assent, Capacity and Special Populations

**Concept.** Where a person cannot legally consent, a *legally authorised representative* (parent, guardian or other) gives permission, and the participant’s own *assent* is sought as far as they can understand. In the ICMR 2017 guidelines, children aged about 7 to 12 years give oral assent and those aged about 12 to 18 years give written assent, in addition to parental consent. For people who cannot read, an impartial witness is present during the consent process. Participants with mental illness, pregnant women, prisoners, students and employees need extra protection because of limited autonomy or power relationships. When new information emerges that may affect willingness to continue, participants are informed and consent is renewed.

**Memorise.**

- Child: parental consent + child assent. Illiterate participant: impartial witness.

- Authorised representative consents for those lacking capacity.

- Re-consent when significant new information appears.

**Analyse / traps.** Assent is not a legal substitute for consent; it adds respect for the child’s own wishes.

### 6C.3 Waiver, Inducement and Coercion

**Concept.** An ethics committee may *waive* informed consent for minimal-risk studies when obtaining consent is impracticable, such as research on anonymised records. *Reasonable compensation* for time, travel and inconvenience is acceptable, but a payment so large that it clouds judgement is *undue inducement*. *Coercion* is the threat of harm or penalty for refusing, as when a teacher requires students to take part or a doctor implies that care depends on participation. *Exploitation* means taking unfair advantage of vulnerable people. The *therapeutic misconception* occurs when participants wrongly believe that the research is designed for their personal benefit. In community studies, permission from community leaders does not replace individual consent.

**Memorise.**

- Waiver: minimal risk + impracticable + committee approval.

- Undue inducement = excessive reward; coercion = threat or penalty.

- Therapeutic misconception: mistaking research for treatment.

**Analyse / traps.** Paying participants is not unethical in itself; the amount and the pressure created are what matter.

### 6C.4 Anonymity, Confidentiality and Privacy

**Concept.** *Anonymity* means that nobody, including the researcher, can link data to a participant. *Confidentiality* means that the researcher knows the identity but promises not to disclose it. *Privacy* is a person’s right to control access to personal information and to be free from intrusion. Techniques include removing direct identifiers, assigning codes (pseudonymisation) with the key stored separately, encryption, password protection, locked storage and limited access. Small samples or unusual details can make a person re-identifiable even without a name, so reports should aggregate data and alter identifying details. Confidentiality has limits: researchers may have to disclose when someone is at risk of serious harm or when the law requires it, and participants should be told so in advance. In focus groups, confidentiality cannot be guaranteed because other participants hear the discussion, so ground rules are agreed.

**Memorise.**

- Anonymity: identity unknown even to the researcher. Confidentiality: identity known but protected.

- Pseudonymisation: codes with a separate key.

- Focus groups: confidentiality cannot be fully guaranteed.

**Analyse / traps.** A study using names on the questionnaire cannot call itself anonymous, though it may be confidential.

### 6C.5 Data Protection Laws and Use of Data

**Concept.** In India the *Digital Personal Data Protection Act, 2023* governs the processing of digital personal data. It speaks of the *data principal* (the individual) and the *data fiduciary* (the entity deciding the purpose and means of processing), requires notice and consent for stated purposes, limits use to those purposes, and gives principals rights to access, correction and erasure. It provides conditional exemptions for research, archiving and statistical use when safeguards are in place, and it requires verifiable parental consent for children; check the status of its implementing rules. Earlier, the Information Technology Act, 2000 and its rules covered sensitive data. Abroad, the EU GDPR (2018) and US HIPAA (1996, for health information) are leading models. Secondary use of data should stay within the scope of the original consent or be approved by an ethics committee, and publicly posted online material still raises privacy and terms-of-service questions.

**Memorise.**

- DPDP Act 2023: data principal (individual), data fiduciary (processor of purpose).

- GDPR 2018 (EU); HIPAA 1996 (US health data).

- Reuse of data must respect the original consent.

**Analyse / traps.** “Public” online posts are not automatically free to use without ethical review.

### 6C.6 Honest Reporting and Reporting Bias

**Concept.** Honest reporting means describing the methods, the results and the limitations accurately, including negative and non-significant findings. It excludes fabrication and falsification, and it also excludes subtler distortions: *selective reporting* of outcomes, *cherry-picking* of data, *p-hacking* (trying analyses until one is significant), *HARKing* and unplanned post-hoc subgroup analysis presented as planned. *Publication bias* (the file drawer problem) arises when only positive results are published, and *outcome-reporting bias* when only some measured outcomes are reported. Images must be adjusted uniformly and any adjustment disclosed. Reporting guidelines improve completeness: CONSORT for randomised trials, PRISMA for systematic reviews, STROBE for observational studies, ARRIVE for animal studies and COREQ or SRQR for qualitative work, catalogued by the EQUATOR Network.

**Memorise.**

- CONSORT = trials; PRISMA = systematic reviews; STROBE = observational; ARRIVE = animals; COREQ/SRQR = qualitative.

- p-hacking, HARKing, cherry-picking = distortions of reporting.

- Honest error is corrected, not concealed.

**Analyse / traps.** Leaving out inconvenient results that were measured is a form of bias even if no number was changed.

### 6C.7 Pre-registration, Transparency and Open Data

**Concept.** *Pre-registration* means recording the hypotheses, design and analysis plan in a time-stamped public registry before collecting data, so that later choices cannot be disguised as plans. Registries include OSF Registries, AsPredicted, ClinicalTrials.gov, the Clinical Trials Registry of India (CTRI) and PROSPERO for systematic reviews. In *registered reports* a journal reviews the protocol first and commits to publishing the paper whatever the result. Transparency also covers sharing data, code and materials, following the FAIR principles and the Transparency and Openness Promotion (TOP) guidelines, and including a data-availability statement. The *reproducibility crisis*, highlighted when a large replication project in psychology (2015) reproduced fewer than half of the studies, drives these reforms. Sharing must still respect consent and confidentiality, so the aim is data “as open as possible, as closed as necessary”.

**Memorise.**

- Pre-registration: plan recorded before data collection.

- Registered report: protocol is peer-reviewed first; publication does not depend on results.

- Registries: OSF, AsPredicted, ClinicalTrials.gov, CTRI, PROSPERO.

**Analyse / traps.** Pre-registration does not forbid exploratory analysis; it requires that exploratory work be labelled as such.

### 6C.8 Correcting Errors: Errata, Retractions and Honest Error

**Concept.** When an error is found after publication, the proper response is a published correction. An *erratum* corrects an error made by the publisher, and a *corrigendum* corrects an error made by the author; both leave the paper standing when the conclusions are unaffected. An *expression of concern* is a notice from editors that questions about a paper are under investigation. A *retraction* withdraws a paper because of serious error or misconduct (fabrication, plagiarism, duplicate publication or unethical research); the paper remains accessible but is marked as retracted, following COPE guidelines. Retraction is not always a finding of fraud, since honest error can lead to it, and researchers who discover their own honest error are expected to report it promptly to the journal.

**Memorise.**

- Erratum = publisher’s error; corrigendum = author’s error.

- Expression of concern = investigation in progress. Retraction = withdrawal for serious problems.

- Honest error ≠ misconduct; concealing it is the wrong.

**Analyse / traps.** A retracted paper is still visible, marked as retracted, so that readers know its status.
