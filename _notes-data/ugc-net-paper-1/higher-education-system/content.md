# Higher Education System: Detailed Notes for UGC NET Paper 1

## Chapter 1: Institutions of Higher Learning and Education in Ancient India

### 1.1 The big picture

Ancient Indian education grew out of a spiritual and social vision rather than a bureaucratic one. In the **Vedic period** (roughly 1500 to 600 BCE) learning was oral, personal and tied to the household of the teacher. In the **Upanishadic period** it became more philosophical and inquiry-driven. From about the 6th century BCE the **Buddhist and Jain traditions** created organised monastic institutions (viharas and mahaviharas) that grew into what many historians call the first residential universities of the world. After the Gupta age, large monastic centres such as Nalanda, Vikramshila and Odantapuri became international hubs, drawing students from China, Korea, Tibet, Sri Lanka and South-East Asia. The tradition declined from the late 12th century as these centres were destroyed and patronage collapsed.

The central aim of education was **holistic development**: moksha (liberation), character formation, mastery of duty (dharma), preservation of culture and, in the later phase, livelihood skills. Examination-driven certification did not exist in the modern sense; the teacher's judgement and public debate were the tests.

### 1.2 Vedic and Brahmanical education

**Gurukul system.** The student (brahmachari or shishya) lived in the home of the teacher (guru, acharya) for years, usually twelve or more, served the household, begged alms for food and learned by listening, repeating and reflecting. Residential life was meant to shape discipline, humility and self-reliance. Education was largely free of formal fees; at the end the student gave **guru dakshina**, a voluntary gift whose form depended on the teacher's wish and the student's means.

**Ashramas and samskaras.** Life was divided into four stages: *brahmacharya* (student), *grihastha* (householder), *vanaprastha* (forest dweller) and *sannyasa* (renunciate). Education belonged to the first stage. Three ceremonies marked it: **Vidyarambha** (beginning of letters in childhood), **Upanayana** (the thread ceremony that formally admitted the child to study) and **Samavartana** (the convocation-like ceremony on completion). The teacher's parting advice, such as the "Satyam vada, dharmam chara" instruction in the Taittiriya Upanishad, functioned like a moral convocation address.

**Curriculum.** Core subjects were the four Vedas, the six Vedangas (phonetics, ritual, grammar, etymology, metre, astronomy), the Upanishads, philosophy and logic (nyaya), grammar, medicine (ayurveda), mathematics, astronomy, polity, military arts and the 64 kalas (arts and crafts) at various centres.

**Methods.** Teaching was mainly oral: recitation (pathana), memorisation, repetition. The Upanishadic ideal described three steps of knowing: **Shravana** (listening), **Manana** (reflection) and **Nididhyasana** (deep contemplation). Debate (**shastrartha**) in assemblies called parishads tested scholars. Senior students acted as **monitors** who taught juniors, an early peer-tutoring system. Teaching was often individualised; the teacher adapted to the capacity of each student.

**Women.** In the early Vedic period women such as Gargi, Maitreyi, Lopamudra and Ghosha took part in learning and debate. Access narrowed in later centuries, though some women remained scholars and teachers.

### 1.3 Buddhist education

Buddhist education was organised around the **sangha** (monastic community). Entry was open across castes, which was a major social departure. The first stage of ordination, **Pabbajja** ("going forth"), was given to a novice around age **8**; the second, **Upasampada** (higher ordination), at age **20**. During training the novice (samanera) lived under a preceptor (upajjhaya) and teacher (acharya) and studied the Tripitaka, logic, grammar and, in later centuries, secular subjects. The medium was largely **Pali** in early centuries and later Sanskrit and regional languages. Monastic life combined study, discipline and debate; viharas received royal and village grants, which gave stability.

Buddhist education was institutional where Vedic education was personal. It used **lecture, discussion and debate**, had defined hierarchies of monks, and managed libraries and hostels. Its influence on later centres of learning was profound.

### 1.4 Jain education

Jain monasteries and temples also taught, using Prakrit and later Sanskrit, with emphasis on ahimsa, ethics, logic and Jain canonical literature. Centres such as Shravanabelagola and Mathura were important. Jain education, like Buddhist education, was open to those who accepted the discipline of the order.

**Nalanda in more detail.** Nalanda is the exam favourite. Admission began with an oral entrance test conducted by the gatekeeper-scholar (dwarpandit), and only a small share of applicants passed. Students studied Mahayana Buddhism along with Vedas, logic, grammar, medicine, astronomy and mathematics. The institution was supported by revenue from many villages granted by kings; food, lodging and teaching were free. The great teacher **Shilabhadra** headed it when the Chinese monk **Hiuen Tsang (Xuanzang)** studied there in the reign of **Harshavardhana** in the 7th century. **I-tsing** visited later in that century. Nalanda was sacked by **Bakhtiyar Khalji** around **1193 CE**. The modern **Nalanda University** was created by the Nalanda University Act 2010 and a new campus at Rajgir was inaugurated in 2024. The ruins are a UNESCO World Heritage Site (2016).

**Chinese visitors.** Fa-Hien (Faxian) came during the reign of Chandragupta II (about 399 to 414 CE). Hiuen Tsang came during the reign of Harsha (630 to 645 CE). I-tsing came in the late 7th century. Their accounts are major sources on ancient Indian learning.

- **Three steps:** Shravana, Manana, Nididhyasana (listen, think, realise).

### Analysis Angle: Chapter 1

- **Why did ancient centres matter?** They were residential, international and supported by public grants, which makes them close ancestors of the modern residential university. NEP 2020 explicitly refers to Takshashila, Nalanda, Vallabhi and Vikramshila as models of multidisciplinary, large, research-oriented institutions.

- **Gurukul versus modern system.** Gurukul stressed character, individual attention, oral learning and lifelong relationship with the teacher; the modern system stresses certification, standardised curricula, large cohorts and professional specialisation. Questions often ask which feature is *not* characteristic of the Gurukul (for example mass examinations or printed textbooks).

- **Why did the tradition decline?** Loss of royal patronage, foreign invasions (destruction of Nalanda and Vikramshila), shift towards ritualism, and the rise of new languages and political structures.

- **Common trap.** Do not confuse the Pali-medium Buddhist education with Sanskrit Brahmanical education, and do not confuse founders (Kumaragupta I for Nalanda; Dharmapala for Vikramshila).

## Chapter 2: Evolution of Higher Learning and Research in Post-Independence India

### 2.1 The big picture

Independence brought three tasks for higher education: **expand** access beyond a small elite, **build** institutions for science, technology and national development, and **reorient** a colonial system towards Indian needs. The story unfolds in phases. The **1950s** were the period of institution-building and the first commissions. The **1960s** brought the Kothari Commission and the national policy framework. The **1970s and 1980s** saw expansion, the 42nd Amendment (education moved to the Concurrent List in 1976), the open university movement and NPE 1986. The **1990s** brought liberalisation, funding constraints and private participation. The **2000s** focused on quality, the Knowledge Commission and the Yash Pal Committee. The **2010s and 2020s** brought accreditation reforms, NEP 2020, credit-based flexibility, foreign campuses and the move towards a single regulator.

### 2.3 UGC: the central regulator of the post-independence era

The **University Grants Commission** began as an interim body in 1953 (inaugurated by Maulana Azad) and became a **statutory body** under the **UGC Act, 1956**. The original University Grants Committee of 1945 had been limited to Aligarh, Banaras and Delhi. The Radhakrishnan Commission's recommendation led to the present form. The UGC has a Chairperson, a Vice-Chairperson and ten other members. It is headquartered in New Delhi, with regional centres at places such as Pune, Bhopal, Kolkata, Hyderabad, Guwahati and Bengaluru.

**Its three functions.** (1) *Funding*: grants to eligible universities and colleges (through Sections 2(f) and 12(B)). (2) *Coordination*: among Centre, States and institutions. (3) *Maintenance of standards*: setting regulations on minimum qualifications, curricula frameworks, degrees and quality.

**Key sections to remember.** Section 2(f): definition of a university. Section 3: declaration of an institution as a "deemed to be university" (by the Central Government on UGC advice). Section 12(B): fitness for central grants. Section 22: only a university can confer degrees. A college must be listed under 2(f) and 12(B) to receive UGC development grants.

**Related bodies created over time.** NAAC (1994, Bengaluru; accreditation), NCERT (1961), NIEPA (planning and administration), INFLIBNET (1991; library networking), CEC (media, television), IUCAA and other inter-university centres, and various fellowship schemes (JRF and NET were introduced in the 1980s).

### 2.4 Expansion and institutions

- **Centres of excellence.** IIT Kharagpur (1951) was the first IIT; the IIT Act came in 1961. IIMs at Calcutta and Ahmedabad were founded in 1961. AIIMS Act 1956. IISc Bengaluru (1909) predates independence. IISERs began in 2006. NITs were created under the NIT Act 2007. IIITs (PPP) Act 2017. National law universities started with NLSIU Bengaluru (1987).

- **Agricultural universities.** G. B. Pant University of Agriculture and Technology at Pantnagar (1960) was the first agricultural university; the idea came from the US land-grant model.

- **Open and distance learning.** Correspondence courses began at Delhi University in 1962; Dr. B. R. Ambedkar Open University (1982) was the first open university; IGNOU followed in 1985.

- **Private and deemed universities.** Growth accelerated after the 1990s and after the Supreme Court's *Yash Pal v. State of Chhattisgarh* (2005) and *TMA Pai* (2002) decisions.

- **Recent expansion schemes.** RUSA (2013) for state institutions, merged into PM-USHA in 2023; Institutions of Eminence (2017).

- **Scale.** According to the All India Survey on Higher Education for 2021 to 22, India had roughly 1,100 to 1,200 universities and over 45,000 colleges, with a Gross Enrolment Ratio of about 28 per cent. These figures change each year, so check the latest AISHE report.

### Analysis Angle: Chapter 2

- **Continuity.** Every commission since 1948 has said the same few things: more funding, more autonomy, more access for disadvantaged groups, better teacher quality, and linking education with work. The differences are of emphasis. Radhakrishnan stressed quality and values; Kothari stressed national development and a system structure; NPE 1986 stressed equity and open learning; NKC and Yash Pal stressed regulation reform and autonomy; NEP 2020 stresses flexibility, multidisciplinarity and a single regulator.

- **Regulation story.** UGC (1956) combined funding and regulation. Later bodies split specialised domains (AICTE, NCTE, NMC, BCI), creating overlap. The NKC (IRAHE), Yash Pal (NCHER), the 2018 draft HECI Act and NEP 2020 (HECI) all attempted to unify them, and the 2025 VBSA Bill continues that arc.

- **Funding.** Kothari's 6 per cent target is still repeated in NPE 1986 and NEP 2020 but actual public spending has stayed well below it.

## Chapter 3: Oriental, Conventional and Non-Conventional Learning Programmes

### 3.1 Three terms, three streams

This topic groups India's learning provision into three streams. **Oriental learning** is the study of classical Indian and Asian languages, philosophy, religion and traditional sciences, delivered by institutions that grew out of the older pathshala, tol, madrasa and monastic traditions. **Conventional learning** means regular, campus-based, face-to-face education in schools, colleges and universities leading to recognised degrees. **Non-conventional learning** covers every route that relaxes the fixed time, place, pace or entry rule of the classroom: open and distance learning, online and blended programmes, MOOCs, open schooling, adult and non-formal education, continuing education and lifelong learning. NTA questions on this topic usually ask for the name of an institution or Act, the distinguishing feature of a mode, or a comparison between regular and distance modes.

### 3.2 Oriental learning

**Historical roots.** Before the colonial period, Sanskrit learning was carried on in **pathshalas, tols** (especially in Bengal) and **Banaras and Nadia** centres, while Persian and Arabic learning was carried on in **maktabs and madrasas**. The East India Company first patronised this tradition: the **Calcutta Madrasa** (1781, Warren Hastings), the **Asiatic Society** (1784, William Jones) and the **Sanskrit College at Banaras** (1791, Jonathan Duncan). **Fort William College** (1800, Lord Wellesley) trained British officials in Indian languages. The **Charter Act of 1813** set aside one lakh rupees annually for the revival and improvement of literature and the learned natives of India, and the **Orientalist versus Anglicist controversy** followed. Macaulay's **Minute of 1835** and Bentinck's resolution of that year shifted official funding towards English education, which sidelined oriental institutions but did not end them.

**National education movements.** In reaction to the colonial system, Indian reformers set up their own institutions: Tagore's school at **Santiniketan (1901)**, which became **Visva-Bharati (1921)**; **Gurukul Kangri** (1902, Swami Shraddhanand); **Jamia Millia Islamia** (1920); **Gujarat Vidyapith** (1920, Gandhi); **Kashi Vidyapith** (1921) and **Tilak Vidyapeeth**. These emerged strongly during the Non-Cooperation Movement and aimed to combine tradition with modern needs.

**After independence.** The Radhakrishnan Commission discussed Sanskrit, and the **Sanskrit Commission (1956 to 57)** led by **Suniti Kumar Chatterji** recommended strengthening Sanskrit education. A series of Sanskrit universities were created by States: **Sampurnanand Sanskrit University** (Varanasi), **Kameshwar Singh Darbhanga Sanskrit University** (Bihar), **Sri Jagannath Sanskrit University** (Puri) and **Kavikulaguru Kalidas Sanskrit University** (Ramtek, Maharashtra), among others. The Centre then created **three Central Sanskrit Universities** through the **Central Sanskrit Universities Act, 2020**: Rashtriya Sanskrit Sansthan (New Delhi), Shri Lal Bahadur Shastri Rashtriya Sanskrit Vidyapeeth (New Delhi) and **Rashtriya Sanskrit Vidyapeeth (Tirupati)**. **Maharshi Sandipani Rashtriya Veda Vidya Pratishthan** (Ujjain) supports Vedic learning.

**Buddhist, Tibetan and other oriental institutions.** **Nava Nalanda Mahavihara** (Bihar) is known for Pali and Buddhist studies; the **Central Institute of Higher Tibetan Studies** is at **Sarnath**; the **Central Institute of Buddhist Studies** is at **Leh**. **Maulana Azad National Urdu University** (Hyderabad, 1998) promotes Urdu and offers distance education. Arabic and Persian learning continues in madrasas, Aligarh and Jamia.

**Classical languages.** The government recognises classical languages: **Tamil (2004), Sanskrit (2005), Telugu and Kannada (2008), Malayalam (2013), Odia (2014)**. In **October 2024** Marathi, Pali, Prakrit, Assamese and Bengali were added, making **eleven**. Classical status brings dedicated centres, chairs and awards.

**Indian Knowledge Systems (IKS).** An IKS Division was created at **AICTE** in 2020. NEP 2020 recommends integrating Indian knowledge, languages, arts and traditions into curricula, and proposes the **Indian Institute of Translation and Interpretation**. UGC and AICTE have framed IKS credit courses.

### 3.3 Conventional learning

Conventional learning is the regular mode: scheduled classes, physical attendance, teacher-led instruction, internal assessment and end-term examinations. Its institutional types are universities (Central, State, Deemed, Private), colleges (government, aided, private, autonomous, affiliated, constituent) and Institutes of National Importance. **Affiliating universities** (such as many State universities) prescribe courses and conduct exams for affiliated colleges; **unitary universities** teach mainly on their own campus; **autonomous colleges** frame their own syllabi but degrees are awarded by the parent university. The Kothari Commission proposed autonomous colleges, and NEP 2020 proposes to phase out affiliation over about 15 years.

**Strengths:** direct interaction, peer learning, laboratories and library access, campus life. **Limitations:** fixed time and place, higher cost, limited seats, and difficulty for working adults.

### 3.4 Non-conventional learning

#### 3.4.1 Open and distance learning (ODL)

**Core ideas.** In distance education teacher and learner are physically separated, and communication is mediated by print, broadcast, digital or other media. "Open" refers to removal of barriers of entry qualifications, age, place and pace. The main instructional input is **Self-Learning Material (SLM)**, supported by counselling at **study centres**, assignments (continuous evaluation) and **term-end examinations**. In IGNOU, term-end examination carries about 70 per cent weightage, assignments 30 per cent, and **one credit equals 30 hours of study**.

**Theorists.** **Charles Wedemeyer**: independent study. **Michael G. Moore**: theory of transactional distance. **Otto Peters**: industrialisation of teaching. **Börje Holmberg**: guided didactic conversation. **Malcolm Knowles**: andragogy (adult learning).

**Milestones.**

- **1962**: correspondence courses at the University of Delhi.

- **1964 to 66**: Kothari Commission recommends correspondence and evening courses.

- **1982**: Andhra Pradesh (now Dr. B. R. Ambedkar) Open University at Hyderabad, the first open university.

- **1985**: **IGNOU** established by Act of Parliament; headquarters at Maidan Garhi, New Delhi; first Vice-Chancellor G. Ram Reddy; called the "People's University".

- **1986**: NPE gives special emphasis to open learning.

- **1991**: **Distance Education Council (DEC)** set up under IGNOU Act; its functions were transferred to UGC in 2013 (UGC-DEB).

- **State Open Universities:** Kota (now **Vardhaman Mahaveer Open University**, 1987), Nalanda Open University (Patna, 1987), **Yashwantrao Chavan Maharashtra Open University** (Nashik, 1989), Bhoj Open University (1991), Netaji Subhas Open University (Kolkata, 1997), Karnataka State Open University (1996), Tamil Nadu Open University (2002), and others in Assam, Uttarakhand, Chhattisgarh and elsewhere.

- **Dual-mode universities** (such as Delhi University) run regular and distance programmes.

- **Media support:** Gyan Darshan (TV), **Gyan Vani** (FM radio), EDUSAT (2004), the Consortium for Educational Communication (CEC), and UGC's Countrywide Classroom.

**UGC (Open and Distance Learning Programmes and Online Programmes) Regulations, 2020.** HEIs can offer ODL or online programmes if they hold a **NAAC score of at least 3.26** (on a 4-point scale) or are within the **top 100 in NIRF** (overall or university category), among other conditions. Degrees are equivalent to those of the conventional mode. Certain programmes such as medicine, engineering and several professional courses are not allowed through ODL or online mode.

#### 3.4.2 Online and blended learning

**MOOCs** (Massive Open Online Courses) are free or low-cost courses open to large numbers. **cMOOCs** follow connectivism (George Siemens, Stephen Downes) and rely on networks; **xMOOCs** are structured and content-led (Coursera, edX, Udacity). **SWAYAM** (2017) is India's MOOC platform with a **four-quadrant approach** (video lecture, reading material, self-assessment, discussion forum). National coordinators: **NPTEL** (engineering), **CEC** (undergraduate non-technical), **UGC** (postgraduate non-technical), **NCERT and NIOS** (school), **IGNOU** (out-of-school), **IIM Bangalore** (management), **NITTTR** (teacher training). UGC allows up to **40 per cent** of credits in a programme through SWAYAM.

**Other digital initiatives.** NPTEL (2003), **NMEICT** (2009), **National Digital Library of India (NDLI)**, **e-PG Pathshala**, **Shodhganga** (theses repository), SWAYAM Prabha (DTH channels), Virtual Labs, DIKSHA and NROER (school level), PM eVidya. **Blended learning** combines face-to-face and online elements; a **flipped classroom** moves content delivery online and uses class time for discussion. **Synchronous** learning happens in real time (live class); **asynchronous** learning is time-independent (recorded lectures, forums). **Moodle** is an open-source LMS. Open Educational Resources are often licensed under Creative Commons.

#### 3.4.3 Non-formal, adult and lifelong education

**Formal** education is structured, graded and leads to recognised qualifications. **Non-formal** education is organised but flexible and outside the formal system. **Informal** education is incidental, lifelong learning from family, peers and media. India's adult education programmes: **National Adult Education Programme (1978)**, **National Literacy Mission (1988)**, **Saakshar Bharat (2009)** and **ULLAS, the New India Literacy Programme (2022 to 27)**. **Jan Shikshan Sansthans** (earlier Shramik Vidyapeeth) provide vocational training to non-literate and neo-literate adults. The **National Institute of Open Schooling (NIOS, 1989)** serves open schooling. The **Faure Commission (1972), *Learning to Be***, promoted lifelong education and the learning society.

**Recognition of Prior Learning (RPL)** certifies skills acquired informally. **Credit transfer through the Academic Bank of Credits** allows learners to move between modes (see topic 6).

### Analysis Angle: Chapter 3

- **Access versus quality.** ODL expands access at low cost, but quality depends on SLM, learner support and examination integrity. Regulation therefore links permission to accreditation scores.

- **Convergence.** NEP 2020 and the ABC blur the boundary: a learner can combine online, open and regular credits, and ODL and online degrees are treated as equivalent.

- **Oriental learning today.** The policy aim is to modernise rather than isolate: pair classical studies with modern disciplines, use IKS credits, and keep classical language centres.

## Chapter 4: Professional, Technical and Skill-Based Education

### 4.1 Concepts and distinctions

**General education** gives broad learning in arts, sciences, commerce and humanities and is not tied to a particular licensed occupation. **Professional education** prepares people for a regulated profession (medicine, law, engineering, teaching, architecture, accountancy) and usually carries a statutory council that controls entry, curriculum and licensing. **Technical education** focuses on engineering, technology, management and applied fields at certificate, diploma, degree and postgraduate levels, with AICTE as the principal regulator. **Vocational and skill-based education** prepares learners for specific jobs and trades, often through shorter, competency-based courses with industry participation. The boundaries overlap; for example engineering is both technical and professional. The distinction matters for questions on *which body regulates which stream*.

### 4.2 Technical and engineering education

**Evolution.** The first engineering college in India was set up at **Roorkee in 1847** (Thomason College of Civil Engineering, now IIT Roorkee). Technical education grew slowly in the colonial period. After independence the **Sarkar Committee (1946)** proposed higher technical institutions on the pattern of MIT, leading to **IIT Kharagpur (1951)**, the first IIT. The **Institutes of Technology Act, 1961** gave IITs the status of Institutes of National Importance; the IIT Council is chaired by the Union Education Minister. Regional Engineering Colleges were upgraded to **National Institutes of Technology**, and the **NIT Act, 2007** declared them Institutes of National Importance. **IIITs (PPP)** were given statutory form by the 2017 Act. The Indian Institute of Science (Bengaluru, 1909, founded by **J. N. Tata**) is the oldest premier research institution in the field.

**AICTE.** The All India Council for Technical Education started in **1945** as an advisory body and received **statutory status through the AICTE Act, 1987**. It plans and coordinates technical education, approves institutions, sets norms and fees, and covers engineering, technology, management, pharmacy, architecture, town planning, hotel management and applied arts. The Supreme Court in **Bharathidasan University v. AICTE (2001)** held that AICTE approval is not mandatory for universities and their own departments, though it applies to affiliated colleges and stand-alone institutions. **NBA** (National Board of Accreditation, set up 1994, autonomous from 2010) accredits **programmes** of technical education; **NAAC** accredits **institutions**. India is a signatory of the **Washington Accord**, which recognises accredited engineering degrees internationally. **GATE** is conducted by IISc and the IITs on a rotational basis; JEE and CUET are conducted by NTA (set up 2017).

**Polytechnics and ITIs.** Polytechnics offer three-year diplomas in engineering; diploma holders enter degree programmes laterally into the second year. **Industrial Training Institutes** started in **1950** and now fall under the **Directorate General of Training** in the Ministry of Skill Development and Entrepreneurship, which awards the National Trade Certificate.

**Management education.** XLRI Jamshedpur (1949) is often cited as the first business school; the Faculty of Management Studies Delhi was set up in 1954; **IIM Calcutta and IIM Ahmedabad (1961)**; IIM Bangalore (1973). The **IIM Act, 2017** gave IIMs degree-granting autonomy. International accreditors include AACSB, EQUIS and AMBA.

### 4.3 Medical and health education

The **Medical College at Calcutta** (1835) was among the first modern medical colleges. The **Indian Medical Council Act, 1933** created the first Medical Council of India; the **IMC Act, 1956** replaced it. The **National Medical Commission Act, 2019** dissolved MCI; the NMC began functioning in 2020 with **four autonomous boards**: Under-Graduate Medical Education Board, Post-Graduate Medical Education Board, Medical Assessment and Rating Board, and Ethics and Medical Registration Board. **NEET** (conducted by NTA) is the common entrance test for medical admissions, and a **National Exit Test (NExT)** is envisaged as a licensing and postgraduate-entry examination. **AIIMS** was created by an Act of 1956 and has since expanded to many cities. Other health councils: **Dental Council of India** (Dentists Act 1948), **Pharmacy Council of India** (Pharmacy Act 1948), **Indian Nursing Council** (Act 1947), **NCISM** for Indian systems of medicine (Act 2020), **National Commission for Homoeopathy** (Act 2020), and the **National Commission for Allied and Healthcare Professions** (Act 2021). The **All India Quota** in state medical colleges is 15 per cent of undergraduate seats.

### 4.4 Legal education

The **Advocates Act, 1961** created the **Bar Council of India (BCI)** and State Bar Councils, which prescribe legal education standards, recognise law degrees and enrol advocates. The **All India Bar Examination (AIBE)** is conducted by BCI. The first National Law School was **NLSIU Bengaluru (1987)**, created by a State Act; **CLAT** is conducted by a consortium of National Law Universities. Integrated law degrees (BA LLB and others) are **five years**, and the LLB after graduation is three years. NEP 2020 proposes to keep legal and medical education outside the single higher-education regulator (HECI) but to move all institutions towards multidisciplinarity.

### 4.5 Teacher education

The **National Council for Teacher Education (NCTE)** was set up in **1973** as an advisory body and became **statutory through the NCTE Act, 1993** (effective 1995). It has regional committees, namely Northern (Jaipur), Eastern (Bhubaneswar), Southern (Bengaluru) and Western (Bhopal). NCTE Regulations 2014 made the **B.Ed. a two-year programme**. NEP 2020 proposes a **four-year integrated B.Ed. (ITEP)** as the minimum qualification for teaching by 2030. **NCERT** (1961) develops school curricula; **SCERTs** work at State level; **DIETs** were set up after NPE 1986 and the Programme of Action. The **Justice J. S. Verma Commission (2012)** reviewed teacher education; the **Pandit Madan Mohan Malaviya National Mission on Teachers and Teaching (2014)** supports training. **CTET** is conducted by CBSE, and the **National Professional Standards for Teachers (NPST)** are proposed by NEP 2020.

### 4.6 Other professional streams

- **Agriculture:** ICAR (1929) oversees agricultural education; the first agricultural university was **G. B. Pant University, Pantnagar (1960)**; accreditation through ICAR's board.

- **Architecture:** Council of Architecture under the **Architects Act, 1972**.

- **Accountancy and related:** ICAI (Chartered Accountants Act 1949), ICMAI (Cost and Works Accountants Act 1959), ICSI (Company Secretaries Act 1980).

- **Veterinary:** Veterinary Council of India (Act 1984).

- **Rehabilitation and special education:** Rehabilitation Council of India (Act 1992).

- **Hospitality:** National Council for Hotel Management and Catering Technology (Ministry of Tourism).

- **Design:** National Institute of Design (NID Act 2014); National Institute of Fashion Technology (NIFT Act 2006); School of Planning and Architecture (2014).

### 4.7 Skill-based education

**Policy and structures.** The **National Skill Development Policy (2009)** targeted 500 million skilled people by 2022. The **National Skill Development Corporation (NSDC, 2008)** is a not-for-profit public limited company (public-private partnership). The **Ministry of Skill Development and Entrepreneurship** was formed in **2014**. The **Skill India Mission and National Skill Development Mission** were launched in **2015**, with **PMKVY** (Pradhan Mantri Kaushal Vikas Yojana) as the flagship short-term training scheme. **NAPS** (National Apprenticeship Promotion Scheme, 2016) supports apprenticeship under the **Apprentices Act, 1961**. **NCVET** (National Council for Vocational Education and Training, 2018) replaced earlier bodies as the regulator of vocational qualifications. **Sector Skill Councils** are industry-led bodies that define **National Occupational Standards (NOS)** and **Qualification Packs (QPs)**.

**NSQF.** The **National Skills Qualifications Framework (2013)** has **ten levels** (level 1 to level 10) described by knowledge, skills, responsibility and related outcomes. The **National Credit Framework (2023)** integrates school, higher and vocational credits, and the **National Higher Education Qualifications Framework** aligns degrees.

**In higher education.** UGC launched the **B.Voc. degree (2013)** with **Certificate, Diploma, Advanced Diploma and Degree** exit points, and encouraged community colleges and DDU Kaushal Kendras. NEP 2020 aims to integrate vocational education into all institutions and to have at least **50 per cent** of learners exposed to vocational education by 2025. Apprenticeship-embedded degrees, internships, **RPL**, and **SWAYAM Plus** (industry-relevant courses) support employability.

**Employability concepts.** *Skill gap* is the difference between skills employers need and skills graduates have. **21st-century skills** include critical thinking, creativity, collaboration and communication (the four Cs). **Soft skills** include communication, teamwork and time management. **Dual system** training (a combination of workplace apprenticeship and school-based learning) originated in Germany.

### Analysis Angle: Chapter 4

- **Fragmented regulation.** Each profession has its own statutory council, which creates overlap with UGC and with universities. NEP 2020's HECI vision (and the VBSA Bill 2025 that replaces UGC, AICTE and NCTE) aims to reduce this, while keeping medical and legal education in their own regulatory domains.

- **Autonomy versus control.** *Bharathidasan University* tilted towards university autonomy; *TMA Pai (2002)* and *Inamdar (2005)* limited State control over private unaided professional colleges, particularly admission and fee fixation.

- **Employability.** Despite expansion, a skill gap persists because curricula lag behind industry. Reforms such as apprenticeship-embedded degrees, NSQF alignment, B.Voc and internships try to close it.

## Chapter 5: Value Education and Environmental Education

### 5.1 Value education

#### 5.1.1 Concept

The word *value* comes from the Latin **valere**, meaning "to be of worth". A value is an enduring belief that guides choices and judgements about what is desirable. **Value education** is the deliberate process of helping learners recognise, internalise and practise values such as truth, honesty, compassion, responsibility, tolerance and respect, so that knowledge is joined to character. It is concerned with the *affective* domain (attitudes, feelings, commitments) and not only with cognition. In India the ideal is the **holistic development** of the person; the hidden aim of education in every era from the gurukul to NEP 2020 has been to produce a good human being as well as a skilled worker.

**Types of values.** *Intrinsic values* are desirable for their own sake (truth, beauty, goodness). *Instrumental (extrinsic) values* are useful as means to something else (money, power, skills). **Eduard Spranger** classified human values into six types: **theoretical, economic, aesthetic, social, political and religious**. Another popular classification lists personal, social, moral, spiritual, aesthetic and democratic values. The Indian **purusharthas** are **dharma, artha, kama and moksha**. The **five core human values** promoted in value-education programmes are **Truth, Right Conduct, Peace, Love and Non-violence**. Gandhi's **seven social sins** include "wealth without work", "commerce without morality" and "science without humanity".

**Constitutional values.** The Preamble promises **Justice, Liberty, Equality and Fraternity** (fraternity assures dignity of the individual and the unity and integrity of the nation). **Fundamental Duties (Article 51A)** were added by the **42nd Amendment (1976)**, and the **86th Amendment (2002)** added the eleventh duty (parents and guardians to provide opportunities for education to children aged 6 to 14). Value education in India is therefore tied to **secularism, democracy, scientific temper, national integration and respect for diversity**.

#### 5.1.2 Theories to know

|------------------------------|----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------|
| **Lawrence Kohlberg**        | Moral development in **three levels, six stages**: pre-conventional (punishment and obedience; instrumental exchange), conventional (good boy/good girl; law and order), post-conventional (social contract; universal ethical principles). Stage 6 is based on universal ethical principles |
| **Jean Piaget**              | Moral realism (rules fixed, from authority) giving way to moral relativism (rules as agreements)                                                                                                                                                                                             |

| **Albert Bandura**           | Social learning: values are learnt through observation and modelling, so teacher example matters                                                                                                                                                                                             |
| **Sri Aurobindo**            | Integral education: physical, vital, mental, psychic and spiritual development                                                                                                                                                                                                               |
| **Delors Commission (1996)** | *Learning: The Treasure Within*; four pillars: learning to know, to do, to live together, to be                                                                                                                                                                                              |

**Approaches to value education.** *Inculcation* (direct instruction and habit formation), *moral reasoning* (discussing dilemmas), *values clarification*, *value analysis* and *action learning* (community work, service). The **hidden curriculum** refers to values and norms transmitted informally through institutional culture, teacher behaviour, discipline and relationships. The most effective approach is generally **teacher role modelling combined with participatory practice**, not lecture alone.

#### 5.1.3 Policy history

- **Radhakrishnan Commission (1948 to 49)** emphasised moral and spiritual education and suggested short meditation or reflection at the start of the day.

- **Sri Prakasa Committee (1959)** examined religious and moral instruction.

- **Kothari Commission (1964 to 66)** recommended education in **social, moral and spiritual values**.

- **NPE 1986** treated value education as integral and defined a **common core** including scientific temper, national identity, constitutional obligations and the removal of social barriers; it spoke of the erosion of values.

- **Ramamurti Review (1990)** and Programme of Action (1992) continued the theme.

- **NEP 2020** stresses ethics, constitutional values, service, Indian knowledge traditions, critical thinking and holistic development; AICTE promotes a course on **Universal Human Values**.

#### 5.1.4 Role of values in higher education and society

**In higher education.** Values underpin **academic integrity** (honesty, avoiding fabrication, falsification and plagiarism, the three forms of research misconduct), fairness in assessment, respect for diversity and the freedom of inquiry. The **UGC (Promotion of Academic Integrity and Prevention of Plagiarism in HEIs) Regulations, 2018** operationalise this. Values also shape campus climate: **anti-ragging regulations (2009)**, equity and inclusion cells, protection against sexual harassment (the *Vishaka* guidelines 1997 and the POSH Act 2013), and transparency in governance.

**In society.** Graduates carry values into professions and civic life. Value-based education promotes social cohesion, responsible citizenship, ethical leadership, environmental care and resistance to corruption. Challenges include **commercialisation of education, examination pressure, consumerism, media influence and the gap between professed and practised values**.

### 5.2 Environmental education

#### 5.2.1 Concept and aims

**Environmental education (EE)** develops awareness, knowledge, attitudes, skills and a sense of responsibility so that people can understand and protect the environment. Its five objectives, as stated at **Belgrade (1975)** and **Tbilisi (1977)**, are **awareness, knowledge, attitudes, skills and participation** (evaluation ability is often added). EE is **interdisciplinary and lifelong**. A common formulation is education *about* the environment (knowledge), *in* the environment (experience) and *for* the environment (action and values). Approaches include the **infusion approach** (embedding EE in existing subjects), separate courses, projects and campus action. **Education for Sustainable Development (ESD)** extends EE to link environment with economy and society.

**Sustainable development** (Brundtland) means development that meets present needs without compromising the ability of future generations to meet theirs.

#### 5.2.3 India: constitution, law, courts, programmes

- **Constitution.** **Article 48A** (DPSP: protect and improve the environment; added by the **42nd Amendment, 1976**) and **Article 51A(g)** (fundamental duty to protect the natural environment). Article 21 has been read to include the right to a healthy environment.

- **Laws.** Wildlife Protection Act 1972, Water Act 1974, Forest Conservation Act 1980, Air Act 1981, **Environment (Protection) Act 1986**, Biological Diversity Act 2002, **National Green Tribunal Act 2010**.

- **Courts.** In **M. C. Mehta v. Union of India (1991)** the Supreme Court directed environmental education in schools and colleges; in **2003** it ordered a **compulsory environmental studies course at all levels**, and UGC introduced a compulsory undergraduate **Environmental Studies** course.

- **Policy and programmes.** NPE 1986 and NCF 2005 emphasised environmental awareness; NEP 2020 includes environmental education and climate change. **National Environment Awareness Campaign (1986)**, **Centre for Environment Education (CEE, Ahmedabad, 1984)**, **ENVIS**, **National Green Corps / Eco-clubs (2001 to 02)**, and **Mission LiFE (2022)**.

- **People's movements.** **Bishnoi sacrifice (1730, Khejarli; Amrita Devi)**, **Chipko (1973; Sunderlal Bahuguna, Chandi Prasad Bhatt, Gaura Devi)**, **Silent Valley (Kerala)**, **Appiko (1983, Karnataka)** and **Narmada Bachao Andolan (Medha Patkar)**.

#### 5.2.4 Environmental education in higher education institutions

Higher education contributes through (1) *curriculum* (environmental studies, sustainability, climate science across disciplines), (2) *research* on pollution, climate, biodiversity and energy, (3) *campus practice* (energy and water conservation, waste management, **green audit**, **ISO 14001**), and (4) *community outreach* (extension activities, eco-clubs). **NAAC** includes environmental consciousness and green campus initiatives within the criterion on institutional values and best practices. The **ecological footprint** measures human demand on nature relative to Earth's capacity; the **3Rs** are reduce, reuse, recycle; India has **four biodiversity hotspots** (Himalaya, Indo-Burma, Western Ghats and Sri Lanka, and Nicobar Islands within Sundaland).

*Boundary note.* Detailed environmental science (pollution types, climate change, natural hazards, energy) belongs to Unit IX, People, Development and Environment. For Unit X, focus on environmental *education*: aims, conferences, laws, courts, institutions and campus practice.

- **Spranger = six** (theoretical, economic, aesthetic, social, political, religious); **Kohlberg = 3 levels, 6 stages**; **Krathwohl** top level = characterisation.

- **Delors four pillars:** know, do, live together, be. **Faure:** *Learning to Be*.

- **EE timeline:** 72 Stockholm, 75 Belgrade, 77 Tbilisi, 87 Brundtland and Moscow, 92 Rio, 2015 SDGs.

### Analysis Angle: Chapter 5

- **Link between the two themes.** Environmental ethics is a branch of value education. Sustainability needs values of restraint, equity and responsibility, not just knowledge. Gandhi's emphasis on needs versus greed fits this link.

- **Approach debate.** Value education works through example and practice; instruction alone produces recall but not conduct. Likewise EE works best when infused into every discipline and tied to campus action.

- **Policy gap.** Courts mandated environmental education decades ago, but implementation is uneven because it is often treated as a low-stakes compulsory paper.

## Chapter 6: Policies, Governance and Administration

### 6.1 Who governs higher education: the constitutional picture

Education is a **shared responsibility** of the Union and the States. Before the **42nd Constitutional Amendment (1976)** education was mainly a State subject (State List, Entry 11); the amendment moved it to the **Concurrent List (now Entry 25)**, which reads "education, including technical education, medical education and universities, subject to the provisions of Entries 63, 64, 65 and 66 of List I". The **Union List** keeps the key national powers: **Entry 63** (BHU, AMU, Delhi University and other institutions declared by Parliament to be of national importance), **Entry 64** (institutions for scientific or technical education financed by the Government of India and declared Institutes of National Importance), **Entry 65** (Union agencies for professional, vocational or technical training) and **Entry 66** (**coordination and determination of standards in higher education and research** and scientific and technical institutions). Entry 32 of the **State List** covers the incorporation and regulation of universities other than those in the Union List. On Concurrent subjects, if a Union law and State law conflict, **Article 254** generally makes the Union law prevail, unless a State law was reserved for the President and received assent.

In practice: **Central universities** are created by Acts of Parliament and funded by the Centre; **State universities** by State Legislatures; **deemed universities** are declared by the Central Government on UGC advice (Section 3 of the UGC Act); **private universities** by State Acts, with UGC regulations (2003) applying; **Institutes of National Importance** by special Acts. The **Central Advisory Board of Education (CABE)**, first set up in **1920**, is the apex advisory forum on education.

**Authorities and bodies.**

- **Executive Council (Syndicate in some State universities):** the **principal executive body**; manages property, finances, appointments and policy; chaired by the VC.

- **Academic Council:** the **principal academic body**; controls teaching, courses, curricula, examinations and academic standards; presided over by the VC.

- **Court (Senate):** a large, broad-based body that reviews the annual report and accounts and advises on policy; Senate is the older name in many universities.

- **Board of Studies (BoS):** recommends **courses of study and syllabi** for a subject or department; its proposals are approved by the Academic Council.

- **Faculty:** deals with the courses in a group of subjects.

- **Finance Committee, Planning Board, Selection Committees:** the Finance Committee advises the Executive Council; Selection Committees (chaired by the VC) recommend teacher appointments.

- **IQAC (Internal Quality Assurance Cell):** created on NAAC advice (2005 onwards); chaired by the **head of the institution**; coordinates quality initiatives and prepares the Annual Quality Assurance Report (AQAR).

In colleges the equivalents are the **Governing Body or Managing Committee** (policy and finance), the **Principal** (day-to-day head), the **Staff Council**, and in autonomous colleges the **Governing Body, Academic Council and Boards of Studies**, which frame syllabi, conduct examinations and publish results, while the degree is awarded by the parent university.

### 6.3 Regulators, accreditors and apex bodies

- **UGC** (Act 1956): funding, coordination and standards for universities (see topic 2). Regulations include minimum qualifications for teachers, ragging (2009), equity (2012), plagiarism (2018), PhD (2022), ODL (2020) and graded autonomy (2018).

- **AICTE** (Act 1987): technical education. **NCTE** (Act 1993): teacher education. **NMC, BCI** and other councils: professional domains (see topic 4).

- **NAAC** (1994; Bengaluru): accreditation of institutions. Its assessment framework has **seven criteria**: curricular aspects, teaching-learning and evaluation, research and innovation, infrastructure and learning resources, student support and progression, governance, leadership and management, and institutional values and best practices. Grades run from C to **A++** on a four-point CGPA scale (A++ is 3.51 to 4.00 and A+ is 3.26 to 3.50 in the older scheme). NAAC has been moving towards **binary accreditation** (accredited or not accredited) with maturity-based graded levels.

- **NBA** (1994; autonomous 2010): programme-level accreditation (technical).

- **NIRF** (2015): ranking on five parameters (teaching, learning and resources; research and professional practice; graduation outcomes; outreach and inclusivity; perception).

- **NTA** (2017): conducts national entrance tests (CUET, NEET, JEE Main, UGC NET and others).

- **AIU** (Association of Indian Universities): grants equivalence to degrees. **NIEPA:** educational planning and administration research. **NCERT** (1961): school curriculum. **AISHE:** annual survey of higher education by the Ministry of Education.

- **UGC Graded Autonomy Regulations (2018):** institutions meeting thresholds (for example NAAC score 3.51 or higher for Category I universities) get freedom to start courses, open campuses and set up incubators without prior approval.

### 6.4 National Education Policy 2020: higher education

**Background.** NEP 2020 was approved by the Union Cabinet on **29 July 2020**, replacing the NPE of 1986 (as modified in 1992). It was drafted by a committee under **K. Kasturirangan** (draft submitted 2019) after the Subramanian committee (2016). The Ministry of Human Resource Development was renamed **Ministry of Education**.

**Targets.** GER in higher education from about **26.3 per cent (2018)** to **50 per cent by 2035**, adding **3.5 crore seats**; public spending of **6 per cent of GDP**; **school structure 5+3+3+4**.

**Institutional restructuring.**

1.  **Type 1** research-intensive universities, **Type 2** teaching-intensive universities, **Type 3** autonomous degree-granting colleges.

2.  **MERUs** (Multidisciplinary Education and Research Universities) as models of excellence, at par with IITs and IIMs.

3.  **Multidisciplinary** institutions: at least one in or near every district by **2030**; each HEI to aim for **3,000 or more students**; standalone technical, health, legal and agricultural universities to become multidisciplinary.

4.  **Affiliation to be phased out over about 15 years**; colleges to become autonomous degree-granting or constituent colleges; graded accreditation leading to graded autonomy.

5.  **Independent Boards of Governors** for HEIs, "light but tight" regulation.

**Curriculum and degrees.**

- **Four-year multidisciplinary undergraduate programme** with multiple exit options: **certificate (after one year), diploma (two years), bachelor's degree (three years), bachelor's with research (four years)**; one-year master's after a four-year degree; **M.Phil. discontinued**; Ph.D. after a master's or four-year bachelor's degree with research.

- **Flexibility**: freedom to combine major and minor subjects across streams; vocational integration (50 per cent of learners exposed by 2025); **Indian Knowledge Systems**, classical languages, **Indian Institute of Translation and Interpretation**; teaching in mother tongue or bilingually.

- **Academic Bank of Credits (ABC)**: digital storage of credits earned from recognised HEIs so that degrees can be awarded with credits earned over time.

**Admissions and assessment.** **National Testing Agency** to offer common aptitude and subject tests (CUET). **Holistic assessment** and the **National Assessment Centre (PARAKH)** for schools.

**Faculty.** **Tenure-track system by 2030**; faculty autonomy; **National Mission for Mentoring**; **four-year integrated B.Ed.** as the minimum teaching qualification by 2030; professional standards (NPST).

**Research.** **National Research Foundation** (Anusandhan National Research Foundation Act 2023), research-intensive universities and competitive funding across disciplines.

**Equity and inclusion.** **Gender Inclusion Fund**, **Special Education Zones**, scholarships, and support for socio-economically disadvantaged groups; all HEIs to be **not-for-profit**.

**Technology.** **National Educational Technology Forum (NETF)**, a **National Digital University**, online and ODL expansion with quality equal to in-class programmes.

**Internationalisation.** Entry of top foreign universities by a legislative framework, credit recognition, research collaboration, and an **International Students Office** in HEIs.

### 6.5 Credit and flexibility reforms (implementation)

- **Academic Bank of Credits**: launched **28 July 2021** (first anniversary of NEP); credits stored in **DigiLocker**; credits are valid for **seven years**; regulations notified by UGC in 2021. ABC is a credit **bank**, not a degree-awarding body. **APAAR ID** ("One Nation One Student ID") links records.

- **National Credit Framework (NCrF, 2023)**: integrates school, higher and vocational credits; **1,200 notional learning hours = 40 credits per year**; one credit corresponds to **15 hours of theory** and **30 hours of practical** in a semester.

- **Typical credit load**: 120 credits for a three-year UG degree; **160 credits** for a four-year honours degree. **Major, Minor, Multidisciplinary, Ability Enhancement (AEC), Skill Enhancement (SEC) and Value Added Courses (VAC)** form the UGC structure.

- **Multiple entry and exit**: learners can leave with certificate, diploma or degree at the end of years 1, 2 or 3 and re-enter later within permitted limits.

- **Dual degrees and twinning**: UGC (2022) allows pursuing two programmes simultaneously and set up regulations for **joint, dual and twinning degrees** with foreign institutions ranked within the top 1,000 globally (Indian partners need NAAC 3.01 or NIRF top 100).

- **PhD norms (2022)**: four-year UG graduates with at least 75 per cent can enter doctoral programmes; NET qualifies for fellowships.

### 6.6 Internationalisation and foreign campuses

**Policy goals** are to attract foreign students (**Study in India, 2018**), make India a global study destination, improve quality through competition, and reduce outbound student flow. **GIAN** (2015) brings foreign academics for short courses; **SPARC** (2018) supports research collaboration; **Institutions of Eminence** (2017; up to 20 institutions) receive greater autonomy, including freedom to admit foreign students up to 30 per cent.

**UGC (Setting up and Operation of Campuses of Foreign Higher Educational Institutions in India) Regulations, 2023** (notified November 2023):

- Eligible: foreign HEIs within the **top 500 global rankings** (overall or subject-wise) or otherwise reputed institutions; they must set up an actual **campus** (not a franchise), cannot offer online or ODL programmes, and the qualification must match that of the home campus.

- Operational autonomy: foreign campuses decide **admission process and fees** (reasonable and transparent) and can recruit faculty from India and abroad; repatriation of funds is allowed under Indian law.

- Approval: **Letter of Intent**, then a Letter of Approval. The **University of Southampton (Gurugram)** was the first under these regulations; **Deakin University** was the first foreign campus at **GIFT City**, Gujarat, regulated by **IFSCA (International Financial Services Centres Authority) rules of 2022**.

- **NEP 2020** had spoken of allowing the world's **top 100** universities, whereas the UGC regulations use a **top 500** threshold.

- **Indian campuses abroad:** **IIT Madras** at Zanzibar, Tanzania (2023) and **IIT Delhi** at Abu Dhabi (2024).

### 6.7 HECI and the Viksit Bharat Shiksha Adhishthan Bill

**Long road to a single regulator.**

1.  **National Knowledge Commission (2005 to 09; Sam Pitroda)** recommended an **Independent Regulatory Authority for Higher Education (IRAHE)**.

2.  **Yash Pal Committee (2009)** recommended the **National Commission for Higher Education and Research (NCHER)**.

3.  **Draft HECI Act (2018)** proposed to repeal the UGC Act 1956 and create a Higher Education Commission of India that would regulate (but not fund) HEIs, with funding handled by the Ministry.

4.  **NEP 2020** proposed **HECI as a single overarching regulator** with **four verticals**: **NHERC** (regulation), **GEC** (standard setting through graduate attributes and the NHEQF), **HEGC** (funding) and **NAC** (accreditation). **Medical and legal education are outside HECI.**

5.  **Viksit Bharat Shiksha Adhishthan (VBSA) Bill, 2025** was introduced in the **Lok Sabha in December 2025** by Education Minister Dharmendra Pradhan and referred to a **31-member Joint Parliamentary Committee (21 from Lok Sabha, 10 from Rajya Sabha)**. It proposes to **repeal the UGC Act 1956, the AICTE Act 1987 and the NCTE Act 1993**, and sets up three councils: the **Viksit Bharat Shiksha Viniyaman Parishad** (regulation), the **Viksit Bharat Shiksha Gunvatta Parishad** (accreditation) and the **Viksit Bharat Shiksha Manak Parishad** (standards), keeping medical and legal education outside its scope.

**Status.** As of **July 2026** the Bill was **pending before the JPC and had not been enacted**. Check the latest PRS India or Lok Sabha updates for anything after that.

### 6.8 Other recent reforms worth knowing

- **PM-USHA** (2023) merged RUSA (2013) to fund State institutions.

- **One Nation One Subscription** (approved 2024, rolled out 2025) gives access to scholarly journals to public institutions.

- **CUET** (2022): common entrance for central and participating universities.

- **NAAC reforms (2024):** binary accreditation and maturity-based levels.

- **HEFA** (2017): Higher Education Financing Agency, loans for infrastructure.

- **Institutions of Eminence** (2017): up to 20 institutions with autonomy.

- **NEP verticals: R, S, F, A = NHERC, GEC, HEGC, NAC.** (Regulate, Standards, Fund, Accredit.)

### Analysis Angle: Chapter 6

- **Centre versus State.** The Concurrent List status means policies like NEP need cooperation of States, and States differ on adoption, affiliation and language issues.

- **Autonomy versus accountability.** NEP and graded autonomy give freedom to high-quality institutions while tying regulation to outcomes. Critics worry about centralisation if one body controls regulation across all disciplines.

- **Flexibility versus quality.** ABC and multiple exits widen choice but need robust credit equivalence and academic counselling to avoid dilution.

- **Foreign campuses.** They promise access to global curricula and reduce student outflow, but fees may limit equity and regulation must safeguard quality.

- **Common exam pitfalls.** NEP's 'top 100' versus UGC's 'top 500'; HECI (proposal) versus VBSA (current Bill); NAC (accreditation) versus GEC (standards).

## Chapter 7: Background: Colonial Period, Constitution, Reservation and Issues

### 7.1 Why this background matters

These themes are not separate headings in the Unit X syllabus list, but they underlie many questions in the policy and governance topic. Treat them as supporting knowledge: revise them after the core topics, and use them to explain why Topic 6 looks the way it does.

### 7.2 Colonial period: the road to the modern university

**Narrative.** The East India Company at first supported oriental learning, then debated whether to teach English. The **Orientalists** (H. T. Prinsep, H. H. Wilson) wanted to promote Sanskrit, Arabic and Persian; the **Anglicists** (T. B. Macaulay, with support from Bentinck) wanted English and western science. **Macaulay's Minute (1835)** and **Bentinck's resolution (1835)** decided that government funds would go mainly to English education, and the **downward filtration theory** held that education would percolate from the upper classes downwards. **William Adam's reports (1835 to 38)** documented indigenous education in Bengal and Bihar. **Wood's Despatch (1854)**, the "Magna Carta of English education in India", set the structure that lasted for decades: a Department of Public Instruction in each province, **universities at Calcutta, Bombay and Madras modelled on the University of London** (founded 1857), a grants-in-aid system, vernacular primary schools, teacher training and attention to women's education. The **Hunter Commission (1882)** reviewed primary and secondary education and recommended that primary education be handed to local bodies. The **Indian Universities Commission (1902; Thomas Raleigh)** and **Indian Universities Act (1904; Lord Curzon)** tightened government control over universities. The **Calcutta University Commission (1917 to 19; Michael Sadler)** examined university reform and recommended that intermediate classes be separated from universities and a three-year degree course; it led to new universities such as **Dacca (1921)**. The **Government of India Act 1919** (dyarchy) transferred education to provincial ministers. The **Hartog Committee (1929)** highlighted wastage and stagnation in primary education. The **Abbott-Wood Report (1936 to 37)** dealt with vocational education. **Gandhi's Wardha Scheme of Basic Education (1937)** and the **Zakir Husain Committee (1938)** advocated learning through productive craft for ages 7 to 14. The **Sargent Report (1944)** by the **Central Advisory Board of Education** planned universal, free, compulsory education for ages 6 to 14 over about **40 years**.

**Landmark cases.**

- ***Champakam Dorairajan* (1951):** communal reservation in admissions struck down, leading to Article 15(4).

- ***Mohini Jain v. State of Karnataka* (1992):** right to education flows from Article 21; capitation fee unconstitutional.

- ***Unni Krishnan v. State of A.P.* (1993):** right to free education up to age 14; beyond that subject to economic capacity.

- ***Indra Sawhney v. Union of India* (1992):** upheld 27 per cent OBC reservation; **50 per cent ceiling**; **creamy layer** exclusion.

- ***TMA Pai Foundation* (2002; 11-judge bench):** establishing and running educational institutions is an occupation under Article 19(1)(g); minority status determined State-wise; no capitation fee.

- ***P. A. Inamdar* (2005):** State cannot impose its reservation policy on unaided private professional institutions.

- ***Prof. Yash Pal v. State of Chhattisgarh* (2005):** invalidated provisions of the State's private-universities Act.

- ***Bharathidasan University v. AICTE* (2001):** AICTE approval not required for universities' own departments.

- ***Ashoka Kumar Thakur* (2008):** upheld 27 per cent OBC quota in central institutions with creamy layer excluded.

- ***Pramati Educational Trust* (2014):** RTE Act does not apply to minority institutions.

- ***Society for Unaided Private Schools of Rajasthan* (2012):** upheld RTE's 25 per cent reservation in private unaided schools.

- ***Janhit Abhiyan* (2022):** upheld the EWS quota (103rd Amendment).

- ***NALSA v. Union of India* (2014):** recognised transgender persons as a third gender.

- ***Vishaka v. State of Rajasthan* (1997):** guidelines on workplace sexual harassment.

**Right to Education Act, 2009.** Came into force on **1 April 2010**; free and compulsory elementary education for ages 6 to 14; **25 per cent** seats for weaker and disadvantaged sections in private unaided schools; **pupil-teacher ratio 30:1** at primary level; **no capitation fee, no screening at admission**; **School Management Committees**; the 2019 amendment allowed States to hold back children in classes V and VIII after a regular examination and re-examination. Although RTE applies to elementary education, questions on higher education often use it as a contrast.

The **All India Quota** in state medical colleges is 15 per cent of UG seats.

### 7.5 Issues and challenges in higher education

**Access.** The GER is about 28 per cent (AISHE 2021 to 22) against the NEP target of 50 per cent by 2035; disparities exist by region, rural-urban location, social group and gender. The **digital divide** limits online learning.

**Equity.** Scheduled Castes, Scheduled Tribes, Muslims, women in some regions, persons with disabilities and first-generation learners have lower participation or higher dropout. Responses: reservation, scholarships, hostels, Equal Opportunity Cells, bridge courses, and the Gender Inclusion Fund and Special Education Zones proposed by NEP.

**Quality.** Faculty shortages, outdated curricula, rote learning, weak laboratories, low NAAC coverage and uneven research culture. Responses: NAAC and NBA reforms, NIRF ranking, outcome-based curricula (LOCF), IQAC, faculty development (Malaviya Mission), and multidisciplinary universities.

**Employability and skills.** A gap between graduate competencies and industry needs. Responses: NSQF, B.Voc, apprenticeship-embedded degrees, internships, SWAYAM Plus, start-up incubation and the National Credit Framework.

**Funding and privatisation.** Public expenditure on education has remained below the 6 per cent of GDP benchmark. Privatisation expanded capacity but raised concerns about capitation fees, commercialisation and unequal access. Responses: HEFA loans, PM-Vidyalaxmi (education loans), fee regulation, Institutions of Eminence, and philanthropic funding.

**Research and innovation.** India's gross expenditure on R&D is about 0.7 per cent of GDP; research is concentrated in a few institutions and national labs. Responses: **ANRF**, PMRF (2018), IMPRINT, SPARC, GIAN, innovation councils and incubators, Atal Innovation Mission (2016), and research-intensive universities under NEP.

**Gender and inclusion.** Female enrolment has risen and gender parity (Gender Parity Index) is now close to or above one in aggregate, but women's share in STEM, research and faculty, and safety on campus remain issues. Schemes: **Savitribai Jyotirao Phule Single Girl Child Fellowship** (UGC), **Pragati** (girls in technical education), **Saksham** (differently-abled in technical education), **Ishan Uday** (North-East), POSH Act 2013 and Internal Committees. Transgender inclusion rests on *NALSA (2014)* and the Transgender Persons (Protection of Rights) Act 2019. The first women's university, **SNDT Women's University**, was founded in 1916.

### Analysis Angle: Chapter 7

- **Reservation debate.** Arguments for: correcting historic exclusion and ensuring representation. Arguments against or for reform: efficiency concerns, creamy-layer leakage, the 50 per cent ceiling, and the need for support after admission. Courts have balanced these through the creamy-layer test and the ceiling, now modified in practice by the EWS quota.

- **Concurrent List effects.** Because the Centre and States both legislate, many disputes arise about fee regulation, minority rights and admissions.

- **Policy cycle.** Most problems (funding, quality, access) have been named by every commission since 1948; the persistent issue is implementation capacity, not diagnosis.

### 8.3 Question types, study strategy and exam technique

Unit X of Paper 1 usually carries about five questions (ten marks), so the goal is not to memorise everything but to recognise the pattern of each question type. NTA tends to mix three kinds of items. **Memory questions** ask for a name, year, place or body (who founded Vikramshila, when was the UGC Act passed). **Concept questions** ask what a term means (autonomous college, deemed university, credit transfer, Kohlberg's stages). **Analysis questions** ask you to compare, sequence or judge (match the commission with its main recommendation, arrange events chronologically, assertion and reason, statement-based items).

**Study strategy.** Read the narrative once to build the storyline, then revise only the tables and bullet lists. For years and names, use the memory aids given. For commissions and policies, always learn the triple: *who chaired it, which year, what was its signature recommendation*. For regulators, learn the triple: *body, Act and year, domain*. Spend the most time on topics 2 and 6, because those produce the most questions and change the most often.

**Exam technique.** In match-the-following items, solve the pairs you are sure of first; usually that eliminates two options. In chronology items, anchor on one fixed year (for example 1854 Wood's Despatch, 1948 Radhakrishnan Commission, 1964 Kothari Commission, 1986 NPE, 2020 NEP) and place the rest around it. In assertion and reason items, first decide whether each statement is true independently, then ask whether R actually explains A.
