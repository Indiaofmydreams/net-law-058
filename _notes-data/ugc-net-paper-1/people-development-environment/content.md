# People, Development and Environment: Detailed Notes for UGC NET Paper 1

## Chapter 1: Development and environment: sustainable development, MDGs and SDGs

### 1.1 Concept: growth, development and sustainability

Economic growth means a rise in output or income, usually measured by GDP, while development is a wider idea that includes health, education, equity, freedom and the quality of the environment. The central question of this topic is whether a society can keep improving human well-being without destroying the natural base that supports it. For a long time the answer was assumed to be yes, because resources looked unlimited. The Club of Rome report Limits to Growth (1972) challenged that belief by arguing that exponential growth on a finite planet must eventually overshoot, and the same year the Stockholm Conference on the Human Environment put environment on the international agenda for the first time and led to the creation of UNEP.

The idea of sustainable development was given its classic definition in Our Common Future (1987), the report of the World Commission on Environment and Development chaired by Gro Harlem Brundtland. It defines sustainable development as development that meets the needs of the present without compromising the ability of future generations to meet their own needs. Two ideas sit inside this definition: the concept of needs, especially the essential needs of the world's poor, and the idea of limitations imposed by technology and social organization on the environment's ability to meet present and future needs. Sustainability is therefore built on intergenerational equity (fairness between generations) and intragenerational equity (fairness within the present generation, between rich and poor and between countries).

Sustainable development is usually shown as three interlinked pillars: economic, social and environmental. The business version of this is John Elkington's triple bottom line (people, planet, profit). Economists separate weak sustainability, which assumes that man-made capital can substitute for natural capital as long as total capital does not fall, from strong sustainability, which holds that some natural capital (such as the ozone layer, climate stability or biodiversity) is critical and cannot be replaced by machines or money. The Environmental Kuznets Curve proposes that pollution first rises and then falls as income grows, giving an inverted U; critics note that it holds for some local pollutants such as sulphur dioxide but not for carbon dioxide or biodiversity loss. The ecological footprint (Wackernagel and Rees) measures the biologically productive area needed to support a population's consumption and absorb its waste, and planetary boundaries (Johan Rockstrom and colleagues) define safe operating limits for Earth-system processes such as climate change, biosphere integrity and nitrogen cycles. Doughnut Economics (Kate Raworth) places a social foundation below and an ecological ceiling above, with the safe and just space for humanity between them. Bhutan's Gross National Happiness is an example of a development philosophy that does not rely on GDP alone, and the Human Development Index (Mahbub ul Haq, 1990, published by UNDP) combines health, education and standard of living.

### 1.3 The eight MDGs (2000 to 2015)

The MDGs were drawn from the Millennium Declaration adopted at the Millennium Summit in 2000 and had 8 goals, 21 targets and about 60 indicators. The target year was 2015 and 1990 was the baseline for measuring progress.

1.  Eradicate extreme poverty and hunger (halve the share of people living on less than \$1.25 a day, earlier stated as \$1 a day)

2.  Achieve universal primary education (every child able to complete a full course of primary schooling)

3.  Promote gender equality and empower women (eliminate gender disparity in education, preferably by 2005 and at all levels by 2015)

4.  Reduce child mortality (cut the under-five mortality rate by two-thirds)

5.  Improve maternal health (cut the maternal mortality ratio by three-quarters)

6.  Combat HIV/AIDS, malaria and other diseases

7.  Ensure environmental sustainability (including reducing biodiversity loss, safe drinking water and sanitation, and improving the lives of at least 100 million slum dwellers by 2020)

8.  Develop a global partnership for development

### 1.4 The seventeen SDGs (2016 to 2030)

The SDGs are set out in the 2030 Agenda titled Transforming Our World. They have 17 goals, 169 targets and about 230 indicators, and they are universal, meaning they apply to all countries. The goals are: 1 No poverty; 2 Zero hunger; 3 Good health and well-being; 4 Quality education; 5 Gender equality; 6 Clean water and sanitation; 7 Affordable and clean energy; 8 Decent work and economic growth; 9 Industry, innovation and infrastructure; 10 Reduced inequalities; 11 Sustainable cities and communities; 12 Responsible consumption and production; 13 Climate action; 14 Life below water; 15 Life on land; 16 Peace, justice and strong institutions; 17 Partnerships for the goals.

The 2030 Agenda is organized around the five Ps: People, Planet, Prosperity, Peace and Partnership. A quick way to remember the environmental SDGs is 6, 7, 12, 13, 14 and 15 (water, energy, consumption, climate, oceans and land), and the social-institutional ones are 1 to 5, 10 and 16.

**Selected target numbers that appear in questions**

- SDG 1: end extreme poverty (people living on less than \$1.25 a day in the original text) for everyone everywhere

- SDG 3: maternal mortality below 70 per 100,000 live births and under-five mortality at least as low as 25 per 1,000 live births, by 2030

- SDG 5: end child, early and forced marriage

- SDG 6: end open defecation (target 6.2); universal and equitable access to safe drinking water (target 6.1)

- SDG 7: substantially increase the share of renewable energy and double the global rate of improvement in energy efficiency

- SDG 8: at least 7 per cent annual GDP growth in the least developed countries; end child labour in all its forms by 2025

- SDG 10: income growth of the bottom 40 per cent of the population

- SDG 12: halve per capita global food waste at retail and consumer level; rationalize inefficient fossil-fuel subsidies

- SDG 16: legal identity for all, including birth registration, by 2030

- SDG 17: developed countries to implement their official development assistance commitment of 0.7 per cent of gross national income

### 1.6 Institutions and India

- Follow-up and review of the SDGs takes place at the High-Level Political Forum on Sustainable Development under the UN Economic and Social Council; it replaced the Commission on Sustainable Development and countries present Voluntary National Reviews there

- The SDG Index and Dashboards report is published by the Sustainable Development Solutions Network (SDSN); the Human Development Report is published by UNDP; the Global Multidimensional Poverty Index is produced by OPHI and UNDP

- India's nodal body for the SDGs is NITI Aayog, which launched the SDG India Index in 2018 to rank states and union territories

### Analysis Angle: Chapter 1

- The SDGs are called integrated and indivisible because progress on one goal affects others. Clean energy (SDG 7) supports health (SDG 3), education (SDG 4) and climate action (SDG 13), while unchecked industrial growth (SDG 8 and 9) can work against SDG 13, 14 and 15. Questions often test this trade-off idea.

- The shift from MDGs to SDGs was a shift from an aid-based donor and recipient model to a shared responsibility model, because rich countries also have to reduce consumption (SDG 12) and emissions (SDG 13).

- Weak sustainability supports technology-led growth, while strong sustainability supports limits on resource use; if an option says natural capital cannot be fully substituted, it describes strong sustainability.

- The Environmental Kuznets Curve is an observation, not a law; it cannot justify delaying environmental protection until a country becomes rich.

- Common trap: Rio+20 launched the SDG process in 2012, but the SDGs were adopted in 2015 and came into effect in 2016, with 2030 as the end year.

## Chapter 2: Human and environment interaction: anthropogenic activities and their impacts

### 2.1 Concept: from adaptation to domination

The relationship between people and the environment has changed from adaptation to domination. Early hunter-gatherers lived within natural limits, agriculture (about 10,000 years ago) began the large-scale transformation of land, and the Industrial Revolution multiplied energy use through coal, oil and gas. Since the middle of the twentieth century the pace of change has become so rapid that scientists describe a 'Great Acceleration' in population, energy use, fertilizer use, urbanization and emissions. Anthropogenic activities are all activities originating from human beings, as opposed to natural processes such as volcanic eruptions, and the word Anthropocene, popularized by Paul Crutzen, names the proposed geological epoch in which human activity is the dominant influence on Earth systems.

A useful way to organize this topic is to ask four questions about each activity: what resource does it use, what waste does it create, what habitat or process does it disturb, and who bears the consequences. The IPAT identity (Impact = Population x Affluence x Technology) summarizes the logic: environmental impact grows with the number of people, the amount each consumes, and the environmental efficiency of the technology used. Garrett Hardin's 'Tragedy of the Commons' (1968) explains why open-access resources such as fisheries, groundwater and the atmosphere are overused when each user gains privately but the cost is shared by all. Rachel Carson's Silent Spring (1962) warned about pesticides such as DDT and began the modern environmental movement, and Jared Diamond's 'evil quartet' (habitat destruction, overexploitation, introduced species and chains of extinction) lists the main drivers of species loss.

### 2.4 Key mechanisms and terms

- Eutrophication: nutrient enrichment (nitrogen and phosphorus) of water bodies leading to algal blooms, oxygen depletion and fish kills

- Biomagnification: increasing concentration of a persistent toxic substance along the food chain; bioaccumulation is build-up within one organism

- Desertification: land degradation in arid, semi-arid and dry sub-humid areas caused mainly by human activities and climatic variation

- Urban heat island: higher temperatures in cities than the surrounding rural areas

- Invasive alien species: water hyacinth (Eichhornia crassipes) and Lantana are standard examples

- Habitat fragmentation isolates populations and is a leading cause of biodiversity loss; the ongoing 'sixth mass extinction' is attributed to human activity

- Natural sources of greenhouse gases (volcanoes, wetlands) exist, but the enhanced greenhouse effect is anthropogenic: fossil fuel burning, cement production (calcination of limestone), deforestation, rice paddies, livestock, landfills and fertilizer use

### Analysis Angle: Chapter 2

- Most environmental impacts are linked in chains, which is why assertion-reason questions are frequent. Example chain: deforestation reduces carbon stored in trees, raises CO2, weakens soil binding, increases erosion and siltation of rivers, and raises flood risk.

- Intensive farming raised food production but shifted the cost to soil and water: groundwater over-extraction lowers water tables, canal irrigation without drainage causes waterlogging and salinity, and fertilizer runoff causes eutrophication. A question that says the Green Revolution had only positive effects is wrong.

- Dams give electricity, irrigation and flood moderation but impose displacement, loss of forests and sediment trapping; the trade-off explains why the Narmada Bachao Andolan became a symbol of development versus displacement.

- Urban impervious surfaces increase runoff and reduce groundwater recharge, so urbanization increases flood risk and also water scarcity.

- The Tragedy of the Commons points to the need for regulation, community management or pricing, not simply to a technical fix.

## Chapter 3: Environmental issues: local, regional and global

### 3.1 Concept: issues by scale and cause

Environmental issues can be sorted by the scale at which their causes and effects operate. Local issues arise from activities in one place and mostly affect that place: garbage piles in a ward, traffic noise, a polluted pond, indoor smoke from biomass stoves. Regional or transboundary issues cross administrative borders but stay within a region: acid rain falling downwind of industrial areas, haze in Southeast Asia from forest and peat fires in Indonesia, river pollution along the Ganga basin, groundwater depletion in north-western India, the Aral Sea. Global issues affect the Earth system as a whole and need international action: climate change, stratospheric ozone depletion, biodiversity loss, ocean acidification and plastic in the oceans. The same pollutant can fall in more than one category, which is why questions usually give clear examples.

### 3.2 Air pollution

Primary pollutants are emitted directly (sulphur dioxide, nitrogen oxides, carbon monoxide, particulate matter, lead, VOCs), while secondary pollutants form in the atmosphere from reactions (ground-level ozone, peroxyacetyl nitrate or PAN, sulphuric acid mist). Particulate matter is classified by size: PM10 (diameter 10 micrometres or less) and PM2.5 (2.5 micrometres or less), and PM2.5 reaches deepest into the lungs. Photochemical smog (Los Angeles type) forms when nitrogen oxides and hydrocarbons react in sunlight; classical or sulphurous smog (London type, the Great Smog of 1952) forms from smoke and SO2 in cold, humid conditions. A temperature inversion traps pollutants near the ground, which explains severe winter smog in the Indo-Gangetic Plain, made worse by stubble burning, vehicles, construction dust and low wind speed. Indoor air pollution (biomass stoves, tobacco smoke, VOCs, radon, mould) is a major health issue in rural homes.

- Acid rain: rainwater with pH below 5.6, caused by SO2 and NOx; harms lakes, forests, soils and marble monuments

- Air (Prevention and Control of Pollution) Act: 1981

- National Air Quality Index (launched 2014): 8 pollutants (PM10, PM2.5, NO2, SO2, CO, O3, NH3, Pb); categories Good, Satisfactory, Moderate, Poor, Very Poor, Severe (0 to 500)

- National Clean Air Programme (2019); Graded Response Action Plan for Delhi-NCR; Bharat Stage VI norms from April 2020; catalytic converters reduce CO, NOx and hydrocarbons; electrostatic precipitators remove particulates; wet scrubbers remove SO2

### 3.3 Water pollution

Water pollution comes from sewage, industrial effluents, agricultural runoff, oil spills, thermal discharge and solid waste. Key indicators are BOD (oxygen needed by microbes to decompose organic matter), COD (oxygen needed for chemical oxidation), dissolved oxygen, turbidity (measured in NTU), hardness (calcium and magnesium salts) and coliform bacteria, with E. coli indicating faecal contamination. Cultural eutrophication is nutrient enrichment by human activity, producing algal blooms, hypoxia and 'dead zones' such as the one in the Gulf of Mexico. Groundwater faces over-extraction, seawater intrusion in coastal aquifers, and geogenic contamination by arsenic (West Bengal and Bangladesh delta) and fluoride. A water-stressed country, by the Falkenmark indicator, has less than 1,700 cubic metres per person per year, and below 1,000 is water scarcity.

- Water (Prevention and Control of Pollution) Act: 1974, which created the Central and State Pollution Control Boards

- Namami Gange Programme (approved 2014), Ganga Action Plan (1985), Jal Jeevan Mission

- MARPOL deals with pollution from ships; ballast water carries invasive species; red tides are dinoflagellate blooms

- Secondary treatment of sewage uses biological processes such as activated sludge

- Tannery effluents (Kanpur) carry chromium

### 3.4 Soil pollution and degradation

Soil is degraded by erosion (water erosion as sheet, rill and gully; wind erosion), salinization and sodification (high evaporation plus irrigation without drainage), waterlogging, acidification, compaction, loss of organic carbon through intensive tillage, and contamination by pesticides, heavy metals (cadmium from phosphate fertilizers), industrial waste and landfill leachate. Water is the main agent of soil erosion in India, and the Chambal ravines are the standard example of gully erosion. Gypsum is used to reclaim sodic soils. Bioremediation uses microbes and phytoremediation uses plants to clean contaminated soil. Persistent organic pollutants are covered by the Stockholm Convention (2001), and endosulfan spraying in Kasaragod (Kerala) became a well-known health tragedy. The Soil Health Card Scheme began in 2015, and contour ploughing, terracing, shelterbelts and zero tillage conserve soil.

### 3.5 Noise pollution

Noise is unwanted sound, measured in decibels (dB) on a logarithmic scale, so an increase of 10 dB means ten times the intensity. Normal conversation is about 60 dB, prolonged exposure above about 85 dB can damage hearing, and the threshold of pain is about 120 to 130 dB. In India the Noise Pollution (Regulation and Control) Rules, 2000 were framed under the Environment (Protection) Act, 1986.

| Area         | Day limit dB(A) | Night limit dB(A) |
|--------------|-----------------|-------------------|
| Industrial   | 75              | 70                |
| Commercial   | 65              | 55                |
| Residential  | 55              | 45                |
| Silence zone | 50              | 40                |

Day time is 6 a.m. to 10 p.m. and night time is 10 p.m. to 6 a.m. A silence zone extends at least 100 metres around hospitals, educational institutions and courts. Green belts and noise barriers reduce urban noise.

### 3.6 Waste

Waste is managed on a hierarchy: prevent and reduce first, then reuse, recycle, recover energy, and dispose in a sanitary landfill last. Landfill gas is mainly methane and carbon dioxide, and leachate pollutes groundwater. Incineration of chlorinated plastics can release dioxins and furans. Composting and vermicomposting (Eisenia fetida) convert organic waste, biogas comes from anaerobic digestion, and pyrolysis is thermal decomposition without oxygen.

- Solid Waste Management Rules, 2016: segregation at source into wet, dry and domestic hazardous waste

- Plastic Waste Management Rules, 2016 with Extended Producer Responsibility; minimum thickness of carry bags raised to 120 microns from 31 December 2022; identified single-use plastic items banned from 1 July 2022

- Bio-medical Waste Management Rules, 2016: yellow bag for human anatomical waste

- E-Waste (Management) Rules, 2016; Seelampur in Delhi is a known informal e-waste hub; Hazardous and Other Wastes Rules, 2016; Basel Convention on transboundary movement of hazardous waste

- Microplastics are plastic particles smaller than 5 mm; the Great Pacific Garbage Patch is in the North Pacific

### 3.7 Climate change

Weather is the short-term state of the atmosphere while climate is the long-term average of weather. The natural greenhouse effect keeps the Earth about 33 degrees Celsius warmer than it would otherwise be (about minus 18 degrees Celsius without it); the enhanced greenhouse effect is the extra warming caused by anthropogenic gases. Carbon dioxide contributes the most to warming because of its quantity, methane is about 25 to 30 times more potent per molecule over 100 years (rice paddies, livestock, landfills), nitrous oxide comes mainly from fertilizer use, and sulphur hexafluoride has the highest global warming potential among common gases. Feedbacks matter: melting ice reduces albedo and absorbs more heat, and thawing permafrost releases methane. Sulphate aerosols have a cooling effect (global dimming), while black carbon from incomplete combustion warms the atmosphere and darkens snow.

- UNFCCC adopted in 1992; IPCC established 1988 by WMO and UNEP; IPCC Special Report on 1.5 degrees Celsius published in 2018

- Impacts: sea level rise (thermal expansion plus ice melt), coral bleaching, ocean acidification from CO2 absorption, heat waves, changing monsoon, glacier retreat in the Himalaya (the 'Third Pole'), loss of crop yields in the tropics, spread of vector-borne diseases, climate-displaced people, threat to low-lying islands such as Tuvalu and the Sundarbans delta

- El Nino is the unusual warming of the eastern and central equatorial Pacific

- India: National Action Plan on Climate Change (2008) with 8 National Missions

- Ozone depletion: CFCs and halons release chlorine and bromine that destroy ozone catalytically; the ozone hole forms over Antarctica; ozone is measured in Dobson units; harmful effects of UV-B include skin cancer and cataract

### Analysis Angle: Chapter 3

- Scale classification is a reasoning test: ask who is affected and whether international cooperation is needed. Climate change and ozone depletion need global action, acid rain and haze need regional agreements, garbage and traffic noise need local action.

- Primary versus secondary pollutant is decided by where the pollutant forms: ozone, PAN and acid mists form by reaction and are secondary.

- Stratospheric ozone protects life, but ground-level ozone is a pollutant; options that call all ozone harmful or all ozone beneficial are wrong.

- Eutrophication links agriculture, water quality and fisheries: fertilizer runoff raises nutrients, algae bloom, bacteria decompose dead algae using oxygen, and fish die from hypoxia.

- Many mitigation measures serve more than one issue: public transport reduces air pollution, noise, and CO2 together; waste segregation reduces landfill methane and groundwater contamination together.

## Chapter 4: Impacts of pollutants on human health

### 4.1 Concept: the exposure chain from source to effect

A pollutant harms health through a chain: source, release, transport in air, water, soil or food, exposure by inhalation, ingestion or skin contact, absorption, and then an effect on a target organ. The size of the effect depends on the dose, the duration of exposure and the vulnerability of the person, which is why toxicology talks about dose-response relationships. Acute exposure is short and intense (the Bhopal gas leak), while chronic exposure is long-term and often low-level (arsenic in drinking water, particulate matter, lead). Children, pregnant women, the elderly and people with existing lung or heart disease are more vulnerable, and children are especially at risk because they breathe more air per unit body weight and their organs are still developing.

Some pollutants are carcinogens (cause cancer), mutagens (cause changes in DNA), teratogens (cause birth defects), endocrine disruptors (interfere with hormones), or neurotoxins (damage the nervous system). The International Agency for Research on Cancer classifies substances in Group 1 when they are carcinogenic to humans, and outdoor air pollution and particulate matter were classified in Group 1 in 2013. Persistent substances (DDT, PCBs, mercury) bioaccumulate in body fat or tissue and biomagnify along the food chain, so top consumers, including humans, carry the highest burdens. Air pollution is generally regarded as the largest single environmental health risk worldwide.

### 4.4 Biological and physical agents

- Waterborne diseases: cholera (Vibrio cholerae), typhoid, hepatitis A, giardiasis (a protozoan), amoebiasis; guinea worm (through Cyclops in water); schistosomiasis (freshwater snails)

- Vector-borne diseases linked with water and climate: malaria (Anopheles), dengue (Aedes aegypti, breeds in clean stagnant water)

- Legionnaires' disease: Legionella bacteria in cooling towers and water systems; aspergillosis: fungal disease of damp indoor spaces

- Heat stroke and heat exhaustion from heat waves; vulnerable groups include outdoor workers, children and the elderly

- Noise: hearing loss, tinnitus, sleep disturbance, hypertension and stress; prolonged exposure above about 85 dB is harmful

- Radiation units: becquerel (radioactivity), gray (absorbed dose), sievert (equivalent dose); radioiodine (I-131) from nuclear accidents raises the risk of thyroid cancer, as seen after Chernobyl

- Occupational lung diseases: coal worker's pneumoconiosis (coal dust), silicosis (silica dust), asbestosis and mesothelioma (asbestos), byssinosis (cotton dust), bagassosis (sugarcane bagasse), farmer's lung (mouldy hay)

- Disasters with health consequences: Bhopal (methyl isocyanate, 1984), Chernobyl (1986), Fukushima (2011), Seveso (dioxin, 1976), Love Canal, Minamata, endosulfan in Kasaragod

### Analysis Angle: Chapter 4

- Link the pollutant to the organ and then to the disease name: mercury to nervous system and Minamata, cadmium to kidney and bone and Itai-itai, fluoride to teeth and bone, nitrate to infant blood and blue baby syndrome, silica to lungs and silicosis. Matching questions use exactly these pairs.

- Persistence, not just toxicity, makes DDT, PCBs and mercury dangerous: they last long, enter fat, and magnify up the food chain, so effects appear far from the source and years later.

- Poverty and exposure are connected. Rural households using biomass stoves, informal e-waste or battery recyclers and communities near mines and tanneries carry the highest burden, so health impacts are also equity issues, which links back to SDG 3, 6, 7 and 10.

- Climate change adds health impacts indirectly: heat stress, spread of vector-borne diseases, food and water insecurity and displacement. An assertion that climate change affects health because it increases heat stress and widens the range of vector-borne diseases is correct.

- Do not mix up units: becquerel measures how many atoms decay, gray measures energy absorbed, sievert measures biological effect; decibel measures sound; Dobson unit measures ozone column; NTU measures turbidity.

## Chapter 5: Natural and energy resources

### 5.1 Concept: renewable and non-renewable resources

Natural resources are materials and conditions provided by nature that people use. They are classified as renewable (replenished on a human timescale, such as solar, wind, hydro, biomass and forests) and non-renewable (fixed stock, such as coal, oil, gas and minerals). Soil sits between the two: it is formed so slowly, often centimetres in hundreds of years, that it is effectively non-renewable on a human timescale. Renewable does not mean inexhaustible or impact-free, because forests can be cut faster than they regrow and hydropower dams change rivers. Energy resources are also grouped as conventional (coal, oil, gas, large hydro, nuclear) and non-conventional or new and renewable (solar, wind, small hydro, biomass, geothermal, tidal and wave).

India has set a target of 500 GW of non-fossil electricity capacity by 2030, and the main drivers are falling solar and wind costs, energy security and climate commitments. The common weaknesses of renewables are intermittency (solar and wind vary with weather), land use, and the need for storage and grid upgrades. Fossil fuels remain dominant because they are dispatchable and energy dense, but they are the main source of CO2 and air pollutants.

### 5.2 Solar energy

Solar energy is used in two ways: photovoltaic (PV) conversion, in which sunlight is directly converted to electricity by semiconductor cells (silicon is the most widely used material), and solar thermal, where sunlight heats water or air or, in concentrated solar power, is focused by mirrors to raise steam for a turbine. The photovoltaic effect is the principle of solar cells. India's advantages are high solar radiation over most of the country and large desert land in Rajasthan and Gujarat.

- Jawaharlal Nehru National Solar Mission launched in 2010

- Bhadla Solar Park in Rajasthan is among the largest in the world

- PM-KUSUM supports solar pumps and solarisation of agriculture; PM Surya Ghar: Muft Bijli Yojana (2024) supports rooftop solar for households

- International Solar Alliance (launched 2015, headquarters Gurugram) works to expand solar use in sunshine countries

### 5.3 Wind energy

Wind turbines convert the kinetic energy of wind into electricity, and the Betz limit says a turbine can extract at most about 59 per cent of the wind's kinetic energy. Wind power is clean in operation but variable, can affect birds and bats, and produces noise and visual impact near habitations. Offshore wind has stronger and steadier winds, and India's National Offshore Wind Energy Policy was notified in 2015 with potential on the Gujarat and Tamil Nadu coasts. Tamil Nadu's Muppandal is a well-known wind farm.

### 5.4 Soil as a resource

Soil forms from parent material through weathering under the influence of climate, organisms, relief (topography) and time. A soil profile has horizons: O (organic), A (topsoil, rich in organic matter and the most fertile), B (subsoil where materials accumulate), C (weathered parent material) and R (bedrock). Most crops do best at a soil pH of about 6 to 7.

| Soil          | Features                                                                      | Where                                |
|---------------|-------------------------------------------------------------------------------|--------------------------------------|
| Alluvial      | Most widespread and fertile soil in India, deposited by rivers                | Indo-Gangetic plain, deltas          |
| Black (regur) | Formed from basalt, retains moisture, suited to cotton                        | Deccan plateau                       |
| Red           | Red colour due to iron oxide, low in nitrogen and humus                       | Peninsular India                     |

Soil conservation methods include contour ploughing, terracing, strip cropping, shelterbelts, afforestation, check dams for gullies, zero tillage and crop rotation. The Soil Health Card Scheme (2015) gives farmers nutrient status and recommendations.

### 5.5 Hydro energy

Hydropower uses the potential energy of stored or flowing water to turn turbines. Run-of-river schemes have little storage and smaller impact, while storage dams give peaking power and irrigation. Pumped-storage plants pump water to a higher reservoir when power is cheap and release it at peak demand, so they function as large batteries. In India, small hydro projects are those up to 25 MW. Hydropower impacts include submergence of land and forest, displacement of people, sedimentation, blocked fish migration, altered downstream flow, and reservoir-induced seismicity (Koyna).

- Tehri Dam on the Bhagirathi (Uttarakhand), the tallest dam in India; Bhakra Nangal on the Sutlej

- Three Gorges Dam in China is the world's largest hydropower station by installed capacity

- Arunachal Pradesh has the largest untapped hydropower potential in India

### 5.6 Geothermal energy

Geothermal energy is heat from within the Earth, tapped from hot water, steam or hot rock using wells. Conventional hydrothermal plants use natural steam or hot water, while Enhanced Geothermal Systems (EGS) create reservoirs in hot dry rock. It gives steady, round-the-clock power but is site-specific and can release hydrogen sulphide and cause induced seismicity. The world's first geothermal power plant was at Larderello, Italy (1904), Iceland is a leader in geothermal heating, and The Geysers in California is a large field. Indian geothermal provinces include Puga in Ladakh, Manikaran in Himachal Pradesh and Tattapani in Chhattisgarh.

### 5.7 Biomass energy

Biomass is organic matter of plant and animal origin that can be burned directly (wood, dung, crop residue), converted to gas, or converted to liquid fuels. It is considered close to carbon neutral because the CO2 released was recently absorbed by plants, but this holds only if harvesting is sustainable. Open burning of residue and traditional stoves cause serious air pollution.

- Biogas (about 55 to 65 per cent methane) is produced by anaerobic digestion of dung and organic waste; gasification of biomass gives producer gas; briquettes and pellets are densified biomass

- Ethanol (blended with petrol) from sugarcane and grains, and biodiesel from non-edible oils such as Jatropha, are biofuels

- GOBARdhan (2018) turns cattle dung and agricultural waste into biogas and compost; SATAT (2018) promotes compressed biogas

### 5.8 Nuclear energy

Nuclear fission splits heavy nuclei, mainly uranium-235, releasing energy and neutrons that sustain a chain reaction; a moderator (heavy water or graphite) slows neutrons and a coolant removes heat. Nuclear fusion joins light nuclei such as deuterium and tritium, powers the Sun, and is not yet commercial. Nuclear power has very low operating greenhouse gas emissions but raises concerns about accidents (Three Mile Island 1979, Chernobyl 1986, Fukushima 2011), radioactive waste and proliferation. High-level waste is immobilized by vitrification and kept in deep geological repositories.

- India's three-stage nuclear programme, conceived by Homi J. Bhabha, aims to use thorium: Stage 1 pressurized heavy water reactors, Stage 2 fast breeder reactors, Stage 3 thorium-based reactors

- Tarapur (Maharashtra) is India's first nuclear power station (1969); Kudankulam is in Tamil Nadu; the Nuclear Power Corporation of India Limited operates the fleet

### 5.9 Forests

Forests provide timber and non-timber products, regulate climate through carbon storage, protect watersheds and soil, support biodiversity, and are home to tribal and forest-dependent communities. Indian forests are broadly of types such as tropical evergreen, tropical dry deciduous (teak), thorn, montane, and mangrove (Sundarbans). Threats include conversion to agriculture, mining, infrastructure, fire, grazing and illegal felling.

- Forest Survey of India publishes the India State of Forest Report every two years

- National Forest Policy, 1988: target of one-third (33 per cent) of the geographical area under forest or tree cover, and two-thirds in hills

- Forest (Conservation) Act, 1980 regulates diversion of forest land; Forest Rights Act, 2006 recognizes the rights of forest-dwelling Scheduled Tribes and other traditional forest dwellers; Joint Forest Management involves local communities in protection

- Madhya Pradesh has the largest forest cover by area; Mizoram has the highest forest cover as a percentage of its area

- REDD+ rewards reduction of emissions from deforestation and forest degradation

### Analysis Angle: Chapter 5

- Renewable is not the same as environmentally harmless. Large hydro displaces people, biomass burning pollutes air, and solar parks need land; exam options that say renewables have no impact are wrong.

- Intermittency is the main technical limit for solar and wind, which is why storage, pumped hydro and grid balancing are linked with their expansion.

- Geothermal and hydro give firm power, solar and wind give variable power, and nuclear gives base-load power with waste and safety issues.

- Soil, water and forests are linked: forest loss increases erosion, siltation of reservoirs and floods, which reduces dam life and farm productivity.

- Energy transition is also a development issue, connecting SDG 7 with SDG 13 and with schemes such as Ujjwala and PM-KUSUM.

## Chapter 6: Natural hazards and disasters: mitigation strategies

### 6.1 Concept: hazard, exposure, vulnerability and risk

A hazard is a potentially damaging physical event, phenomenon or human activity, such as an earthquake, a cyclone or a chemical leak. Exposure is the presence of people, property and systems in the path of a hazard, vulnerability is the degree to which they are likely to be harmed, and capacity is the strength and resources available to cope. A disaster occurs when a hazard strikes a vulnerable and exposed community that cannot cope with its own resources. This is summarized as Risk = Hazard x Vulnerability / Capacity, which shows that risk can be reduced even when hazards cannot be prevented, by reducing vulnerability and increasing capacity. That is the logic of mitigation and of disaster risk reduction (DRR): the same cyclone kills far fewer people in a place with warning systems, shelters and strong houses than in a place without them.

Hazards are classified as geophysical (earthquake, volcano, tsunami, landslide), hydro-meteorological (cyclone, flood, drought, heat wave, cold wave, lightning, hailstorm, avalanche), climatological (wildfire), biological (epidemic, pest attacks), and human-made or technological (industrial and chemical accidents, nuclear accidents, oil spills, fires, stampedes). Disaster management is described as a cycle of four phases: mitigation (reducing or limiting adverse effects, before the event), preparedness (plans, training, early warning, stockpiles), response (rescue, relief, first aid) and recovery (rehabilitation and reconstruction, ideally 'build back better').

### 6.2 Earthquakes and tsunamis

- Focus (hypocentre) is the point of origin inside the Earth; epicentre is the point on the surface directly above it; instrument is the seismograph

- Seismic waves: P waves (primary, fastest, compressional), S waves (secondary, shear, cannot pass through liquids), surface waves (slowest, cause most damage)

- Richter scale measures magnitude and is logarithmic; Modified Mercalli scale measures intensity (I to XII) from observed effects

- India's seismic zones: Zones II, III, IV and V, with Zone V the most hazardous (Himalaya, north-east, Rann of Kachchh); the Himalaya is earthquake-prone because the Indian plate is colliding with the Eurasian plate

- Notable Indian earthquakes: Uttarkashi 1991, Latur (Killari) 1993, Bhuj 2001 (26 January), Kashmir 2005

- Indian standards: IS 1893 for earthquake-resistant design, with IS 4326 and IS 13920 for construction and ductile detailing

- Tsunami: sea waves generated mostly by submarine earthquakes (also landslides and volcanoes); the 26 December 2004 Indian Ocean tsunami followed an earthquake off Sumatra; the Indian Tsunami Early Warning Centre is at INCOIS, Hyderabad

- Ring of Fire is the volcanic and seismic belt around the Pacific; Barren Island in the Andaman Sea is India's only active volcano

### 6.3 Cyclones, floods, droughts, landslides and others

- Tropical cyclones are called hurricanes in the Atlantic, typhoons in the north-west Pacific and cyclones in the Indian Ocean; in India warnings come from the India Meteorological Department

- The main killer in cyclones is the storm surge, a rise in sea level caused by strong winds and low pressure; the Odisha Super Cyclone occurred in October 1999

- Floods: the Kosi is called the Sorrow of Bihar and the Brahmaputra causes yearly floods in Assam; flash floods are rapid-onset floods; Glacial Lake Outburst Floods (GLOF) occur when glacial lake dams fail; Kedarnath (2013, Uttarakhand) combined heavy rain, flash floods and landslides

- Drought types: meteorological (rainfall deficiency), hydrological (low streamflow and groundwater), agricultural (soil moisture and crop failure), socio-economic

- Landslides occur mostly in the Himalaya, north-east and Western Ghats; human triggers include slope cutting for roads, deforestation and blocking of natural drainage

- Heat waves: Ahmedabad's Heat Action Plan (2013) was the first in South Asia; forest fires are controlled by fire lines and firebreaks

### Analysis Angle: Chapter 6

- Mitigation versus preparedness is a frequent trap. Mitigation reduces the cause or the damage in advance (building codes, embankments, land-use rules), while preparedness is about readiness to act (warning, drills, stockpiles). Relief distribution belongs to response, and reconstruction to recovery.

- Structural measures use engineering; non-structural measures use policy, planning, knowledge and behaviour. Flood plain zoning is non-structural, an embankment is structural.

- Early warning is among the most cost-effective measures. The fall in cyclone deaths in Odisha after 1999 reflects forecasting, evacuation and shelters, not a change in the cyclone itself.

- Human action turns natural hazards into disasters: deforestation raises landslide and flood risk, building on flood plains and coasts raises exposure, and poor construction turns earthquakes into mass-casualty events. This is why the DM Act and Sendai stress risk governance and community participation.

- Climate change increases the frequency and intensity of hydro-meteorological hazards (heat waves, extreme rain, cyclones, GLOFs), so disaster risk reduction and climate adaptation are now planned together.

## Chapter 7: The Environment (Protection) Act, 1986

### 7.1 Concept: why India needed an umbrella law

Before 1986 India had separate laws on water (1974), air (1981) and forests (1980), but no single law that could deal with all environmental problems, fill gaps between those laws, and give the Central Government wide powers to act quickly. The Bhopal gas tragedy of December 1984, in which methyl isocyanate leaked from a pesticide plant, exposed these gaps. The Environment (Protection) Act (EPA) was enacted in 1986 to implement the decisions taken at the United Nations Conference on the Human Environment (Stockholm, 1972), under Article 253 of the Constitution, which lets Parliament legislate to implement international decisions. It extends to the whole of India and is often described as an umbrella or enabling legislation, because it authorizes the Central Government to make rules, issue directions and set standards on almost any aspect of environmental protection, rather than listing detailed technical limits itself. The Ministry of Environment, Forest and Climate Change is the administering ministry.

The Act is short (26 sections) and has four chapters: Preliminary (sections 1 and 2), General Powers of the Central Government (sections 3 to 6), Prevention, Control and Abatement of Environmental Pollution (sections 7 to 17), and Miscellaneous (sections 18 to 26). Its central idea is that the Central Government may take all measures it considers necessary or expedient to protect and improve the quality of the environment and to prevent, control and abate environmental pollution.

### 7.2 Key definitions (Section 2)

- Environment includes water, air and land and the inter-relationship which exists among and between water, air and land, and human beings, other living creatures, plants, micro-organisms and property

- Environmental pollutant is any solid, liquid or gaseous substance present in such concentration as may be, or tend to be, injurious to the environment

- Environmental pollution is the presence in the environment of any environmental pollutant

- Hazardous substance is any substance or preparation which, by reason of its chemical or physico-chemical properties or handling, is liable to cause harm to human beings, other living creatures, plants, micro-organisms, property or the environment

- Handling, in relation to any substance, includes manufacture, processing, treatment, package, storage, transportation, use, collection, destruction, conversion, offering for sale, transfer or the like

### Analysis Angle: Chapter 7

- The EP Act is a framework law. Section 3 gives general powers, Section 5 gives direction-making power, and Section 6 allows rule-making, so most of the actual standards and procedures are in rules and notifications, not in the Act. Questions that ask where emission standards or EIA procedure come from therefore point to the rules and notifications made under the Act.

- The Act is Central-government centred, whereas the Water and Air Acts work through pollution control boards. This is why the EP Act is said to fill gaps and give a single point of coordination.

- Absolute liability and the polluter pays principle make the polluter responsible for compensation and restoration; the precautionary principle shifts the burden of proof to the developer when serious environmental harm is possible.

- Common weakness noted in analysis: enforcement depends heavily on executive action and on limited inspection capacity, and criticism of weaker penalties is a continuing theme in debates on the Act.

- Trap to avoid: The Water Act 1974 created the pollution control boards and the Air Act 1981 extended their powers to air; the EP Act 1986 came after Bhopal and implements the Stockholm decisions.

## Chapter 8: International agreements and efforts

### 8.1 Concept: why environmental problems need global agreements

Environmental problems such as ozone depletion, climate change and biodiversity loss ignore national borders, and the atmosphere, oceans and many species are global commons, so no country can solve them alone. International environmental law works in a typical sequence: a framework convention sets the objective and institutions (for example the Vienna Convention, the UNFCCC and the CBD), and later protocols or agreements add binding commitments (the Montreal Protocol, the Kyoto Protocol, the Paris Agreement). The Conference of the Parties (COP) is the supreme body of each convention and meets regularly. Several principles run through these treaties: common but differentiated responsibilities (CBDR), because developed countries caused most historical emissions and have more capacity, the precautionary principle, the polluter pays principle, sustainable development, and intergenerational equity.

### 8.3 Montreal Protocol

The Montreal Protocol on Substances that Deplete the Ozone Layer was adopted on 16 September 1987 (observed as World Ozone Day) under the Vienna Convention and entered into force on 1 January 1989. It controls the production and consumption of ozone-depleting substances (ODS): CFCs, halons, carbon tetrachloride, methyl chloroform, HCFCs (transitional substitutes with lower ozone depletion potential) and methyl bromide. The reference substance for the Ozone Depleting Potential scale is CFC-11 with a value of 1. The Protocol works through differentiated timetables, with developing countries (Article 5 parties) given a grace period, and through the Multilateral Fund (set up under the London Amendment of 1990) which pays the agreed incremental costs of developing countries. It was the first treaty ratified by every UN member state and is widely regarded as the most successful environmental treaty, with the ozone layer expected to recover to 1980 levels around the middle of the century (about the 2060s over Antarctica).

- Amendments: London 1990 (Multilateral Fund), Copenhagen 1992 (faster phase-out, HCFCs and methyl bromide added), Montreal 1997 (licensing), Beijing 1999, Kigali 2016

- The Kigali Amendment (adopted October 2016, in force 1 January 2019) phases down hydrofluorocarbons (HFCs), which do not harm ozone but are potent greenhouse gases; India ratified in 2021 and its schedule begins with a freeze in 2028 and reaches about an 85 per cent reduction by 2047

- India phased out CFC production and consumption by 2010 and is now phasing out HCFCs under the Protocol

### 8.4 Rio Earth Summit (1992)

The United Nations Conference on Environment and Development (UNCED), the Earth Summit, was held in Rio de Janeiro, Brazil, from 3 to 14 June 1992, with Maurice Strong as Secretary-General, to link environment with development twenty years after Stockholm. It produced the Rio Declaration on Environment and Development (27 principles), Agenda 21 (a non-binding action plan with 40 chapters), a non-binding Statement of Forest Principles, and opened two treaties for signature: the UNFCCC and the Convention on Biological Diversity. A third Rio Convention, the UNCCD on desertification, followed in 1994. The Commission on Sustainable Development was created to monitor follow-up and was later replaced by the High-Level Political Forum.

- Rio Declaration: Principle 7 (CBDR), Principle 10 (public participation and access to information), Principle 15 (precautionary approach), Principle 16 (polluter pays and internalizing environmental costs), Principle 17 (environmental impact assessment)

- Follow-up: Johannesburg World Summit on Sustainable Development (2002), Rio+20 (2012, The Future We Want), which launched the SDG process

- Not an outcome of Rio: the Kyoto Protocol (1997) and the Montreal Protocol (1987)

### 8.5 Convention on Biological Diversity (CBD)

The CBD text was adopted in Nairobi in May 1992 (22 May, observed as the International Day for Biological Diversity), opened for signature at Rio in June 1992 and entered into force on 29 December 1993. It has three objectives: conservation of biological diversity, sustainable use of its components, and fair and equitable sharing of the benefits arising from the use of genetic resources. Its Secretariat is in Montreal. Parties meet at COPs, and the 11th COP was held in Hyderabad in 2012.

- Cartagena Protocol on Biosafety (adopted 2000, in force 2003): safe transfer, handling and use of living modified organisms (LMOs) from biotechnology

- Nagoya Protocol on Access and Benefit Sharing (adopted at COP10, Nagoya, 2010; in force 2014)

- Aichi Biodiversity Targets (2011 to 2020): 20 targets under a strategic plan adopted at Nagoya

- Kunming-Montreal Global Biodiversity Framework (adopted 2022 at COP15): 23 targets for 2030, including the '30 by 30' goal to protect 30 per cent of land and sea and restore 30 per cent of degraded ecosystems

- India: Biological Diversity Act, 2002 gives effect to the CBD, with the National Biodiversity Authority (Chennai), State Biodiversity Boards and local Biodiversity Management Committees

- Related conventions: Ramsar (wetlands, 1971), World Heritage (1972), CITES (trade in endangered species, 1973), Bonn Convention on migratory species (1979), Basel (1989), Rotterdam (1998), Stockholm POPs (2001), Minamata Convention on Mercury (adopted 2013, in force 2017)

### 8.6 Kyoto Protocol

The Kyoto Protocol to the UNFCCC was adopted in December 1997 at COP3 in Kyoto, Japan, and entered into force on 16 February 2005 after Russia's ratification brought it over the required threshold. It set legally binding emission targets only for developed (Annex I) countries, averaging about 5 per cent below 1990 levels over the first commitment period 2008 to 2012, and it covered six greenhouse gases: CO2, methane, nitrous oxide, HFCs, PFCs and sulphur hexafluoride. Developing countries, including India and China, had no binding targets, reflecting CBDR. The United States signed but never ratified, and Canada withdrew in 2011. The Doha Amendment (2012) set a second commitment period from 2013 to 2020.

- Three flexibility mechanisms: Emissions Trading, Joint Implementation (JI) between Annex I countries, and the Clean Development Mechanism (CDM), under which an Annex I country invests in an emission-reduction project in a developing country and earns Certified Emission Reductions (CERs)

- India ratified Kyoto in 2002 and was a major CDM host country

- Approach: top-down, with targets negotiated and assigned to countries

### 8.7 Paris Agreement

The Paris Agreement was adopted on 12 December 2015 at COP21 and entered into force on 4 November 2016. Its long-term goal is to hold the rise in global average temperature to well below 2 degrees Celsius above pre-industrial levels and to pursue efforts to limit it to 1.5 degrees Celsius. Unlike Kyoto, every country submits its own Nationally Determined Contribution (NDC), updated every five years with the expectation of increasing ambition (a ratchet), and progress is reviewed through a Global Stocktake every five years (the first concluded in 2023). It also covers adaptation, loss and damage, climate finance (developed countries' goal of mobilizing 100 billion US dollars a year from 2020), an enhanced transparency framework, and Article 6 on carbon markets and cooperative approaches.

| Feature              | Kyoto Protocol                     | Paris Agreement                              |
|----------------------|------------------------------------|----------------------------------------------|
| Adopted and in force | 1997; 2005                         | 2015; 2016                                   |
| Who has targets      | Annex I (developed) countries only | All countries through NDCs                   |
| Approach             | Top-down, assigned targets         | Bottom-up, nationally determined             |
| Time frames          | 2008 to 2012, then 2013 to 2020    | Five-year cycles, long-term temperature goal |
| Review               | Compliance mechanism               | Transparency framework and Global Stocktake  |
| Mechanisms           | ET, JI, CDM                        | Article 6 mechanisms                         |

- India's commitments: Panchamrit announced at COP26 (Glasgow, 2021): 500 GW non-fossil electricity capacity by 2030, 50 per cent of energy requirements from renewables by 2030, reduce projected emissions by 1 billion tonnes by 2030, cut the emissions intensity of GDP by 45 per cent by 2030 from the 2005 level, and reach net zero by 2070; Mission LiFE promotes sustainable lifestyles

- Later COP milestones: Glasgow 2021 (COP26), Sharm el-Sheikh 2022 (COP27, agreement to create a loss and damage fund), Dubai 2023 (COP28, first Global Stocktake and a call for transitioning away from fossil fuels), Baku 2024 (COP29, new collective climate finance goal and carbon market rules)

### 8.8 International Solar Alliance (ISA)

The International Solar Alliance was launched jointly by India and France on 30 November 2015 at COP21 in Paris. It is a treaty-based intergovernmental organization headquartered in Gurugram, India; its Framework Agreement opened for signature in 2016 and entered into force on 6 December 2017. It focuses on 'sunshine countries' lying fully or partially between the Tropic of Cancer and the Tropic of Capricorn, and a later amendment opened membership to all UN member states. Its aim is to reduce the cost of solar technology and finance, promote large-scale solar deployment and energy access, and mobilize about 1 trillion US dollars of investment by 2030 under the 'Towards 1000' vision. India also proposed the 'One Sun, One World, One Grid' (OSOWOG) initiative to interlink solar power grids across regions.

### Analysis Angle: Chapter 8

- Framework versus protocol is the key conceptual pair: the UNFCCC, CBD and Vienna Convention set objectives and institutions, while the Montreal Protocol, Kyoto Protocol and Paris Agreement and the Cartagena and Nagoya Protocols create specific obligations.

- The shift from Kyoto to Paris was a response to Kyoto's limited coverage: major emitters in the developing world and the United States were outside binding targets. Paris broadened participation but depends on national ambition, so a gap exists between pledges and the temperature goal.

- Montreal succeeded because substitutes existed, the controlled substances were limited, trade measures and a financial mechanism supported compliance, and there were clear timetables. Climate change is harder because fossil fuels underpin whole economies.

- Kigali shows how the ozone and climate regimes are linked: replacing CFCs with HFCs saved ozone but added warming, so HFCs are now being phased down.

- India's positions reflect CBDR and climate justice: historical responsibility of developed countries, the right to development, climate finance and technology transfer, with ISA and LiFE as leadership initiatives.

- Typical traps: Cartagena concerns biosafety whereas Nagoya concerns access and benefit sharing; Rio produced the UNFCCC and CBD but not Kyoto; the ISA was launched at Paris in 2015, not Marrakech; and Kyoto's first period ended in 2012 and its second was 2013 to 2020.

## Chapter 9: Syllabus Map, Question Framing and Study Method

### 9.1 Unit Overview and Official Syllabus

Unit IX (People, Development and Environment) usually contributes about five questions to UGC NET Paper 1, so it is a high-return unit: the syllabus is small, the facts repeat across years, and most questions can be answered by anyone who has fixed the numbers, years and names given here. These notes follow the official syllabus order. Every topic is written in three layers so that you can prepare for the three kinds of questions the exam asks: Concept (paragraphs that explain how and why), Memory (bullet lists and tables of facts, years, numbers and names to be learned by heart) and Analysis (cause-and-effect points that help with statement-based, assertion-reason and 'which is correct' questions).

- Development and environment: Millennium Development Goals and Sustainable Development Goals

- Human and environment interaction: anthropogenic activities and their impacts on environment

- Environmental issues: local, regional and global; air, water, soil, noise, waste and climate change and their impacts on human life

- Impacts of pollutants on human health

- Natural and energy resources: solar, wind, soil, hydro, geothermal, biomass, nuclear and forests

- Natural hazards and disasters: mitigation strategies

- Environmental Protection Act (1986)

- International agreements and efforts: Montreal Protocol, Rio Summit, Convention on Biodiversity, Kyoto Protocol, Paris Agreement, International Solar Alliance

### 9.2 How Questions Are Usually Framed

- Direct memory: 'The Montreal Protocol was adopted in...', 'Which SDG deals with clean energy?'

- Matching (List-I and List-II): disaster or disease with cause, section with subject, convention with theme

- Statement I and II: two statements on a law, treaty or process, where one detail (a year, a gas, a body) is deliberately wrong

- Assertion and Reason: usually cause and effect, such as deforestation and CO2 or fertilizer runoff and eutrophication

- Chronological order: Stockholm, Rio, Kyoto, Millennium Summit, Paris, SDGs, Sendai

- Application and analysis: which measure reduces a hazard, which gas links to which impact, which law covers which situation

### 9.3 Study Method That Works for This Unit

1.  Read the Concept paragraph of a topic once, slowly, so the logic is clear.

2.  Memorize the Memory lists and tables of that topic in a single sitting, then revise them the next day.

3.  Read the Analysis points and try to restate each in your own words, because exam options are often disguised versions of these cause-and-effect links.

4.  Finish with the Revision toolkit at the end, which gathers timelines, comparison tables and common traps in one place.

5.  Watch for near-identical pairs, which are the commonest source of lost marks: Kyoto and Paris, Montreal and Vienna, Water Act and Air Act, Richter and Mercalli, MDG and SDG, Cartagena and Nagoya.

### 10.4 Cross-Topic Chains for Analysis Questions

- Fossil fuels: burning coal and oil gives CO2 (climate change, ocean acidification, sea level rise), SO2 and NOx (acid rain, respiratory disease), particulates (heart and lung disease), and fly ash (soil and water). The response sits in SDG 7 and 13, the Paris Agreement, renewable energy, ISA and Bharat Stage norms.

- Agriculture: fertilizers give nitrous oxide and nitrate runoff (eutrophication, blue baby syndrome), paddies and livestock give methane, irrigation gives salinity and groundwater loss, pesticides give biomagnification. The response is soil health cards, organic farming, zero tillage and efficient irrigation.

- Forests: deforestation reduces carbon storage, increases erosion, floods and landslides, destroys habitat, and affects tribal livelihoods; the response is the Forest Conservation Act, Forest Rights Act, JFM, REDD+ and SDG 15.

- Cities: impervious surfaces cause floods, vehicles and construction cause smog, waste causes landfill methane and leachate, and noise adds stress; the response is the Solid Waste Rules, NCAP, GRAP and SDG 11.

- Hazards: vulnerability, not just the hazard, causes disasters; mitigation, early warning, building codes and community preparedness reduce risk under the DM Act and the Sendai Framework.

- Law and treaties: the EP Act implements Stockholm and creates the rule-making framework; the CBD led to the Biological Diversity Act; Basel connects to the Hazardous Waste Rules; Montreal leads to the ODS Rules, and Paris connects with the NAPCC and India's NDC.
