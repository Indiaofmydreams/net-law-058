Data Interpretation is Unit 7 of UGC NET Paper 1 and contributes 5 questions (10 marks) out of 50. It is the most predictable unit in the paper: the 5 questions usually come from one data set, so a student who practises calculation speed can score close to full marks in about 8 to 10 minutes. This document assumes "Chapter 1" in your request means this unit; confirm the unit number against your own syllabus copy.

### 0.3 Trends you should expect

In most recent papers, the 5 questions are built on a single table, bar graph, line graph or pie chart, and sometimes on two linked charts. The questions ask for a percentage, a ratio, an average, a difference, a growth rate, or the highest and lowest value. Occasionally a paper replaces the data set with direct concept questions, for example on qualitative versus quantitative data, primary versus secondary sources, or e-governance and open data. I have not verified the exact split of calculation versus concept questions in every shift, so treat this as the typical pattern and confirm it against previous-year papers.

The difficulty is calculation-heavy rather than conceptually deep. Marks are lost through three habits: misreading units (thousands, lakhs, crores), using the wrong base for a percentage, and spending too long on exact division when the options are far apart.

### 0.5 Time strategy

- Give the whole unit 8 to 10 minutes, about 2 minutes per question.

- Read the title, axis labels, legend and units before the first question. Most wrong answers come from skipping this step.

## Chapter 1: Data: Meaning, Characteristics and Types

Data are raw facts, figures, observations or measurements collected for reference or analysis. On their own they carry little meaning: the number "72" is data, but "72 per cent of students passed" is information. Information is data that has been processed, organised and given context. Knowledge is information combined with understanding and experience, and wisdom is the judgement to apply it well. This ladder (data, information, knowledge, wisdom) is a favourite one-line MCQ.

### 1.1 Characteristics of good data

Data are useful for decision making only when they have these qualities:

- **Accuracy:** free from error and close to the true value.

- **Relevance:** related to the question being studied.

- **Timeliness:** available when the decision is needed, and not out of date.

- **Completeness:** no important cases or variables missing.

- **Consistency:** the same definitions and units are used throughout.

- **Reliability:** repeated collection gives similar results.

- **Comparability:** can be compared across places and periods.

### 1.2 Types of data by form

Quantitative data are numerical and measurable, such as marks, income and age. Qualitative data are descriptive and non-numerical, such as opinion, gender, caste and colour. Topic 4 covers this split in detail.

### 1.3 Types of data by source

Primary data are collected first-hand by the researcher for the study at hand. Secondary data were collected earlier by someone else for another purpose and are reused. Topic 2 covers this in detail.

### 1.4 Types of data by time dimension

This classification is often tested through examples, so learn the example with each type.

| Type                 | Meaning                                  | Example                                                  |
|----------------------|------------------------------------------|----------------------------------------------------------|
| Cross-sectional      | Many units observed at one point in time | Literacy rate of all 28 states in 2024                   |
| Time series          | One unit observed over many time points  | India's GDP for every year from 2000 to 2024             |
| Panel (longitudinal) | The same units followed over time        | The same 500 households surveyed every year for 10 years |

A quick way to remember: cross-section is a snapshot, time series is a film of one thing, and panel is a film of many things.

### 1.5 Other useful terms

- **Population (universe):** the entire group under study.

- **Sample:** the part of the population actually studied.

- **Parameter:** a numerical value describing a population (for example the true population mean).

- **Statistic:** a numerical value describing a sample (for example the sample mean). Memory aid: **P**arameter goes with **P**opulation, **S**tatistic goes with **S**ample.

- **Unit and variable:** the unit is the object observed (a student); the variable is the characteristic measured (marks).

- **Raw data versus array:** raw data are unordered; an array is the same data sorted in ascending or descending order.

- **Metadata:** data about data, such as the source, date, definition and unit of a data set.

### 1.6 Worked examples

**Example 1.** A study records the monthly rainfall of Ahmedabad for 12 months of 2025. Which type of data is this? *Answer: time series, because one place is observed over several time points.*

**Example 2.** A survey records the monthly household income of 200 families in one city in a single week. Which type of data is this? *Answer: cross-sectional, because many units are observed at one time.*

**Example 3.** "The average height of all students in a university is 165 cm" is a parameter, and "the average height of 50 selected students is 163 cm" is a statistic.

## Chapter 2: Sources and Acquisition of Data

Acquisition of data means the process of collecting data from the field or from existing records. The first decision a researcher makes is whether to collect fresh data (primary) or to use data that already exist (secondary). The choice depends on cost, time, the exact fit with the research problem, and the need for control over quality.

### 2.1 Primary data and methods of collection

Primary data are original, collected first-hand and specific to the study. The main methods are:

- **Observation:** the researcher watches and records behaviour. It can be structured or unstructured, and participant (the observer joins the group) or non-participant.

- **Interview:** a face-to-face or telephone conversation. It can be structured (fixed questions), semi-structured, or unstructured (open conversation).

- **Questionnaire:** a set of written questions that the respondent fills in personally. It is cheap, reaches a wide area and avoids interviewer bias, but response rates are low and it needs literate respondents.

- **Schedule:** a set of questions filled in by the investigator while meeting the respondent. It suits illiterate respondents and gives a higher response rate, but costs more.

- **Experiment:** data generated by manipulating one variable and observing the effect on another.

- **Survey:** collecting data from a large group, either by census or by sample.

- **Focus group discussion and case study:** in-depth qualitative methods that explore a topic with a small group or a single case.

### 2.2 Secondary data and their sources

Secondary data come from published or unpublished records.

- **Published, government:** Census of India, National Statistical Office (NSO, formerly NSSO) surveys, Economic Survey, National Family Health Survey (NFHS), UDISE+, All India Survey on Higher Education (AISHE), and Reserve Bank of India reports.

- **Published, international:** UN, World Bank, WHO, UNESCO, IMF.

- **Published, other:** research journals, books, newspapers, trade and business reports.

- **Unpublished:** diaries, letters, institutional files, office records.

Before using secondary data, check the compiler's purpose, definitions used, date of collection, and possible bias. This checking step is called assessing the suitability and reliability of the data.

### 2.3 Primary versus secondary data

| Basis            | Primary data          | Secondary data                   |
|------------------|-----------------------|----------------------------------|
| Originality      | Fresh and original    | Already available                |
| Cost and time    | High                  | Low                              |
| Fit with problem | Tailor-made           | May not fit exactly              |
| Quality control  | With the researcher   | Depends on the original compiler |
| Example          | Your own field survey | Census tables                    |

### 2.4 Questionnaire versus schedule

| Basis              | Questionnaire  | Schedule                    |
|--------------------|----------------|-----------------------------|
| Filled by          | Respondent     | Investigator                |
| Respondent must be | Literate       | Can be illiterate           |
| Response rate      | Low            | High                        |
| Cost               | Low            | High                        |
| Area covered       | Wide           | Limited                     |
| Sent by            | Post or online | Carried by the investigator |

### 2.5 Census versus sample survey

A census is complete enumeration of every unit in the population. India's decadal Population Census is the standard example. It gives full detail but costs a great deal and takes years. A sample survey studies a selected part of the population and infers about the whole. It is faster and cheaper, but results carry sampling error.

- **Probability sampling** (every unit has a known chance of selection): simple random, systematic, stratified, cluster and multistage sampling.

- **Non-probability sampling** (selection is not by chance): convenience, purposive (judgement), quota and snowball sampling.

Only probability sampling allows a sampling error to be estimated. Stratified sampling divides the population into homogeneous groups (strata) and samples from each. Cluster sampling divides it into natural groups (clusters) and samples whole clusters. Snowball sampling is used for hard-to-reach groups where existing respondents refer the next ones.

### 2.6 Worked examples

**Example 1.** A researcher visits 300 farmers and records their answers on a form by speaking to each. Which tool is used? *Answer: a schedule, because the investigator fills it.*

**Example 2.** A student uses NFHS tables to study child nutrition without meeting any child. Which data are these? *Answer: secondary data.*

**Example 3.** A university wants the opinion of every one of its 120 faculty members. Which approach is this? *Answer: census (complete enumeration), because every unit is covered.*

## Chapter 3: Classification of Data

Classification is the process of arranging data into groups or classes according to common characteristics. Raw data are a heap of numbers; classified data are concise, comparable and ready for tabulation and graphing. Classification is the first step after collection and editing, and the step before tabulation.

### 3.1 Objectives of classification

- Condense large data into a small, understandable form.

- Bring out similarities and differences between groups.

- Make comparison possible.

- Prepare data for tabulation, graphs and statistical analysis.

### 3.2 Types of classification

| Type                     | Basis                                      | Example                                       |
|--------------------------|--------------------------------------------|-----------------------------------------------|
| Geographical (spatial)   | Place                                      | State-wise population, district-wise rainfall |
| Chronological (temporal) | Time                                       | Year-wise student enrolment                   |
| Qualitative              | Attribute (cannot be measured numerically) | Gender, religion, marital status, literacy    |
| Quantitative             | Measurable variable                        | Age groups, income groups, marks ranges       |

Quantitative classification gives a frequency distribution. Qualitative classification gives groups by attribute and can be simple (two classes such as male and female, called dichotomous) or manifold (many classes).

### 3.3 Variable and attribute

A **variable** is a characteristic that can take different numerical values. A **discrete variable** takes only whole or separate values (number of students, number of cars). A **continuous variable** can take any value in a range (height, weight, time, temperature). An **attribute** is a qualitative characteristic that is described, not measured (honesty, colour, religion).

### 3.4 Frequency distribution: key terms

- **Class interval:** the range of one group, for example 10 to 20.

- **Class limits:** the lower limit and upper limit of a class.

- **Class width (size):** upper limit minus lower limit.

- **Class mark (midpoint):** the average of the two limits.

- **Frequency:** the number of observations in a class.

- **Cumulative frequency:** the running total of frequencies. "Less than" cumulative adds from the top class downward, and "more than" cumulative adds from the bottom.

- **Relative frequency:** class frequency divided by the total frequency, often shown as a percentage.

- **Frequency density:** frequency divided by class width. It is plotted when class widths are unequal.

\text{Class width} = \text{Upper limit} - \text{Lower limit}

\text{Class mark} = \frac{\text{Lower limit} + \text{Upper limit}}{2}

**Exclusive method:** the upper limit of a class is not included in that class (10 to 20, 20 to 30; a value of 20 goes into the second class). **Inclusive method:** both limits are included (10 to 19, 20 to 29). For inclusive classes, adjust to true class boundaries by subtracting 0.5 from the lower limit and adding 0.5 to the upper limit before drawing a histogram.

### 3.5 Levels (scales) of measurement: NOIR

| Scale        | Property                   | Operations allowed          | Example                               |
|--------------|----------------------------|-----------------------------|---------------------------------------|
| **N**ominal  | Names or categories only   | Counting, mode              | Gender, blood group, religion         |
| **O**rdinal  | Order, but unequal gaps    | Ranking, median             | Class rank, grades, Likert scale      |
| **I**nterval | Equal gaps, no true zero   | Addition, subtraction, mean | Temperature in Celsius, calendar year |
| **R**atio    | Equal gaps and a true zero | All operations, ratios      | Height, weight, income, age           |

A fast test: if "twice as much" makes sense, it is a ratio scale (40 kg is twice 20 kg). It does not make sense for Celsius (40°C is not twice as hot as 20°C), so that is an interval scale.

### 3.6 Tabulation: parts of a table

Tabulation is the systematic arrangement of classified data in rows and columns. The parts are: table number, title, caption (column headings), stub (row headings), body (the figures), headnote (units or notes below the title), footnote (explanations at the bottom), and source note. Tables can be simple (one characteristic), double or two-way (two characteristics), and complex or multi-way (more than two).

### 3.7 Worked examples

**Example 1.** A class interval is 30 to 40. Find its class width and class mark. *Answer: width = 40 − 30 = 10; class mark = (30 + 40) / 2 = 35.*

**Example 2.** Frequencies of five classes are 4, 7, 12, 9, 3. Find the cumulative frequencies. *Answer: 4, 11, 23, 32, 35.*

**Example 3.** Classify these as discrete or continuous: number of books in a library, weight of a bag, number of accidents, distance travelled. *Answer: discrete are books and accidents; continuous are weight and distance.*

**Example 4.** "Marital status of a population" is which type of classification? *Answer: qualitative, and manifold if more than two categories are used.*

**Example 5.** Student rank in a class is measured on which scale? *Answer: ordinal.*

## Chapter 4: Quantitative and Qualitative Data

Quantitative data answer the question "how much or how many?" and are expressed in numbers. Qualitative data answer "what kind?", "why?" or "how?" and are expressed in words, categories, images or descriptions. Research in education and social science uses both, and UGC NET often tests the differences, examples and matching tools.

### 4.1 Comparison

| Basis             | Quantitative data                                    | Qualitative data                                                          |
|-------------------|------------------------------------------------------|---------------------------------------------------------------------------|
| Nature            | Numerical and measurable                             | Descriptive and categorical                                               |
| Question answered | How much, how many, how often                        | Why, how, what kind                                                       |
| Examples          | Marks, income, age, number of children               | Opinion, attitude, gender, colour, religion                               |
| Collection tools  | Structured questionnaire, tests, scales, experiments | Interviews, observation, focus groups, case studies, open-ended questions |
| Analysis          | Statistics: mean, correlation, regression, t-test    | Content analysis, thematic analysis, narrative analysis, grounded theory  |
| Sample size       | Usually large                                        | Usually small                                                             |
| Approach          | Objective, can be generalised                        | Subjective, in-depth, context bound                                       |
| Reasoning         | Deductive (tests a hypothesis)                       | Inductive (builds a theory)                                               |
| Presentation      | Tables, graphs, charts                               | Quotations, themes, descriptions, concept maps                            |

### 4.2 Subtypes

**Quantitative data** can be discrete (counts such as number of students) or continuous (measurements such as height). By measurement scale they are interval or ratio.

**Qualitative data** can be nominal (categories with no order, such as gender or state of residence) or ordinal (categories with an order, such as satisfaction levels from low to high). Binary data (yes or no) are a special nominal case.

A useful trick: a Likert scale response such as "strongly agree" to "strongly disagree" is ordinal. If it is coded 1 to 5 and averaged, it is being treated as quantitative, which is a debated practice.

### 4.3 Converting between the two

Qualitative data can be quantified by coding, for example male = 1 and female = 2, or by counting how often a theme appears. This does not make the variable quantitative in meaning: the codes are labels only. Quantitative data can be turned into categories by grouping, for example marks into grades A, B and C.

### 4.4 Mixed-methods research

Mixed-methods research combines both kinds of data in one study. A common design uses a survey (quantitative) to find patterns, followed by interviews (qualitative) to explain them. Triangulation means checking a finding using more than one method, source or data type to improve validity.

### 4.5 Strengths and limitations

- Quantitative strengths: precise, comparable, easy to generalise and to test statistically. Limitations: may miss context and meaning.

- Qualitative strengths: rich detail, flexible, explores new topics. Limitations: time-consuming, subjective, hard to generalise.

### 4.6 Worked examples

**Example 1.** Which is quantitative: "customer feedback comments", "daily sales in rupees", "favourite colour" or "type of school"? *Answer: daily sales in rupees.*

**Example 2.** A researcher interviews 15 teachers about why they resist technology and groups the answers into themes. Which type of data and reasoning is this? *Answer: qualitative data with inductive reasoning.*

**Example 3.** The number of books issued each day is which type of data? *Answer: quantitative and discrete.*

**Example 4.** Grades A, B, C, D are which kind of data? *Answer: qualitative and ordinal.*

## Chapter 5: Graphical Representation and Mapping of Data

Graphs and charts turn numbers into pictures so that trends, comparisons and patterns are seen at a glance. The syllabus names five forms: the bar chart, histogram, pie chart, table chart and line chart, plus the mapping of data. For every graph, first read the title, axis labels, scale, units and legend, and check whether the axis starts at zero.

### 5.1 Table chart (tabular presentation)

A table gives exact values in rows and columns, so it is the best form when precision matters. Its weakness is that trends are harder to spot than in a graph. In DI questions, a table is usually the data source, and the work is arithmetic on its cells. Always look for totals rows, units in the headnote (for example "in thousands") and footnotes.

### 5.2 Bar chart

A bar chart uses rectangular bars of equal width and equal gaps, and the length (or height) of a bar shows the value. It is suited to discrete or categorical data, such as sales by region or enrolment by state.

- **Simple bar chart:** one bar per category.

- **Multiple (grouped) bar chart:** bars for two or more series side by side, used to compare groups, for example boys and girls in each year.

- **Sub-divided (stacked) bar chart:** each bar is split into parts that add up to the whole.

- **Percentage bar chart:** every bar is scaled to 100 per cent, so it shows composition and not absolute size.

- **Horizontal bar chart:** used when category names are long.

Exam tip: in a stacked chart the top segment is read as the difference between the top and the lower edge, not from zero. Many students misread this.

### 5.3 Histogram

A histogram is a set of adjacent rectangles with no gaps, drawn for a continuous frequency distribution. The class intervals are on the horizontal axis and frequencies on the vertical axis. The area of each rectangle is proportional to the frequency. If class widths are unequal, plot frequency density instead of frequency.

- **Frequency polygon:** made by joining the midpoints of the tops of the histogram bars with straight lines, and closing at both ends on the axis.

- **Frequency curve:** a smoothed polygon.

- **Ogive (cumulative frequency curve):** a "less than" ogive rises from left to right, and a "more than" ogive falls. The point where the two ogives cross gives the median. Plotting is done against the class boundaries.

| Bar chart                    | Histogram                   |
|------------------------------|-----------------------------|
| Gaps between bars            | No gaps                     |
| Categorical or discrete data | Continuous data             |
| Bar width carries no meaning | Width shows class interval  |
| Only the length matters      | Area matters                |
| Bars can be reordered        | Classes cannot be reordered |

### 5.4 Pie chart (circular diagram)

A pie chart is a circle divided into sectors, each proportional to a part of the whole. It shows share or composition. It works well for 3 to 6 categories and poorly for many categories or for small differences.

\text{Angle of a sector} = \frac{\text{Value of the part}}{\text{Total}} \times 360^{\circ}

\text{Percentage share} = \frac{\text{Sector angle}}{360^{\circ}} \times 100

Useful angles: 1 per cent = 3.6°, 10 per cent = 36°, 25 per cent = 90°, 50 per cent = 180°, and 1° = 0.2778 per cent (about 5/18).

### 5.5 Line chart

A line chart joins points with straight lines and is the standard choice for a time series. It shows rise, fall, fluctuation and the rate of change: a steeper line means a faster change, a flat line means no change. With several lines, the points where they cross mark times when the two values are equal. To find a percentage change between two years, read both values from the graph and use the percentage-change formula; do not judge it from the slope alone.

### 5.6 Other representations

- **Pictogram:** uses symbols, with a key such as one symbol = 100 units.

- **Scatter plot (scatter diagram):** plots pairs of values to show the relationship between two variables. Points rising from left to right show positive correlation, falling points show negative correlation, and a cloud with no pattern shows no correlation.

- **Area chart:** a line chart with the area below filled, used for cumulative totals.

- **Radar (spider) chart:** compares several variables for one item on axes radiating from a centre.

- **Box plot:** shows median, quartiles and outliers.

- **Stem-and-leaf plot:** keeps the original values while showing the shape of the distribution.

- **Bubble chart:** a scatter plot where bubble size shows a third variable.

### 5.7 Mapping of data

Mapping presents data by geographic location, which makes spatial patterns visible.

- **Choropleth map:** regions are shaded or coloured by a value, such as literacy rate by state.

- **Dot map:** each dot stands for a fixed count, such as 1 dot per 10,000 people.

- **Proportional symbol map:** the size of a symbol shows magnitude.

- **Cartogram:** the size of each region is distorted in proportion to the data value.

- **Heat map:** colour intensity shows density or concentration.

- **GIS (Geographic Information System):** software that captures, stores, analyses and displays spatial data in layers. It is used in disaster management, urban planning, agriculture, health surveillance and e-governance.

- **Remote sensing:** gathering data about the earth through satellites and sensors, often used with GIS.

### 5.8 Choosing the right graph

| Purpose                                     | Best graph               |
|---------------------------------------------|--------------------------|
| Compare categories                          | Bar chart                |
| Show a trend over time                      | Line chart               |
| Show parts of a whole                       | Pie chart or stacked bar |
| Show a continuous frequency distribution    | Histogram                |
| Show the relationship between two variables | Scatter plot             |
| Show median from cumulative data            | Ogive                    |
| Show variation across regions               | Choropleth map           |
| Show exact values                           | Table                    |

### 5.9 Worked examples

**Example 1.** In a pie chart of a university's 3,600 students, the science sector has an angle of 120°. How many science students are there? *Answer: (120 / 360) × 3,600 = 1,200.*

**Example 2.** A pie chart shows household spending: food 30 per cent, rent 25 per cent, education 20 per cent, others 25 per cent. Find the angle for education. *Answer: 20 per cent of 360° = 72°.*

**Example 3.** Which graph best shows the monthly temperature of a city over a year? *Answer: a line chart, because it is a time series.*

**Example 4.** A histogram has classes 0 to 10, 10 to 20 and 20 to 40 with frequencies 5, 8 and 12. What should be plotted on the vertical axis for the last class? *Answer: frequency density = 12 / 20 = 0.6, because its class width differs from the others.*

**Example 5.** In a grouped bar chart, boys' enrolment is 40 and girls' is 50 in 2024. Girls are what per cent of total? *Answer: 50 / 90 × 100 = 55.6 per cent.*

## Chapter 6: Core Measures and Formulas for Data Interpretation

Every calculation question in this unit reduces to a small set of formulas: average, percentage, percentage change, ratio and growth. Learn each formula together with the one trap attached to it, because the examiner builds the wrong options around that trap.

### 6.1 Measures of central tendency

The **mean** is the sum of all observations divided by their number. The **median** is the middle value of ordered data; with an even number of items it is the average of the two middle values. The **mode** is the value that occurs most often. Mean is affected by extreme values, median is not, and mode can be absent or have more than one value (bimodal).

\text{Mean} = \frac{\text{Sum of observations}}{\text{Number of observations}}

\text{Weighted mean} = \frac{\sum w x}{\sum w}

For a frequency distribution, the mean is the sum of frequency times value, divided by total frequency. In a symmetric distribution, mean, median and mode are equal. For a moderately skewed distribution the empirical relation holds:

\text{Mode} = 3\\\text{Median} - 2\\\text{Mean}

If the distribution is positively skewed (long tail to the right), mean is greater than median, which is greater than mode. For negative skew, mode is greater than median, which is greater than mean.

For grouped data, the median class is the class where the cumulative frequency first reaches N / 2, and the modal class is the class with the highest frequency.

\text{Median} = L + \frac{N/2 - cf}{f} \times h

Here L is the lower limit of the median class, cf is the cumulative frequency before it, f is its frequency and h is its width.

### 6.2 Measures of dispersion

Dispersion shows how spread out the data are. The **range** is the largest value minus the smallest. The **quartile deviation** is half the difference between the third and first quartiles. The **mean deviation** is the average of the absolute deviations from the mean or median. The **variance** is the average of squared deviations from the mean, and the **standard deviation (SD)** is its square root. The **coefficient of variation (CV)** is SD divided by mean, times 100, and is used to compare the consistency of two series: a smaller CV means more consistency.

### 6.3 Percentage

A percentage expresses a number as a part of 100.

\text{Percentage} = \frac{\text{Part}}{\text{Whole}} \times 100

\text{Percentage change} = \frac{\text{New value} - \text{Old value}}{\text{Old value}} \times 100

The base of a percentage change is always the **old (earlier) value**. This is the most common source of wrong answers.

**Percentage point versus percentage change.** A rise in a rate from 20 per cent to 25 per cent is a rise of 5 percentage points (a simple difference) but a 25 per cent increase (5 / 20). When a question says "by how many percentage points", subtract. When it says "by what percentage", divide by the old value.

**Successive changes.** If a value changes by a per cent and then by b per cent, the net change is a + b + (a × b) / 100, with decreases entered as negatives. A rise of 20 per cent followed by a fall of 20 per cent gives 20 − 20 − 4 = −4 per cent, not zero.

**Asymmetry of increase and decrease.** If A is x per cent more than B, then B is x / (100 + x) × 100 per cent less than A. If A is 25 per cent more than B, then B is 20 per cent less than A.

### 6.4 Ratio and proportion

A ratio a : b compares two quantities of the same unit and equals the fraction a / b. To divide a total in the ratio a : b, the first part is a / (a + b) of the total. A ratio has no units. When two ratios must be compared, convert them to decimals or percentages. A proportion states that two ratios are equal (a : b = c : d, so a × d = b × c).

### 6.5 Growth rates

The **growth rate** between two periods is the percentage change. The **average annual growth** over several years is not the simple average of the yearly rates unless a question explicitly asks for that. The **compound annual growth rate (CAGR)** gives the constant yearly rate that would take the starting value to the ending value:

\text{CAGR} = \left(\frac{\text{End value}}{\text{Start value}}\right)^{1/n} - 1

Here n is the number of years between the two values. The rule of 72 gives a quick estimate: the doubling time in years is about 72 divided by the yearly growth rate in per cent.

### 6.6 Index numbers

An index number measures change relative to a base period whose value is set to 100. If the index of a price is 130 in 2024 with 2020 as base, the price has risen 30 per cent since 2020. To compare two years that are both after the base, divide one index by the other, not subtract.

### 6.7 Worked examples

**Example 1 (mean).** Marks of five students are 12, 15, 18, 20 and 25. *Mean = 90 / 5 = 18.*

**Example 2 (median, even count).** Data: 4, 8, 10, 14. *Median = (8 + 10) / 2 = 9.*

**Example 3 (mode from mean and median).** Mean = 20 and median = 18. *Mode = 3 × 18 − 2 × 20 = 14.*

**Example 4 (weighted mean).** A student scores 80 in a paper of weight 2, 70 in a paper of weight 3 and 90 in a paper of weight 5. *Weighted mean = (160 + 210 + 450) / 10 = 82.*

**Example 5 (combined mean).** A class of 40 students has an average of 60 and another class of 60 students has an average of 70. *Combined average = (40 × 60 + 60 × 70) / 100 = 66.* Note that it is not the simple average of 60 and 70.

**Example 6 (missing observation).** The average of 5 numbers is 20. When a sixth number is added the average becomes 22. *Sixth number = 6 × 22 − 5 × 20 = 32.*

**Example 7 (percentage).** 45 of 180 students are girls. *Percentage = 45 / 180 × 100 = 25 per cent.*

**Example 8 (percentage change).** Enrolment rises from 400 to 500. *Change = 100 / 400 × 100 = 25 per cent increase.* If it falls from 500 to 400, the decrease is 100 / 500 × 100 = 20 per cent.

**Example 9 (percentage points).** Pass percentage rises from 60 to 66. *The rise is 6 percentage points, and the percentage increase is 6 / 60 × 100 = 10 per cent.*

**Example 10 (successive change).** A price is raised by 10 per cent and then by 20 per cent. *Net rise = 10 + 20 + (10 × 20) / 100 = 32 per cent.*

**Example 11 (ratio split).** A grant of ₹50,000 is divided between two departments in the ratio 3 : 2. *First gets 3 / 5 × 50,000 = ₹30,000; second gets ₹20,000.*

**Example 12 (CAGR).** A value grows from 100 to 144 in 2 years. *CAGR = (1.44)^(1/2) − 1 = 1.2 − 1 = 20 per cent per year.*

**Example 13 (variance).** Data: 2, 4, 6, 8. *Mean = 5; squared deviations are 9, 1, 1, 9, which total 20; variance = 20 / 4 = 5 and SD ≈ 2.24.*

**Example 14 (compound growth).** A population of 50,000 grows by 10 per cent per year for 2 years. *Population = 50,000 × 1.1 × 1.1 = 60,500.*

## Chapter 7: Data Interpretation: Method, Question Types and Solved Illustrations

Data interpretation means reading data from a table or graph, analysing it, and drawing a correct conclusion. The knowledge needed is small; the skill is in reading the display correctly and choosing the quickest calculation. The data sets below are invented for illustration, so do not quote their numbers as facts.

### 7.1 Five-step method

1.  **Read the display:** title, axis labels, legend, units (thousands, lakhs, crores, per cent) and time period.

2.  **Read the question:** note exactly what is asked (value, percentage, ratio, average, difference, rank) and the base, if one is named.

3.  **Extract only the needed numbers** from the table or graph.

4.  **Choose the formula or shortcut,** and approximate if the options are well separated.

5.  **Check the unit and the direction** of the answer (increase or decrease, A to B or B to A).

### 7.2 Question types

| Type                      | Typical wording                                      | How to solve                         |
|---------------------------|------------------------------------------------------|--------------------------------------|
| Direct reading            | "In which year was production highest?"              | Scan the graph or column             |
| Percentage of total       | "What per cent of the total is X?"                   | Part / Total × 100                   |
| Percentage change         | "By what per cent did sales rise from 2020 to 2021?" | (New − Old) / Old × 100              |
| Percentage point          | "By how many points did the share change?"           | Subtract the two percentages         |
| Ratio                     | "Ratio of boys to girls in 2022"                     | Divide and simplify                  |
| Average                   | "Average of the five years"                          | Sum / 5                              |
| Difference                | "Difference between exports and imports"             | Subtract                             |
| Comparison                | "Which had the larger increase?"                     | Compute both, compare                |
| Missing value             | "If the total is 360, find X"                        | Reverse the formula                  |
| Pie-chart value           | "Amount for the 72° sector"                          | 72 / 360 × Total                     |
| Maximum or minimum growth | "In which year was growth highest?"                  | Compute year-on-year changes         |
| Inference                 | "Which statement is correct?"                        | Test each statement against the data |

### 7.3 Wording decoder

| Phrase                            | Meaning                                 |
|-----------------------------------|-----------------------------------------|
| "A is what per cent of B"         | A / B × 100; base is B                  |
| "A is what per cent more than B"  | (A − B) / B × 100; base is B            |
| "A is what per cent less than B"  | (B − A) / B × 100; base is B            |
| "Increase over the previous year" | Base is the previous year               |
| "Per cent of the total"           | Base is the total, not another part     |
| "Percentage points"               | Simple difference of two percentages    |
| "Twice as much" / "double"        | Ratio of 2; an increase of 100 per cent |
| "Ratio of A to B"                 | A : B, in that order                    |

### 7.4 Illustration A: a table (enrolment in a university, in thousands)

| Year | Boys | Girls | Total |
|------|------|-------|-------|
| 2020 | 40   | 30    | 70    |
| 2021 | 45   | 35    | 80    |
| 2022 | 50   | 50    | 100   |
| 2023 | 48   | 52    | 100   |
| 2024 | 60   | 60    | 120   |

- **Girls' share in 2022:** 50 / 100 × 100 = 50 per cent.

- **Growth in total enrolment, 2020 to 2024:** (120 − 70) / 70 × 100 = 71.4 per cent.

- **Average boys' enrolment over the five years:** (40 + 45 + 50 + 48 + 60) / 5 = 243 / 5 = 48.6 thousand.

- **Ratio of boys to girls in 2021:** 45 : 35 = 9 : 7.

- **Year with the highest growth in total over the previous year:** 2021 gives 10 / 70 = 14.3 per cent; 2022 gives 20 / 80 = 25 per cent; 2023 gives 0 per cent; 2024 gives 20 / 100 = 20 per cent. The highest is 2022.

- **Change in girls' share between 2020 and 2024:** 2020 share is 30 / 70 = 42.9 per cent; 2024 share is 50 per cent. The change is about 7.1 percentage points.

- **Girls' growth from 2023 to 2024:** (60 − 52) / 52 × 100 = 15.4 per cent.

Lesson: the percentage growth in the total is not the same as the growth in either component, and a share and its growth must not be mixed.

### 7.5 Illustration B: a bar chart (values read from a grouped bar chart, ₹ thousand crore)

| Year | Exports | Imports |
|------|---------|---------|
| 2019 | 200     | 240     |
| 2020 | 180     | 210     |
| 2021 | 240     | 270     |
| 2022 | 300     | 360     |
| 2023 | 330     | 330     |

- **Trade deficit (imports minus exports):** 40, 30, 30, 60 and 0 for the five years. The largest deficit is in 2022.

- **Imports as a per cent of exports in 2022:** 360 / 300 × 100 = 120 per cent.

- **Average exports:** (200 + 180 + 240 + 300 + 330) / 5 = 1,250 / 5 = 250.

- **Rise in exports from 2020 to 2023:** (330 − 180) / 180 × 100 = 83.3 per cent.

- **Ratio of exports in 2019 to imports in 2022:** 200 : 360 = 5 : 9.

- **In which year did imports fall compared with the previous year?** Only 2020 (240 to 210).

Lesson: in a grouped bar chart, always read the legend to know which bar is which series before reading any value.

### 7.6 Illustration C: a pie chart (budget of an institution, total ₹20 crore)

| Head           | Angle | Share       | Amount (₹ crore) |
|----------------|-------|-------------|------------------|
| Salaries       | 144°  | 40 per cent | 8                |
| Infrastructure | 72°   | 20 per cent | 4                |
| Research       | 54°   | 15 per cent | 3                |
| Scholarships   | 36°   | 10 per cent | 2                |
| Others         | 54°   | 15 per cent | 3                |

The angles add to 360°, which is the first check on any pie chart. Each share is the angle divided by 3.6.

- **Ratio of salaries to scholarships:** 144 : 36 = 4 : 1 (angles can be used directly, no need to convert).

- **Difference between infrastructure and research:** (72 − 54) / 360 × 20 = 1 crore.

- **Research budget after a 20 per cent rise:** 3 × 1.2 = ₹3.6 crore.

- **Salaries and scholarships together, as per cent of the total:** (144 + 36) / 360 × 100 = 50 per cent.

**Two-pie trap.** Suppose research was 20 per cent of a ₹10 crore budget last year (₹2 crore) and is 15 per cent of ₹20 crore this year (₹3 crore). The share fell by 5 percentage points, but the amount rose by 50 per cent. Never compare absolute amounts using percentages from two pies with different totals.

### 7.7 Illustration D: a line graph (literacy rate, per cent, illustrative)

| Year | State A | State B |
|------|---------|---------|
| 2001 | 60      | 50      |
| 2011 | 70      | 66      |
| 2021 | 80      | 80      |

- **Where do the lines cross?** In 2021, when both are 80 per cent.

- **Rise of State B from 2001 to 2021:** 30 percentage points, and 30 / 50 × 100 = 60 per cent in relative terms.

- **Rise of State A over the same period:** 20 percentage points, or 33.3 per cent in relative terms.

- **Which state had the faster growth?** State B, on both measures.

- **Which decade had the larger rise for State B?** 2011 to 2021 (14 points) versus 2001 to 2011 (16 points); the earlier decade was larger.

Lesson: a steeper line means a faster change per unit of time, but compare actual numbers before answering.

### 7.8 Illustration E: statement-based inference

Some questions give statements and ask which are correct. Test each statement against the data, and mark only those that follow from the data shown. For Illustration A, "girls' enrolment exceeded boys' in 2023" is true (52 against 48), "total enrolment doubled between 2020 and 2024" is false (70 to 120 is a rise of 71.4 per cent), and "the reason for rising enrolment is a new scholarship scheme" cannot be concluded because the data give no cause.

A data display shows what happened, not why. Statements about causes, future values or other places are not supported by the data unless the question says so.

## Chapter 8: Speed Tricks, Approximation and Common Traps

With about two minutes per question and no calculator, speed comes from three habits: knowing fraction and percentage equivalents by heart, using simple decompositions (10 per cent, 5 per cent, halves), and approximating when the options are far apart. This section collects the tricks that save the most time.

### 8.1 Fraction and percentage equivalents to memorise

| Fraction | Per cent | Fraction | Per cent |
|----------|----------|----------|----------|
| 1/2      | 50       | 1/9      | 11.11    |
| 1/3      | 33.33    | 1/10     | 10       |
| 1/4      | 25       | 1/11     | 9.09     |
| 1/5      | 20       | 1/12     | 8.33     |
| 1/6      | 16.67    | 1/15     | 6.67     |
| 1/7      | 14.28    | 1/16     | 6.25     |
| 1/8      | 12.5     | 1/20     | 5        |

Multiples follow easily: 3/8 = 37.5 per cent, 5/8 = 62.5 per cent, 7/8 = 87.5 per cent, 2/3 = 66.67 per cent, 5/6 = 83.33 per cent.

### 8.2 Percentage tricks

1.  **Swap trick:** x per cent of y equals y per cent of x. So 8 per cent of 25 is the same as 25 per cent of 8, which is 2.

2.  **Build from 10 per cent:** find 10 per cent by moving the decimal point, then add or halve. 15 per cent of 240 = 24 + 12 = 36. 5 per cent of 240 = 12. 25 per cent of 240 = 60.

3.  **Use the fraction form:** 12.5 per cent of 160 is 160 / 8 = 20; 33.33 per cent of 84 is 84 / 3 = 28; 16.67 per cent of 108 is 108 / 6 = 18.

4.  **Increase and decrease by fractions:** a 25 per cent increase multiplies by 5/4, so 80 becomes 100. A 20 per cent decrease multiplies by 4/5, so 150 becomes 120. A 50 per cent increase multiplies by 3/2.

5.  **Percentage change by difference:** find the difference first, then divide by the old value. From 180 to 225 the difference is 45 and 45 / 180 = 25 per cent.

6.  **Odd percentages by splitting:** 17 per cent of 300 = 10 per cent (30) + 7 per cent (21) = 51.

### 8.3 Approximation

If the four options are well separated (differences of 5 to 10 per cent or more), round the numbers to the nearest convenient value and compute roughly. For example, 487 out of 1,962 is close to 500 out of 2,000, which is 25 per cent; the true value is 24.8 per cent. Decimals such as 49.7 can be treated as 50. Do not approximate when options are close (for example 24.8, 25.1, 25.4), or when the question asks for a difference between nearly equal numbers, because rounding errors can exceed the gap.

After approximating, look at the options and eliminate by size and last digit before calculating further.

### 8.4 Comparing fractions and ratios

To see which of two fractions is larger, cross-multiply. To compare 7/12 and 9/16: 7 × 16 = 112 and 9 × 12 = 108, so 7/12 is larger. For "which year had the highest per cent", do not calculate all years fully; round each ratio and compare the top two or three.

### 8.5 Average shortcuts

Use an **assumed mean**: pick a convenient central number, add up the deviations from it, divide by the count and add the result back. Marks 48, 52, 55, 45, 50: take 50 as the assumed mean; deviations are −2, +2, +5, −5, 0, which total 0, so the mean is 50. If the observations are in an evenly spaced series, the average equals the middle value (or the average of the two middle values).

### 8.6 Pie-chart shortcuts

If the question asks for a ratio or a comparison of two sectors, use the angles directly; you do not need the total. If it asks for a value, convert the angle into a fraction of 360 (for example 90° = 1/4, 120° = 1/3, 45° = 1/8) and multiply by the total. Check that all the angles add up to 360° before using any of them.

### 8.7 Table and graph reading shortcuts

- For "highest growth", look first at columns with a small base; a small base with a moderate rise often gives the highest per cent.

- For "highest absolute increase", compare simple differences and ignore the base.

- For a stacked bar chart, subtract the lower edge from the upper edge to find a segment.

- For a line graph with two series, the vertical gap between the lines is the difference at that point.

- If a graph has two y-axes (left and right), check which axis each series uses.

### 8.8 Common traps and how option sets are built

Wrong options in DI questions are usually not random: each one matches a specific mistake. Knowing the mistakes helps you reject options quickly.

| Trap                      | Wrong approach                                                | Correct approach                      |
|---------------------------|---------------------------------------------------------------|---------------------------------------|
| Wrong base                | Dividing by the new value                                     | Divide by the old value               |
| Points versus per cent    | Giving 5 when 25 per cent was asked                           | Check the exact wording               |
| Units                     | Mixing thousands with lakhs or crores                         | Read the headnote or axis title       |
| Reverse ratio             | Giving B : A when A : B was asked                             | Follow the order of the question      |
| Wrong series              | Reading the girls' bar for boys                               | Check the legend and colour key       |
| Cumulative data           | Treating a running total as a yearly figure                   | Read the graph title                  |
| Average of averages       | Averaging two group means without weights                     | Use the weighted mean                 |
| Truncated axis            | Judging bar size by length alone                              | Check whether the axis starts at zero |
| Pie with different totals | Comparing shares across two pies                              | Convert to absolute values first      |
| Assuming a trend          | Extending a line beyond the data                              | Answer only from the data shown       |
| Increase equals decrease  | Believing +20 per cent then −20 per cent returns to the start | Net change is −4 per cent             |
| "Approximately" ignored   | Computing exactly and failing to match                        | Pick the nearest option               |

### 8.9 Typical arrangement of the five questions

In a set of five questions on one data display, a common arrangement (not a fixed rule) is: one direct-reading or total question, one average or ratio question, one percentage or percentage-change question, one comparison or maximum question, and one slightly longer question that combines two steps. Solve the easiest first, leave the two-step question for last, and use the time you save on the earlier ones.

### 8.10 Practice routine for speed

- Memorise the fraction table above until you recall it without thinking.

- Time yourself: 5 questions on one data set in 8 minutes at first, then in 6 minutes.

- After each set, note which trap caught you, and keep a personal list of traps.

- Practise approximating first, and exact calculation only when needed.

## Chapter 9: Data and Governance

This sub-topic asks how governments and institutions collect, share, protect and use data to make decisions and deliver services. The questions are factual, so they are quick marks if you know the terms, acronyms and the purpose of each programme. Dates and rules in this area change, so check anything recent against an official source before the exam.

### 9.1 Meaning and scope

Data governance, in the institutional sense, is the set of policies, roles, standards and processes that ensure data are accurate, secure, private, available and used responsibly across their lifecycle (creation, storage, use, sharing, archiving and deletion). Data in governance, in the public-policy sense, means using reliable data to plan, monitor and evaluate policies, so that decisions rest on evidence and not on guesswork. This is called **evidence-based policy making**.

The main roles in data governance are the **data owner** (accountable for a data set), the **data steward** (looks after quality and standards) and the **data custodian** (manages storage and security). Core elements are data quality, metadata, standards, access control, privacy and security, and compliance with law.

### 9.2 E-governance

E-governance is the use of information and communication technology (ICT) to deliver government services, exchange information and make administration transparent, efficient and accountable. India's National e-Governance Plan began in 2006 and its work has since been absorbed into the Digital India programme.

| Model | Meaning                  | Example                                          |
|-------|--------------------------|--------------------------------------------------|
| G2C   | Government to Citizen    | Online tax filing, passport services, DigiLocker |
| G2B   | Government to Business   | e-Procurement, GST portal, company registration  |
| G2G   | Government to Government | Data sharing between departments and states      |
| G2E   | Government to Employee   | Employee portals, payroll and service records    |

Benefits of e-governance include transparency, reduced corruption, saving of time and cost, wider access and better accountability. Challenges include the digital divide, lack of digital literacy, cyber-security threats, poor interoperability between systems and resistance to change.

### 9.3 Open data

Open data are data that anyone can freely access, use, modify and share, subject to conditions such as attribution. In India, the **National Data Sharing and Accessibility Policy (NDSAP, 2012)** governs the sharing of government data, and the **Open Government Data Platform at data.gov.in** is the portal that publishes it. NITI Aayog also runs the **National Data and Analytics Platform (NDAP)**, which brings government data sets together in one place. Good open data are often described by the **FAIR** principles: Findable, Accessible, Interoperable and Reusable.

Benefits are transparency, public participation, innovation and research. Risks are privacy loss if personal data are not anonymised, and misuse or misinterpretation of data.

### 9.4 Big data and analytics

Big data refers to data sets so large, fast and varied that ordinary tools cannot handle them. They are described by the **5 Vs**: Volume (size), Velocity (speed of generation), Variety (types such as text, image, video and sensor data), Veracity (reliability) and Value (usefulness). Governments use big data and analytics for predicting disease outbreaks, planning transport, detecting tax fraud, targeting welfare and monitoring crops. The main types of analytics are descriptive (what happened), diagnostic (why it happened), predictive (what will happen) and prescriptive (what should be done).

### 9.5 Digital India and related initiatives

The Digital India programme was launched on 1 July 2015 to make government services available electronically, build digital infrastructure and promote digital literacy. Its vision rests on three areas: digital infrastructure as a core utility, governance and services on demand, and digital empowerment of citizens. It is organised around nine pillars, including broadband highways, universal access to mobile connectivity, public internet access, e-governance, e-Kranti, information for all, electronics manufacturing, IT for jobs and early harvest programmes.

| Initiative                     | Purpose                                                                   |
|--------------------------------|---------------------------------------------------------------------------|
| Aadhaar (UIDAI)                | A 12-digit unique identity number based on biometric and demographic data |
| DigiLocker                     | Cloud storage for digital copies of documents                             |
| UMANG                          | A single mobile app for many government services                          |
| MyGov                          | Platform for citizen participation in governance                          |
| GeM (Government e-Marketplace) | Online public procurement                                                 |
| UPI                            | Instant digital payments                                                  |
| JAM trinity                    | Jan Dhan accounts, Aadhaar and Mobile linked for direct benefit transfer  |
| Bhashini                       | Language technology to give access to services in Indian languages        |

### 9.6 Education data systems

- **UDISE+** (Unified District Information System for Education Plus): school-level data on enrolment, teachers and infrastructure.

- **AISHE** (All India Survey on Higher Education): data on higher education institutions, students and staff.

- **NIRF** (National Institutional Ranking Framework): ranks institutions on parameters such as teaching, research and outreach.

- **NAD** (National Academic Depository): a digital store of academic awards and certificates.

- **ABC** (Academic Bank of Credits): stores credits earned by students, linked with the National Education Policy 2020.

- **NAAC and NBA:** accreditation data for institutions and programmes.

### 9.7 Data privacy, protection and ethics

Data privacy is a person's right to control how their personal information is collected and used. The main principles are consent, purpose limitation (use data only for the stated purpose), data minimisation (collect only what is needed), accuracy, storage limitation, security and accountability. India's law on this subject is the **Digital Personal Data Protection Act, 2023**, which governs the processing of digital personal data; its rules and phased commencement should be checked for the current position. The **Information Technology Act, 2000** deals with cyber offences and electronic records, and the **Right to Information Act, 2005** gives citizens access to information held by public authorities. Ethical issues include surveillance, algorithmic bias, exclusion of people who are not in the system, data breaches and use of data without consent.

### 9.8 Digital divide

The digital divide is the gap between people who have access to and can use ICT and those who cannot. It arises from differences in income, location (urban and rural), gender, age, education, disability and language, and from the cost of devices and connectivity. It has three levels: **access** (who has devices and internet), **skills** (who can use them well) and **outcomes** (who benefits). Remedies include public internet access points, digital literacy campaigns, low-cost devices, services in local languages and accessible design.

### 9.9 Spatial data in governance

GIS and remote sensing support land records, disaster response, city planning, agriculture and infrastructure monitoring. Dashboards bring indicators together so that officials can track progress on schemes and targets in near real time.

### 9.10 Example conceptual points

**Example 1.** Which e-governance model describes a citizen paying a property tax online? *G2C.*

**Example 2.** Which characteristic of big data refers to the reliability and accuracy of data? *Veracity.*

**Example 3.** Which policy governs the sharing of government data in India? *NDSAP, 2012.*

**Example 4.** A firm collects only the data it needs for a service and deletes it after use. Which principle is being followed? *Data minimisation and storage limitation.*

### 10.5 Rapid-fire true and false

- Mean is not affected by extreme values. **False**: it is affected; the median is not.

- A histogram has gaps between bars. **False**: the bars touch.

- A pie chart suits time series. **False**: use a line chart.

- Secondary data are collected first-hand. **False**: that is primary data.

- Temperature in Celsius is a ratio scale. **False**: it is an interval scale.

- Mode can be absent in a data set. **True**.

- A rise from 40 per cent to 50 per cent is a 10 per cent increase. **False**: it is 10 percentage points and a 25 per cent increase.

- In a symmetric distribution, mean, median and mode are equal. **True**.

- A questionnaire needs a literate respondent. **True**.

- A census always has sampling error. **False**: sampling error belongs to sample surveys (non-sampling errors can still occur).

- Keep roughly 8 to 10 minutes for the unit, and do not let one hard question take more than 3 minutes.

- Leave a spare minute at the end to recheck any answer where you changed the unit or the base.
