# Communication: Detailed Notes for UGC NET Paper 1

## Chapter 0: Exam Pattern and Question Trends

### 0.1 Exam pattern and where Communication fits

**Overall UGC NET pattern (current, as followed through the 2026 cycles):**

- The exam is a Computer-Based Test (CBT) with two papers taken in one continuous sitting of **3 hours (180 minutes) with no break** between them.

- **Paper 1** (General Teaching and Research Aptitude, common for all subjects): **50 MCQs, 100 marks**, i.e. 2 marks per question.

- **Paper 2** (your chosen subject): 100 MCQs, 200 marks.

- Total: 150 questions, 300 marks. **There is no negative marking.** Attempt every question.

- Paper 1 has **10 units** with roughly **5 questions per unit**. Communication is **Unit 4**; the others are Teaching Aptitude, Research Aptitude, Comprehension, Mathematical Reasoning, Logical Reasoning, Data Interpretation, ICT, People Development and Environment, and Higher Education System.

- Always re-check the latest NTA information bulletin before applying, because NTA can alter pattern details between cycles.

**Official syllabus of Unit 4 – Communication (the topic blocks you must cover):**

1.  Communication: meaning, types and characteristics of communication

2.  Effective communication: verbal and non-verbal, inter-cultural and group communications, classroom communication

3.  Barriers to effective communication

4.  Mass media and society

Everything below is organised to cover each of these blocks plus the theory background (models, theorists, laws, institutions) that MCQs commonly draw from.

### 0.2 Question trends: how the unit is actually tested

**Caution:** the points below are patterns observed across past papers and coaching analyses, not official NTA statistics. Use them as a guide to priorities, and verify against the last 8 to 10 years of previous-year papers.

- **Number of questions:** usually about **5 per paper** (sometimes 4 to 5). It is one of the more *scoring* units because it is theory-heavy and not calculation-heavy.

- **Difficulty:** generally easy to moderate. Paper 1 in the June 2026 cycle was reported by coaching analyses as easy to moderate overall.

- **Highest-frequency areas (in rough order):**

  1.  Barriers to communication (types, examples, remedies)

  2.  Classroom communication (teacher behaviour, feedback, interaction, Flanders)

  3.  Verbal and non-verbal communication (kinesics, proxemics, paralanguage)

  4.  Mass media and society (theories, media types, effects, Indian media institutions)

  5.  Types and levels of communication (formal/informal, grapevine, directions)

  6.  Models and elements of the communication process

  7.  Inter-cultural and group communication (less frequent but recurring)

- **Question formats you will see:**

  - *Memory-based:* "Who proposed X?", "Which is NOT an element of communication?", "Proxemics deals with...", "Grapevine is a type of..."

  - *Concept-based:* identify the barrier or type from a short scenario; match a definition to a term.

  - *Analysis-based:* statement I / statement II (true/false), assertion–reason, match-the-following (List I with List II), arrange in correct sequence, and "which of the following statements are correct" with combination options.

- **Typical traps:** options that mix up similar terms (hypodermic needle vs two-step flow; agenda setting vs cultivation; encoding vs decoding; semantic vs psychological barrier; downward vs horizontal communication), and "NOT / EXCEPT" questions.

- **Study priority:** master definitions + theorist names + one-line examples. For analysis questions, practise explaining *why* an option is wrong.

## Chapter 1: Meaning, Nature and Characteristics of Communication

### 1.1 Meaning and Origin

The word *communication* comes from the Latin **communis** ("common") and **communicare** ("to share, to make common"). Communication is the process of **sharing information, ideas, thoughts, feelings and meaning between two or more persons (or entities) so that a common understanding is created**. The key idea is *shared meaning*: if the receiver does not understand what the sender intended, communication is incomplete even though a message was sent.

A widely used working definition: communication is a **two-way, dynamic process of exchanging messages through a medium so that the sender and receiver develop mutual understanding**. In teaching, the aim is not only transmission of information but also change in knowledge, attitude and behaviour of the learner.

### 1.2 Characteristics of Communication

- **Process, not an event:** it is continuous and has no fixed beginning or end.

- **Two-way (interactive):** feedback completes the cycle. One-way communication (lecture, broadcast) is incomplete without feedback.

- **Dynamic and transactional:** both parties send and receive at the same time and change each other.

- **Symbolic:** words, gestures, images and sounds are symbols that stand for ideas; meaning lives in people, not in words.

- **Irreversible:** once a message is sent it cannot be taken back; you can only correct or clarify it.

- **Unavoidable:** "One cannot not communicate" (Watzlawick): even silence or absence sends a message.

- **Purposeful / goal-oriented:** it has an intention (inform, persuade, entertain, educate, instruct).

- **Contextual:** physical, social, cultural, psychological and temporal context shape meaning.

- **Involves both content and relationship:** the way something is said conveys the relationship between speakers.

- **Can be intentional or unintentional,** verbal or non-verbal, formal or informal.

### 1.3 Functions of Communication

- **Informative:** passing facts and knowledge.

- **Educative / instructive:** teaching and training.

- **Persuasive / motivational:** changing opinions and behaviour (advertising, propaganda, counselling).

- **Expressive / emotive:** sharing feelings.

- **Integrative / social:** building relationships and group unity.

- **Control / regulatory:** guiding conduct through rules and instructions.

- **Entertainment** (especially in mass communication).

### 1.4 Principles of Effective Communication – the 7 Cs

- **Clarity:** clear, simple language and a single main idea.

- **Conciseness:** brief and to the point, without wasteful words.

- **Correctness:** accurate facts, grammar and tone.

- **Completeness:** all required information is provided.

- **Courtesy:** respectful, polite, positive tone.

- **Consideration:** a receiver-oriented approach ("you" attitude).

- **Concreteness:** specific facts and figures instead of vague statements.

(Some books add *Coherence* and *Credibility*. If an MCQ gives the 7 Cs, the list above is the standard one.)

### 1.5 In simple words: What communication really is

Think of communication as **passing a parcel and checking that it arrived undamaged**. Sending is only half the job. If a teacher says "Photosynthesis is how plants make food using sunlight" and students can later explain it in their own words, communication happened. If students only heard sounds but understood nothing, then only *transmission* happened, not communication. **Transmission is not the same as communication; shared understanding is what completes it.**

**Why "two-way" matters:** compare the blue ticks on a messaging app with an actual reply. Blue ticks only tell you the message was *delivered*. A reply tells you it was *understood*. That reply is **feedback**, and feedback is what makes communication a cycle instead of a one-way street.

### 1.6 In simple words: Characteristics explained with everyday examples

- **Process:** it never "stops". A classroom conversation continues through looks, silence and later questions.

- **Symbolic:** the word "tree" is not a tree; it is a symbol agreed upon by speakers of a language. Different languages use different symbols for the same thing, which is why misunderstandings arise.

- **Irreversible:** once you say something harsh in anger, saying "sorry" does not erase it. You can only repair, not delete.

- **Unavoidable:** a student sitting with folded arms and a blank face is *saying* "I am bored or upset" without a single word. You cannot *not* communicate.

- **Contextual:** "Fine." can mean agreement, anger or sadness depending on tone, relationship and situation.

- **Transactional:** in a real conversation both people talk, listen and react at the same moment, and both change during the exchange.

- **Purposeful:** even small talk has a purpose (building friendship). Exam wording: "communication is goal-oriented".

### 1.7 In simple words: Functions with examples

- **Informative:** the news bulletin or a notice board circular.

- **Educative:** a lecture, a tutorial video.

- **Persuasive:** an advertisement or an election speech.

- **Expressive:** telling a friend you are anxious about results.

- **Integrative:** a farewell party that strengthens team bonding.

- **Control:** college rules, traffic signs, exam instructions.

- **Entertainment:** films, songs, reels.

### 1.8 In simple words: The 7 Cs with a before-and-after example

- **Poor message:** "Send the thing soon." (unclear, vague, incomplete, no courtesy)

- **Better message:** "Please submit your Unit 2 assignment on the college portal by 5 pm on Friday. Thank you."

What changed: it is *clear* (what is wanted), *concise* (no extra words), *concrete* (a date and time), *complete* (where and when), *correct* (accurate), *courteous* (please and thank you) and *considerate* (written from the reader's point of view, so they know exactly what to do).

**Exam cue:** "you attitude" means **consideration**; "specific facts and figures" means **concreteness**; "saying only what is needed" means **conciseness**.

- *Intrapersonal* = within oneself; *interpersonal* = two people; *mass* = large, anonymous audience with delayed feedback.

## Chapter 2: Elements, Process and Models of Communication

### 2.1 Basic Elements

- **Sender / source / communicator:** originates the message.

- **Encoding:** converting ideas into symbols (words, signs, images).

- **Message:** the content, the idea or information being shared.

- **Channel / medium:** the route (speech, writing, phone, email, TV, internet).

- **Receiver:** the person who gets the message.

- **Decoding:** interpreting the symbols to extract meaning.

- **Feedback:** the receiver's response that tells the sender how the message was understood.

- **Noise:** anything that distorts or interferes with the message at any stage (physical, psychological, semantic, technical).

- **Context / environment:** the situation in which communication occurs.

Sequence to remember: **Sender → Encoding → Message → Channel → Decoding → Receiver → Feedback** (noise can act at any point).

### 2.2 Important Models and Theorists (High Memory Value)

- **Aristotle's model (c. 4th century BCE):** the earliest, a linear model focused on public speaking. Elements: **speaker, speech, occasion, audience, effect**. Persuasion rests on **ethos** (credibility), **pathos** (emotion) and **logos** (logic).

- **Lasswell's model (1948):** "**Who** says **what** in which **channel** to **whom** with what **effect**?" Linked to control analysis, content analysis, media analysis, audience analysis and effect analysis. A linear model for mass communication.

- **Shannon and Weaver's model (1949):** the **mathematical / information theory model** developed for telephone engineering. Components: information source, transmitter, signal, channel, noise source, receiver, destination. Linear and one-way, with the idea of **noise**. Weaver later added **semantic noise**. Originally lacked feedback.

- **Schramm's models (1954):** the idea of the **field of experience** (overlapping experiences needed for communication to occur) and the **Osgood–Schramm circular model**, in which each party is both encoder and decoder, with an **interpreter** role. It stresses **circular, equal, interactive** communication and feedback.

- **Berlo's SMCR model (1960):** **Source, Message, Channel, Receiver**. The source and receiver are influenced by communication skills, attitudes, knowledge, social system and culture. The message has content, elements, treatment, structure and code. Channels correspond to the five senses (seeing, hearing, touching, smelling, tasting).

- **Newcomb's ABX model (1953):** the sender (A) and receiver (B) are oriented toward an object (X) and seek **symmetry / balance** (a sociopsychological model).

- **Westley and Maclean's model (1957):** adds the **gatekeeper / channel role (C)** between source (A) and receiver (B), with objects of orientation (X) and feedback. Useful for mass communication.

- **Gerbner's model (1956):** a general model that includes perception and the selection of events.

- **Dance's helical model (1967):** communication as a **helix**, spiralling and evolving over time; it shows that communication is never a closed circle.

- **Two-step flow model (Lazarsfeld, Berelson and Gaudet, 1944):** see topic 9.

**Classification quick-check:** Aristotle, Lasswell, Shannon–Weaver, Berlo = **linear**. Osgood–Schramm, Westley–Maclean (with feedback) = **interactive/circular**. Dance = **helical/transactional**.

### 2.3 Johari Window (Interpersonal Awareness)

Developed by **Joseph Luft and Harry Ingham (1955)**. Four panes: **Open (Arena)** known to self and others; **Blind spot** unknown to self but known to others; **Hidden (Façade)** known to self but not to others; **Unknown** unknown to both. Self-disclosure and feedback enlarge the open area, which improves interpersonal communication.

### 2.4 In simple words: Elements walked through one example

Priya wants to tell Ravi to bring his class notes tomorrow.

- **Sender:** Priya.

- **Encoding:** she turns her thought into words ("Please bring your notes tomorrow").

- **Message:** the request itself.

- **Channel:** a phone call (it could be a text or a face-to-face chat).

- **Noise:** poor network signal makes parts of the sentence unclear (this is *physical/technical noise*).

- **Decoding:** Ravi interprets the words. If Priya meant "class notes" but Ravi thinks of "currency notes", that mismatch is *semantic noise*.

- **Receiver:** Ravi.

- **Feedback:** Ravi replies "Which notes? Maths?" Now Priya knows how her message landed and can correct it.

This is why *encoding and decoding* are described as the two "translation" steps: ideas to symbols, then symbols back to ideas. Errors can creep into either step.

**Types of noise (simple):** *physical* (loud sound), *technical* (bad network), *psychological* (anger, worry), *semantic* (words with different meanings).

### 2.5 In simple words: Models in plain language

**Aristotle's model (speaker-centred persuasion).** Imagine a speaker on a stage persuading a crowd. Three tools: **ethos** (the speaker's credibility, e.g., a doctor speaking about health), **pathos** (appeal to emotion, e.g., a moving story) and **logos** (appeal to logic, e.g., data and reasoning). *Weakness:* the audience is treated as passive and there is no feedback. *Cue:* "oldest", "public speaking", "ethos, pathos, logos".

**Lasswell's model (five questions).** A reporter's checklist: Who? Says what? In which channel? To whom? With what effect? Useful to *analyse* a news broadcast or advertisement from five angles (communicator, message, medium, audience, effect). *Weakness:* ignores noise and feedback. *Cue:* "who says what...", "mass communication", "1948".

**Shannon and Weaver's model (the telephone line).** Picture a phone call with crackling static on the line. The source sends a signal through a channel, noise interferes, and the receiver gets a slightly different signal. It was built by engineers for telephones, so it treats people like machines. Weaver described three levels of problems: **technical** (is the signal sent accurately?), **semantic** (does the message carry the intended meaning?) and **effectiveness** (does it change the receiver's behaviour as intended?). *Weakness:* linear, no feedback. *Cue:* "noise", "mathematical model", "1949".

**Osgood and Schramm's model (a ping-pong game).** Both players hit and receive the ball. Each person is *both* sender and receiver, and each interprets before replying. There is no fixed start or end, which is why it is called **circular**. *Cue:* "circular", "encoder-decoder-interpreter", "equal participants".

**Berlo's SMCR model (a four-part checklist).** **S**ource, **M**essage, **C**hannel, **R**eceiver. Berlo adds that both source and receiver are shaped by five factors: *communication skills, attitudes, knowledge, social system and culture*. A teacher with strong skills and the right attitude who knows the subject and understands the students' culture communicates better. *Channels = the five senses.* *Cue:* "SMCR", "five senses", "1960".

**Westley and Maclean's model (the editor in the middle).** In mass communication, a news channel does not send everything. A **gatekeeper (C)**, like an editor, selects what reaches the audience. *Cue:* "gatekeeper", "mass media version".

**Newcomb's ABX model (two friends and a movie).** A and B are friends, X is a film. If both like X, they are in harmony. If A loves it and B hates it, there is *tension*, and they will talk (communicate) to restore balance. *Cue:* "balance", "symmetry", "social-psychological".

**Dance's helical model (a growing spiral).** Communication does not go round in the same circle. Each conversation builds on previous ones, so the circle widens into a **helix** as knowledge and experience grow, like a student whose understanding deepens each class. *Cue:* "helix", "spiral", "evolving".

**Linear vs circular vs transactional (very easy to confuse):**

- *Linear:* one direction, sender to receiver (Aristotle, Lasswell, Shannon-Weaver, Berlo).

- *Circular / interactive:* messages go back and forth in turns (Osgood-Schramm).

- *Transactional:* both people send and receive *at the same time* and keep changing each other (a real conversation; Dance's helix is closest).

### 2.6 In simple words: Johari Window made simple

Imagine a window with four panes:

- **Open:** things you and others both know (your name, your subject).

- **Blind:** things others notice about you but you do not (you say "you know" too often).

- **Hidden:** things you know but keep private (your fear of public speaking).

- **Unknown:** things nobody knows yet (a talent you have not discovered).

When you **share** about yourself (self-disclosure) and **receive feedback**, the Open pane grows and the others shrink. Better communication means a bigger Open pane.

## Chapter 3: Types and Levels of Communication

### 3.1 Based on Level or Number of Participants

- **Intrapersonal:** communication within oneself (thinking, self-talk, reflection, decision-making).

- **Interpersonal:** between two persons or a small number, usually face to face (dyadic), with immediate feedback. Examples: conversation, interview, counselling, a teacher talking to a student.

- **Group communication:** among members of a small group with a common purpose (class, committee, team).

- **Public communication:** one speaker to a large live audience (lecture, speech, rally).

- **Organizational communication:** within and across an organization (formal and informal).

- **Mass communication:** from an organised source through a mass medium to a large, heterogeneous, anonymous and scattered audience, with delayed and indirect feedback.

### 3.2 Based on Mode

- **Verbal:** uses words, oral or written.

- **Non-verbal:** uses body language, gestures, signs, silence, space, time, appearance.

- **Visual:** pictures, charts, maps, symbols.

### 3.3 Based on Formality – Organizational Communication

**Formal communication** follows the official hierarchy and channels, is documented, and includes:

- **Downward:** from superior to subordinate (orders, instructions, policies, circulars). Risk: distortion and delay.

- **Upward:** from subordinate to superior (reports, suggestions, grievances, feedback). Often limited by fear of authority.

- **Horizontal / lateral:** between people at the same level (coordination between departments).

- **Diagonal / crosswise:** between people at different levels and different departments.

**Informal communication** is not bound by the official structure. Its main form is the **grapevine**.

- The term **grapevine** is traditionally linked to the American Civil War era, from the winding telegraph lines resembling grape vines.

- **Keith Davis** classified grapevine chains: **single strand, gossip, probability and cluster**. The **cluster chain** is the most common (one person tells a select few, who in turn tell a few others).

- Characteristics: fast, selective, spreads by word of mouth, often partly accurate, cannot be fully controlled but can be managed and used as feedback.

### 3.4 Communication Networks (Bavelas and Leavitt)

- **Chain:** information flows in a line through a hierarchy.

- **Wheel (Star):** one central person communicates with all others; fast and efficient for simple tasks but low member satisfaction.

- **Y network:** a mix of chain and wheel.

- **Circle:** each member communicates with two neighbours; moderate speed, higher satisfaction.

- **All-channel (Comcon / Web):** everyone communicates with everyone; best for complex problem-solving and high satisfaction, but slow to organise.

### 3.5 In simple words: Levels with a college example

- **Intrapersonal:** you silently decide "I should revise Unit 4 tonight".

- **Interpersonal:** you discuss a doubt with your teacher.

- **Group:** a project team of five planning a presentation.

- **Public:** the principal addressing the entire college at an annual function.

- **Organizational:** university notices, official emails, and staff meetings.

- **Mass:** a newspaper or news channel reaching millions who are strangers to the source.

**Exam cue:** immediate feedback and small numbers = *interpersonal*; large, scattered, anonymous audience = *mass*; talking to yourself = *intrapersonal*.

### 3.6 In simple words: Direction of flow in an organization (with a college example)

- **Downward:** the principal issues a circular to all staff. *Risk:* messages get distorted as they pass through many levels.

- **Upward:** a lecturer submits a request or feedback to the HOD; students' feedback forms. *Risk:* fear of authority makes people hide bad news (**filtering**).

- **Horizontal / lateral:** two teachers from the same department coordinate the timetable. Purpose: coordination and teamwork.

- **Diagonal / crosswise:** a lecturer from the English department directly contacts an officer in the accounts section about a bill, skipping the usual chain of command. Purpose: speed and cross-department work.

### 3.7 In simple words: Formal vs informal communication

- **Formal:** follows rules and the official chain, is documented, is slower, and is reliable (letters, circulars, meetings, reports).

- **Informal:** free of official rules, spontaneous, fast, builds relationships, but can carry rumours (canteen chat, WhatsApp groups of colleagues).

**Grapevine in plain words:** the "canteen talk" about who might be promoted. It spreads quickly because people trust friends. Smart managers *listen* to it, because it reveals staff worries.

**Keith Davis's four grapevine chains (how the rumour moves):**

- **Single strand:** A tells B, B tells C, C tells D, like a long chain of whispers. Very distorted by the end.

- **Gossip:** one person (A) tells everyone directly.

- **Probability:** A tells people at random, who tell others at random.

- **Cluster:** A tells a few selected people, and some of them tell a few others. This is the **most common** in real life, because people pass news only to those they trust.

### 3.8 In simple words: Communication networks: easy picture

- **Chain:** orders passing down a ladder, person to person.

- **Wheel (star):** a class teacher at the centre; every student talks only to the teacher. Quick and efficient for simple tasks, but members may feel less satisfied.

- **Y:** a mixture, with one person at the junction.

- **Circle:** each person talks only to two neighbours; slow but friendly.

- **All-channel:** everyone talks to everyone, like a brainstorming session. Best for complex problems, but takes time.

**Exam cue:** "central person" = wheel; "everyone with everyone" = all-channel; "each person with two neighbours" = circle.

### 3.9 In simple words: Other useful classifications

- **Synchronous vs asynchronous:** a phone or video call is synchronous (real-time). An email or discussion forum post is asynchronous (reply later).

- **One-way vs two-way:** a TV broadcast is mostly one-way; a classroom discussion is two-way.

- **Formal vs informal language:** a research paper versus a chat with a friend.

- **Private vs public:** a counselling session versus a public lecture.

### Memory Capsule: Topic 3

- Keith Davis: **grapevine types**
- *Downward* = superior to subordinate; *upward* = subordinate to superior; *horizontal* = same level; *diagonal* = different level and different department.
- *Grapevine* is informal; its **cluster chain** is the most common.

## Chapter 4: Verbal Communication and Listening

### 4.1 Oral Communication

- Uses spoken words: conversation, telephone, interviews, meetings, lectures, presentations.

- **Advantages:** immediate feedback, personal touch, flexible, faster, can use tone and expression, builds rapport.

- **Limitations:** no permanent record, chances of distortion, speaker may be impulsive, not suited to complex data, depends on memory.

### 4.2 Written Communication

- Letters, emails, reports, memos, notices, manuals, circulars.

- **Advantages:** permanent record and legal evidence, careful drafting, wide reach, reference value, suitable for complex details.

- **Limitations:** delayed feedback, costly and time-consuming, needs literacy, lacks personal touch.

### 4.3 Listening (Critical for Effectiveness)

- **Hearing** is a physiological act; **listening** is a psychological, active process of attending, interpreting and responding.

- **Types of listening:** appreciative (for enjoyment), empathic (for emotional support), comprehensive/informational (to learn), critical/evaluative (to judge arguments), discriminative (distinguishing sounds and cues).

- **Active listening** involves attention, eye contact, paraphrasing, clarifying, withholding judgment and giving feedback.

- Barriers to listening: distractions, prejudgment, daydreaming, emotional bias, speaker-listener speed gap.

### 4.4 Tips for Effective Verbal Communication

Know your audience, structure the message (opening, body, closing), use simple and precise language, maintain appropriate pace and volume, use examples, invite and give feedback, and keep the tone respectful.

### 4.5 In simple words: Choosing oral vs written

- **Use oral** when it is urgent, emotional, needs immediate clarification, or involves a small group (e.g., resolving a student's misunderstanding).

- **Use written** when you need a record, legal proof, detailed data, or a message that must reach many people (e.g., an exam notification).

**Why written feels "permanent":** an email can be saved and shown later as proof. That is why contracts, notices and reports are in writing.

### 4.6 In simple words: Listening: why it is hard

People can think much faster than others can speak, so the mind gets spare time and *wanders*. This is the **speed gap**, and it is a major cause of poor listening. Good listeners use that spare time to summarise what they hear and predict the next point.

**Listening types with examples:**

- **Appreciative:** enjoying music or a poem.

- **Empathic:** a friend is upset and you listen to understand their feelings, without rushing to give advice.

- **Comprehensive / informational:** listening to a lecture to learn and remember facts.

- **Critical / evaluative:** listening to a political speech and judging whether the claims are logical.

- **Discriminative:** noticing a change in someone's tone or telling similar sounds apart (the base of other types).

**Active listening steps:** pay full attention, keep eye contact, do not interrupt, nod and use small encouragers, paraphrase ("So you mean..."), ask clarifying questions, and respond with feedback.

### 4.7 In simple words: Written communication basics

Common forms are letters, emails, memos, notices, circulars, reports and minutes. A good written message has a **clear purpose, logical order, simple sentences, correct grammar and a polite close**. In emails, use a meaningful subject line, a greeting, a short body and a sign-off; avoid ALL CAPS (which reads as shouting) and think before pressing "send" (communication is irreversible).

## Chapter 5: Non-Verbal Communication

Non-verbal communication conveys meaning **without words**. It often **reinforces, contradicts, substitutes, complements, regulates or accents** verbal messages. When verbal and non-verbal messages conflict, receivers tend to trust the non-verbal cues (especially for feelings and attitudes).

**Mehrabian's 7-38-55 rule:** 7% words, 38% tone, 55% facial expression. This applies **only** when a person is communicating feelings or attitudes and the cues are inconsistent. It is not a general rule that 93% of all communication is non-verbal. Examiners sometimes test this nuance.

### 5.1 Forms of Non-Verbal Communication

- **Kinesics (body language):** facial expressions, gestures, posture, body movement (term associated with **Ray Birdwhistell**).

  - **Ekman and Friesen** classified gestures as **emblems** (direct verbal translation, e.g., thumbs-up), **illustrators** (accompany speech), **affect displays** (show emotion), **regulators** (control flow of conversation, e.g., nodding) and **adaptors** (self-touching to release tension).

- **Oculesics:** eye contact and gaze.

- **Haptics:** communication through touch (handshake, pat on the back); its acceptability varies by culture, gender and context.

- **Proxemics:** use of **space and distance**, coined by **Edward T. Hall**. Hall's four zones for North American norms:

  - **Intimate:** up to about 18 inches (0 to 1.5 ft)

  - **Personal:** about 1.5 to 4 ft

  - **Social:** about 4 to 12 ft

  - **Public:** beyond 12 ft

- **Chronemics:** use of **time** (punctuality, waiting, pace). Hall also described **monochronic** cultures (one thing at a time, strict schedules) and **polychronic** cultures (multitasking, flexible time).

- **Paralanguage / Vocalics:** **how** something is said: pitch, tone, volume, rate, pauses, stress, fillers. It is *vocal but not verbal*.

- **Appearance and artifacts:** dress, grooming, objects, colour, hairstyle.

- **Olfactics:** smell.

- **Silence:** can show agreement, disapproval, thoughtfulness, respect or anger.

- **Environment / physical setting:** seating arrangement, lighting, decor.

- **Sign language and signals:** traffic signs, sirens, flags, symbols (sometimes called *sign communication* or *object language*).

### 5.2 Characteristics of Non-Verbal Cues

Mostly unconscious, culture-dependent, continuous, harder to control than words, ambiguous in isolation (read cues in **clusters**), and highly influential for emotional meaning.

### 5.3 Non-Verbal Communication in Teaching

Eye contact with students, positive posture, purposeful movement, appropriate gestures, pleasant tone, a smile, and a calm manner help build rapport and attention. Negative cues (frowning, turning your back, folded arms, monotone) reduce participation.

### 5.4 In simple words: "Study of ..." cue list (one of the most asked patterns)

- **Kinesics:** study of body movement, gestures, posture, facial expression.

- **Proxemics:** study of space and distance between people.

- **Haptics:** study of touch.

- **Chronemics:** study of time use (being punctual, making someone wait).

- **Oculesics:** study of eye behaviour and gaze.

- **Olfactics:** study of smell.

- **Paralanguage / vocalics:** study of voice qualities apart from words (pitch, tone, volume, speed, pauses).

- **Artifactics / appearance:** study of dress, objects, and grooming.

- **Semiotics:** study of signs and symbols and how they create meaning.

### 5.5 In simple words: Each one with an everyday example

- **Kinesics:** a teacher's smile and nod when a student answers shows approval; slumped shoulders show tiredness or low confidence.

- **Oculesics:** steady, friendly eye contact signals honesty and interest in many cultures. In some cultures, long direct eye contact with elders can seem disrespectful, so context matters.

- **Haptics:** a firm handshake in a job interview, a pat on a child's back. Appropriate touch depends on culture, gender, age and relationship.

- **Proxemics:** in a crowded lift you stand stiffly because strangers are inside your personal zone. A teacher who walks among desks reduces distance and builds rapport.

- **Chronemics:** arriving late to an interview says "I do not value this" even if you never say it. Making a student wait outside can signal status.

- **Paralanguage:** the sentence "I didn't say she stole the money" changes meaning depending on which word is stressed. Shouting, whispering and a trembling voice carry emotion beyond the words.

- **Artifacts:** a police uniform, a lab coat, or a graduation gown signals role and authority.

- **Silence:** a pause after a question gives students time to think; a long silence after a rude remark shows disapproval.

### 5.6 In simple words: Functions of non-verbal cues in relation to words

- **Repeating:** saying "yes" and nodding.

- **Contradicting:** saying "I am fine" with a sad face (listeners believe the face).

- **Substituting:** waving instead of saying goodbye.

- **Complementing:** a warm smile with a warm greeting.

- **Accenting:** thumping the table to stress a point.

- **Regulating:** raising a hand or nodding to control who speaks next.

**Congruence:** when words and body language match, the message feels honest and credible. When they clash, people trust the body language.

### 5.7 In simple words: Quick note on Mehrabian

Albert Mehrabian's "7-38-55" figures come from studies on how people judge a speaker's *feelings or attitudes* when the words, the tone and the face do not agree. They do **not** show that only 7% of all communication is verbal. If an MCQ states "93% of all communication is non-verbal" as a general fact, treat it with suspicion.

### Memory Capsule: Topic 5

- *Paralanguage* is how you say it (tone, pitch); it is **not** verbal content, but it is *vocal*.
- *Proxemics* = space; *chronemics* = time; *haptics* = touch; *kinesics* = body movement; *oculesics* = eyes; *olfactics* = smell.
- Mehrabian's rule applies only to **feelings and attitudes** with inconsistent messages.

## Chapter 6: Inter-Cultural and Group Communication

### 6.1 Inter-cultural communication: meaning

**Meaning:** communication between people from **different cultural backgrounds** (language, religion, values, customs, communication styles). It requires awareness of how culture shapes perception, meaning and behaviour.

### 6.2 Key Concepts

- **Edward T. Hall – High-context vs low-context cultures:**

  - **High-context** (e.g., Japan, China, Arab countries; India has many high-context features): much of the meaning lies in the context, relationships and non-verbal cues; indirect, implicit communication.

  - **Low-context** (e.g., USA, Germany, Scandinavia): meaning is carried in explicit words; direct, precise, detail-oriented.

- **Geert Hofstede's cultural dimensions:** power distance, individualism vs collectivism, masculinity vs femininity, uncertainty avoidance, long-term vs short-term orientation, and indulgence vs restraint. India is generally characterised by high power distance and collectivism.

- **Ethnocentrism:** judging other cultures by the standards of one's own, assuming one's own culture is superior.

- **Stereotyping and prejudice:** generalised, fixed beliefs or attitudes about a group.

- **Cultural relativism:** understanding behaviour within its own cultural context.

- **Culture shock (Kalervo Oberg):** stages commonly described as honeymoon, crisis/frustration, adjustment and acceptance/adaptation.

### 6.3 Barriers in Inter-Cultural Communication

Language differences and translation problems, different meanings of gestures (e.g., the Indian head wobble, the thumbs-up, the OK sign), different time concepts, different attitudes to hierarchy, ethnocentrism, stereotypes, religion and food taboos, and differing norms of eye contact and personal space.

### 6.4 Improving Inter-Cultural Communication

Develop cultural awareness and sensitivity, learn about other cultures, avoid assumptions, use simple language and avoid slang and idioms, be patient, seek feedback and clarification, listen actively and show respect.

### 6.5 Group communication: meaning

**Meaning:** exchange of information among members of a group (generally 3 to about 20) who share a common goal, interact, and influence each other.

### 6.6 Group Basics

- **Primary groups** (family, close friends; Charles Cooley) vs **secondary groups** (formal, goal-oriented, e.g., committees).

- **Formal vs informal groups; in-group vs out-group.**

- **Group norms, roles, cohesion and status** shape communication.

- **Tuckman's stages of group development (1965; adjourning added 1977):** **Forming, Storming, Norming, Performing, Adjourning.**

- **Benne and Sheats** classified member roles as **task roles, group-building/maintenance roles, and individual (self-centred) roles**.

- **Leadership styles (Kurt Lewin):** autocratic, democratic, laissez-faire.

### 6.7 Group Phenomena

- **Groupthink (Irving Janis, 1972):** the desire for harmony overrides critical thinking.

- **Social loafing (Ringelmann effect):** individuals put in less effort in a group.

- **Group polarization:** group discussion pushes opinions to extremes.

- **Synergy:** the group's combined output exceeds the sum of individual outputs.

### 6.8 Group Communication Formats (Often Asked in Matching)

- **Group discussion (GD):** free exchange among 8 to 12 participants on a topic, assessed on content, communication, leadership and teamwork.

- **Panel discussion:** a small panel of experts discusses a topic before an audience, led by a moderator.

- **Symposium:** several experts present formal papers or talks on different aspects of a topic, usually followed by audience questions.

- **Seminar:** participants present and discuss papers on a theme, usually in an academic setting.

- **Workshop:** hands-on, skill-oriented sessions.

- **Conference:** a large formal gathering for presenting and exchanging research or views.

- **Brainstorming (Alex Osborn):** rapid idea generation with **no criticism** in the idea-generation phase; quantity first.

- **Buzz groups (Phillips 66):** small groups of about six discussing for about six minutes.

- **Debate:** structured argument for and against a motion.

- **Round table, forum, colloquium:** variants of open discussion.

### 6.9 Skills for Effective Group Communication

Listen actively, stay on topic, give everyone an opportunity, summarise points, respect differences, use polite assertiveness, manage conflict constructively and use feedback.

### 6.10 In simple words: High-context vs low-context cultures

- **High-context:** the meaning is "between the lines". People rely on relationships, tone, and unspoken understanding. A polite "that might be difficult" often means "no". Examples often given: Japan, China, many Arab and South Asian cultures.

- **Low-context:** the meaning is in the words. "No" means no; contracts are detailed. Examples often given: USA, Germany, Scandinavia.

**Exam cue:** "indirect, relationship-based, implicit" = high-context; "direct, explicit, detail in words" = low-context.

### 6.11 In simple words: Hofstede's dimensions in plain words

- **Power distance:** how comfortable a society is with unequal power. High power distance: students never question the teacher; titles are important. Low: first names, open challenge to bosses.

- **Individualism vs collectivism:** "I" versus "we". Individualist cultures stress personal goals; collectivist cultures stress family and group loyalty.

- **Masculinity vs femininity:** achievement, competition and success versus caring, quality of life and cooperation.

- **Uncertainty avoidance:** how uncomfortable people feel with ambiguity. High: many rules and detailed plans. Low: flexible and comfortable with risk.

- **Long-term vs short-term orientation:** future-focused thrift and perseverance versus respect for tradition and quick results.

- **Indulgence vs restraint:** freedom to enjoy life versus strict social norms controlling desires.

### 6.12 In simple words: Three terms often mixed up

- **Stereotype** is a *belief* ("all X are lazy").

- **Prejudice** is a *feeling or attitude* (disliking X without evidence).

- **Discrimination** is an *action* (treating X unfairly).

**Ethnocentrism:** treating your own culture as the standard and judging others by it. **Cultural relativism** is its opposite: judging a behaviour in its own cultural context.

### 6.13 In simple words: Culture shock in four stages (simple)

1.  **Honeymoon:** everything is new and exciting.

2.  **Crisis / frustration:** differences become irritating and homesickness appears.

3.  **Adjustment:** you learn local ways and feel more comfortable.

4.  **Acceptance / adaptation:** you function comfortably in the new culture.

### 6.14 In simple words: Group communication simplified

**Group vs crowd:** a *group* has a shared goal, interaction and roles (a project team). A *crowd* is a physical gathering without structure (people at a bus stop).

**Tuckman's stages as a project-team story:**

- **Forming:** members meet, are polite and unsure of roles.

- **Storming:** disagreements and power struggles appear.

- **Norming:** rules and roles are accepted; cooperation grows.

- **Performing:** the team works smoothly and delivers results.

- **Adjourning:** the project ends and the team disbands.

**Group problems explained:**

- **Groupthink:** everyone agrees quickly to avoid conflict, even when the plan is bad (nobody dares say "this will fail").

- **Social loafing:** in a group assignment, one member puts in less effort because they think others will cover.

- **Group polarization:** after discussing among like-minded people, the group's view becomes more extreme than it was at the start.

- **Synergy:** the group achieves more together than the sum of individual efforts.

**Group formats: the distinguishing cue for each**

- **Group discussion:** free exchange among participants, no formal speeches; often used in selection.

- **Panel discussion:** a few experts discuss in front of an audience with a moderator; informal conversation among panellists.

- **Symposium:** each expert gives a *prepared formal presentation* on one aspect of a theme.

- **Seminar:** participants present papers and discuss a theme, usually academic and led by a chairperson.

- **Workshop:** *hands-on* practical skill building.

- **Conference:** a large gathering with many sessions for research and policy exchange.

- **Brainstorming:** generate many ideas *without criticism* first, evaluate later.

- **Debate:** two sides argue for and against a motion.

**Panel vs symposium (common confusion):** panel = *conversation* between experts; symposium = a *series of formal talks* by experts.

- Hall: **proxemics, high and low context, monochronic and polychronic**
- Hofstede: **cultural dimensions**

## Chapter 7: Classroom Communication and Teaching

### 7.1 Meaning and Nature

Classroom communication is the **purposeful, structured exchange of ideas and information between teacher and students (and among students)** to achieve learning objectives. It is generally face to face, goal-directed, and involves verbal, non-verbal and visual elements.

### 7.2 Types of Classroom Communication Flow

- **One-way (teacher to student):** lecture; feedback is minimal.

- **Two-way (teacher to student and back):** question-answer, response, feedback.

- **Three-way / multi-way (interaction among students and teacher):** group work, discussion, peer teaching. Considered the most effective for active learning.

### 7.3 Teacher's Role as Communicator

- Clear objectives and well-organised content, simple and audible language.

- Use of examples, analogies and learner-appropriate vocabulary.

- Effective questioning (clear, brief, graded, distributed fairly, with adequate wait time).

- **Feedback:** immediate, specific, constructive and encouraging.

- Use of teaching aids and ICT to support communication.

- Positive classroom climate, empathy, and acceptance of student ideas.

- Awareness of individual differences, language and cultural background of learners.

### 7.4 Flanders' Interaction Analysis Categories (FIAC) – Memory Favourite

Ned Flanders developed the system to **observe and quantify verbal behaviour in the classroom**. It has **10 categories**, observed typically **every 3 seconds**:

- **Teacher talk – indirect influence (1 to 4):** 1 accepts feelings; 2 praises or encourages; 3 accepts or uses students' ideas; 4 asks questions.

- **Teacher talk – direct influence (5 to 7):** 5 lecturing; 6 giving directions; 7 criticising or justifying authority.

- **Student talk (8 and 9):** 8 student talk – response; 9 student talk – initiation.

- **Silence or confusion (10).**

(Teacher talk = 7 categories; student talk = 2; silence = 1.) A classroom with more indirect influence is seen as more democratic and learner-friendly.

### 7.5 Edgar Dale's Cone of Experience

A model showing a continuum from **concrete (direct, purposeful experiences)** at the base to **abstract (visual and verbal symbols)** at the top. The message: use varied, concrete, multi-sensory experiences for stronger understanding. (Popular percentages linked to the cone are not Dale's own research findings, so do not rely on them.)

### 7.6 Barriers in the Classroom

Language mismatch, poor audibility, a large class size, monotonous delivery, teacher-centred approach, lack of feedback, student anxiety or disinterest, prejudice, inadequate teaching aids, physical discomfort and noise, and distractions from mobile phones.

### 7.7 Improving Classroom Communication

Plan the lesson, use varied methods and media, encourage questions, vary pitch and pace, use positive reinforcement, arrange seating for interaction, use ICT meaningfully, maintain eye contact, and be sensitive to diversity.

### 7.8 Communication in teaching and higher education

- Teaching is a **communication process**: teacher (sender) → content (message) → methods and media (channels) → learner (receiver) → feedback.

- Effective teacher communication is **clear, learner-centric, interactive, supported by ICT**, and aware of barriers.

- **Verbal, non-verbal and visual** modes combine in teaching aids (chalkboard, projector, videos, simulations).

- Online and blended learning rely on **asynchronous and synchronous communication** (discussion forums, video lectures), and need netiquette and digital literacy.

- Feedback should be **timely, specific, constructive**, and should focus on performance, not the person.

### 7.9 In simple words: Flanders' system explained

Imagine an observer sitting at the back with a stopwatch. Every 3 seconds he writes a number from 1 to 10 for what is happening.

- **1 Accepts feelings:** "I can see this topic is worrying you."

- **2 Praises or encourages:** "Very good", "Go on."

- **3 Accepts or uses students' ideas:** "So, as Rahul suggested, we can try this method."

- **4 Asks questions:** "Why do you think it happens?"

- **5 Lectures:** giving facts, opinions or explanation.

- **6 Gives directions:** "Open page 45."

- **7 Criticises or justifies authority:** "Sit down, I am the teacher here."

- **8 Student talk, response:** answering the teacher's question.

- **9 Student talk, initiation:** asking their own question or sharing their own idea.

- **10 Silence or confusion:** pauses, noise, or periods where communication cannot be understood.

**How to remember:** 1 to 4 = *indirect* teacher influence (encourages freedom); 5 to 7 = *direct* influence (restricts freedom); 8 to 9 = *student talk*; 10 = *silence/confusion*.

**Useful ratios (simple meaning):**

- **Teacher talk ratio:** percentage of time the teacher talks.

- **Student talk ratio:** percentage of time students talk.

- **I/D ratio (indirect/direct):** more indirect than direct means a more democratic, student-friendly classroom.

- **Flanders' rule of two-thirds:** in a typical classroom, about two-thirds of the time someone is talking; about two-thirds of that is the teacher talking; and about two-thirds of the teacher's talk is direct influence. This highlights how teacher-dominated most classes are.

### 7.10 In simple words: Questioning and wait time

- **Low-order questions:** recall facts ("What is the capital of India?").

- **High-order questions:** analyse, evaluate, create ("Why might this policy fail?").

- **Wait time:** pausing after a question (research by Mary Budd Rowe suggests around 3 seconds or more) leads to longer, better answers from more students.

- Good practice: ask the question first, pause, then choose who answers; give every student a chance; and respond with praise or probing rather than humiliation.

### 7.11 In simple words: Feedback types

- **Positive / encouraging:** reinforces correct work.

- **Corrective / constructive:** shows what to improve and how.

- **Immediate vs delayed:** immediate feedback works best for new skills.

- **Formative** (during learning) vs **summative** (at the end).

### 7.12 In simple words: Dale's Cone of Experience from base to top

From most concrete to most abstract:

1.  Direct, purposeful experiences

2.  Contrived experiences (models, simulations)

3.  Dramatized experiences (role-play)

4.  Demonstrations

5.  Field trips

6.  Exhibits

7.  Educational television

8.  Motion pictures

9.  Recordings, radio and still pictures

10. Visual symbols (charts, maps)

11. Verbal symbols (spoken and written words)

**Idea:** learners understand better when teaching moves from *concrete experience toward abstract ideas* and uses multiple senses. **Exam cue:** the base is *direct purposeful experience*; the top is *verbal symbols*.

### 7.13 In simple words: A good classroom communicator checklist

- Starts with a clear aim and connects to what students already know.

- Speaks audibly and not too fast, and varies pitch to avoid monotony.

- Uses simple examples and teaching aids.

- Watches students' faces to catch confusion early.

- Encourages questions and treats mistakes as part of learning.

- Uses inclusive language and respects different backgrounds.

- Ends by summarising and checking understanding.

### Memory Capsule: Topic 7

- Flanders: **10-category classroom interaction analysis**
- Dale: **Cone of Experience**

## Chapter 8: Barriers to Effective Communication

A **barrier** is anything that prevents, distorts or delays the correct transmission and understanding of a message. **Noise** is the general term for such interference.

### 8.1 Classification with Examples

- **Physical / environmental barriers:** noise, distance, poor lighting, faulty equipment, network failure, uncomfortable seating, time pressure.

- **Semantic / language barriers:** ambiguous words, jargon, technical terms, slang, poor vocabulary, different meanings of the same word, translation errors, accent, bad grammar, symbols interpreted differently.

- **Psychological / emotional barriers:** stress, anger, fear, anxiety, prejudice, stereotyping, closed mind, attitudes, mistrust, inattention, shyness, low self-esteem, selective perception, **halo effect**, defensiveness.

- **Personal barriers:** lack of communication skills, poor listening, lack of interest, poor memory, differences in intelligence or education, lack of confidence.

- **Organizational barriers:** rigid hierarchy, too many levels, status differences, poor communication policies, information overload, lack of feedback channels, complexity of structure, unclear authority.

- **Cultural barriers:** different customs, norms, values, gestures and attitudes toward time and hierarchy.

- **Technological barriers:** technical failure, lack of access to technology, digital divide, information overload, over-reliance on technology.

- **Perceptual barriers:** people interpret the same message differently because of different backgrounds and mindsets.

- **Gender and status barriers:** differences in communication styles or perceived power.

- **Mechanical barriers** (sometimes listed separately): failure of the medium or machine.

### 8.2 Commonly Tested Specific Barriers

- **Filtering:** the sender manipulates or edits information so the receiver sees it as favourable (common in upward communication).

- **Selective perception:** receivers see and hear according to their needs, motivation and experiences.

- **Information overload:** too much information to process.

- **Emotions:** strong feelings distort reception.

- **Defensiveness:** reacting to a perceived threat by blaming or avoiding.

- **Gatekeeping:** a person or agency controlling what information passes through.

- **Jargon:** specialised language understood only by insiders.

- **Poor timing, lack of trust, and status differences.**

### 8.3 Overcoming Barriers

Use clear and simple language; know the receiver; get and give feedback; use multiple channels; listen actively; develop empathy; use good timing; reduce noise and distractions; use proper organisational structure with open channels; train people in communication skills; and keep messages concise and well organised.

### 8.4 Scenario-to-barrier cue guide

MCQs often describe a situation and ask which barrier is operating. Match the *cause* of the failure.

- **A loud fan or poor microphone made the lecture inaudible** → *physical / mechanical barrier (noise).*

- **The teacher used technical jargon with first-year students** → *semantic / language barrier.*

- **A student is so anxious about exams that he cannot absorb the instructions** → *psychological / emotional barrier.*

- **A listener has already decided the speaker is wrong and stops listening** → *psychological barrier (prejudgement / closed mind).*

- **An employee reports only good news to the boss and hides problems** → *filtering.*

- **Two people watch the same event and describe it very differently because of their own views** → *selective perception / perceptual barrier.*

- **A manager sends 200 emails a day and staff skim them** → *information overload.*

- **A junior is scared to question a senior** → *status or organizational barrier.*

- **Message passes through many levels and changes** → *organizational barrier (too many levels leads to distortion).*

- **A gesture that is polite in one country is rude in another** → *cultural barrier.*

- **Rural students with no internet cannot attend an online class** → *technological barrier (digital divide).*

- **A newspaper editor drops an important story** → *gatekeeping.*

- **A speaker is judged by one good quality and all else is assumed good** → *halo effect.*

**Barrier vs noise:** a barrier is a specific obstacle; **noise** is the broad term for anything that interferes with the message. In MCQs, "noise" can be physical, technical, psychological or semantic.

**How to overcome (match to the problem):** language barrier → simple words and explain terms; physical → good venue and equipment; psychological → empathy, trust, patience; filtering → open-door policy and anonymous feedback; overload → prioritise and summarise; cultural → cultural awareness; status → participative culture; poor listening → active listening and feedback.

### Memory Capsule: Topic 8

- *Semantic barrier* = language and meaning; *psychological barrier* = emotions and attitudes; *physical barrier* = noise and environment.

## Chapter 9: Mass Media and Society

### 9.1 Mass Communication – Meaning and Features

Mass communication is the process of sending messages through **technological media** to **large, heterogeneous, anonymous and widely scattered audiences**.

- **Source:** organised institution or professional communicator.

- **Message:** public, standardised, produced in large quantities.

- **Audience:** large, diverse, anonymous.

- **Feedback:** indirect and delayed (letters to the editor, TRP ratings, social media reactions).

- **Gatekeeping** determines what reaches the audience.

- It is **impersonal**, one-to-many, and uses mechanical or electronic means.

### 9.2 Types of Media

- **Print:** newspapers, magazines, books, journals, pamphlets.

- **Electronic/broadcast:** radio, television, film.

- **Digital / new media:** the internet, social media, blogs, podcasts, OTT streaming, mobile apps. They are interactive and enable user-generated content, personalisation and instant global reach.

- **Traditional / folk media:** street plays, puppetry, folk songs, nautanki, kathputli, harikatha. Valuable for development communication in rural India.

- **Outdoor / mixed media:** hoardings, posters, signage.

### 9.3 Functions of Mass Media

- **Lasswell's functions (1948):** **surveillance** of the environment, **correlation** of the parts of society, and **transmission** of the social heritage.

- **Charles Wright added entertainment.**

- Broadly: to **inform, educate, entertain, persuade/influence, set the agenda, mobilise, and act as a watchdog**. Media also supports development communication.

### 9.4 Key Media and Communication Theories (Very High Memory Value)

- **Hypodermic needle / magic bullet theory:** media directly and uniformly inject messages into passive audiences with powerful effects. It was influenced by propaganda concerns of the 1920s to 1930s and the panic after the 1938 *War of the Worlds* radio broadcast. It is now considered simplistic.

- **Two-step flow theory (Lazarsfeld, Berelson and Gaudet, 1944; developed by Katz and Lazarsfeld, 1955):** media messages reach **opinion leaders** first, who then influence others. It emphasises personal influence over direct media effect.

- **Uses and gratifications theory (Katz, Blumler and Gurevitch, 1974):** audiences are **active** and use media to satisfy needs (information, entertainment, social interaction, identity, escape). The question moves from "what does media do to people?" to "**what do people do with media?**"

- **Agenda-setting theory (McCombs and Shaw, 1972):** media may not tell people **what to think** but tell them **what to think about** (issue salience).

- **Cultivation theory (George Gerbner):** long-term heavy exposure to television shapes viewers' perception of reality; related to the **"mean world syndrome"**.

- **Spiral of silence (Elisabeth Noelle-Neumann, 1974):** people hesitate to express opinions they believe are in the minority for fear of isolation, and the dominant opinion appears stronger.

- **Gatekeeping theory (Kurt Lewin; David Manning White's "Mr. Gates" study, 1950):** editors and others decide which news passes through.

- **Media dependency theory (Ball-Rokeach and DeFleur, 1976):** the more a person depends on media to meet needs, the more influence media has on them.

- **Knowledge gap hypothesis (Tichenor, Donohue and Olien, 1970):** groups with higher socio-economic status gain information from mass media faster, widening the gap.

- **Diffusion of innovations (Everett Rogers):** adopters categorised as innovators (2.5%), early adopters (13.5%), early majority (34%), late majority (34%) and laggards (16%).

- **Framing theory (Goffman; Entman):** how an issue is presented influences how audiences interpret it.

- **Marshall McLuhan:** **"The medium is the message"**; the concept of the **global village**; *Understanding Media* (1964). **Hot media** (high definition, low participation, e.g., radio, film, print) vs **cool media** (low definition, high participation, e.g., television, telephone).

- **Propaganda model (Herman and Chomsky, *Manufacturing Consent*, 1988):** news is filtered by ownership, advertising, sourcing, flak and a common ideology.

- **Frankfurt School (Adorno and Horkheimer):** the **culture industry** critique.

- **Habermas:** the **public sphere**.

### 9.5 Normative Theories of the Press

*Four Theories of the Press* (**Siebert, Peterson and Schramm, 1956**):

- **Authoritarian:** the press serves the ruler or state; control by licensing and censorship.

- **Libertarian:** a free marketplace of ideas; minimal state control.

- **Social responsibility:** freedom with responsibility to society; ethics and professional codes (Hutchins Commission, 1947).

- **Soviet communist:** the press is an instrument of the party and state.

**Denis McQuail** later added **development media theory** (media supports national development) and **democratic-participant theory** (access for citizens and community groups).

### 9.6 Impact of Media on Society

- **Positive:** awareness, education, democratic participation, public opinion, social reform, cultural exchange, development communication, rapid information in disasters, and economic benefits through advertising and employment.

- **Negative:** misinformation and fake news, sensationalism, paid news, commercialisation, cultural homogenisation, violence and consumerism, privacy invasion, addiction, trial by media, cyberbullying, echo chambers and polarisation.

- **Media literacy:** the ability to access, analyse, evaluate and create media; a key remedy.

- **Digital divide:** unequal access to digital technology.

- **Media ethics:** accuracy, objectivity, fairness, accountability, privacy, and avoiding hate speech.

### 9.7 Indian Media: Facts Frequently Tested

- **First Indian newspaper:** *Hicky's Bengal Gazette* (1780, James Augustus Hicky, also called *Bengal Gazette*).

- **Samachar Darpan** (1818, Serampore) is an early Bengali weekly. **Raja Ram Mohan Roy** published *Sambad Kaumudi* (1821) and *Mirat-ul-Akhbar* (1822).

- **All India Radio:** the Indian Broadcasting Service was renamed **All India Radio in 1936**; the name **Akashvani** was adopted in 1956. Its motto: *Bahujan Hitaya, Bahujan Sukhaya*.

- **Doordarshan:** began as an experimental telecast in **Delhi on 15 September 1959**; regular service from 1965; colour broadcasts in **1982**, around the Asian Games.

- **Prasar Bharati** (Broadcasting Corporation of India): created by the **Prasar Bharati Act, 1990** and operational from **1997**; it governs AIR and Doordarshan.

- **Press Council of India:** first set up in **1966**; functioning currently under the **Press Council Act, 1978**; a statutory, quasi-judicial body that preserves the freedom of the press and maintains and improves press standards. **National Press Day: 16 November.**

- **Freedom of the press** in India is derived from **Article 19(1)(a)** (freedom of speech and expression), subject to reasonable restrictions under Article 19(2).

- **Cinematograph Act, 1952:** film certification through the **CBFC**.

- **Information Technology Act, 2000:** governs cyber law and electronic communication; **Right to Information Act, 2005:** transparency.

- **Community radio** and **educational channels** (e.g., Gyan Darshan, Gyan Vani, SWAYAM Prabha) support educational communication and the National Education Policy goals.

- **International days:** World Press Freedom Day, **3 May**; World Radio Day, 13 February; World Television Day, 21 November.

### 9.8 In simple words: Mass communication vs interpersonal communication

- **Interpersonal:** few people, face to face, immediate feedback, personal.

- **Mass:** large, anonymous audience, through technology, delayed feedback, impersonal and organised by institutions.

### 9.9 In simple words: Media theories in plain language with examples

- **Hypodermic needle / magic bullet:** the media "inject" ideas straight into a passive audience. Example: believing every viral video instantly. *Now seen as too simple* because audiences think and choose.

- **Two-step flow:** media message reaches **opinion leaders** (people others trust), who pass it on with their own interpretation. Example: your aunt reads the newspaper and tells the family what to think about a new law.

- **Uses and gratifications:** audiences are **active** and choose media for specific needs. Example: you watch cricket for excitement, news for information, reels to relax. The focus is on *what people do with media*.

- **Agenda setting:** media decide what issues seem important. Example: if every channel talks about inflation all week, viewers say inflation is the main problem. Media tell us *what to think about*, not necessarily *what to think*.

- **Cultivation:** years of heavy TV watching make the world seem more like TV shows it. Example: heavy viewers of crime shows believe the world is more dangerous than it really is ("mean world syndrome").

- **Spiral of silence:** people who think their opinion is in the minority stay quiet, making the majority view look even stronger. Example: in a class WhatsApp group, one student with a different view says nothing.

- **Gatekeeping:** an editor chooses 10 stories out of 1,000 available. The gatekeeper's judgement shapes what the public knows.

- **Framing:** the same fact, different packaging. "Tax relief for businesses" versus "tax cut for the rich" creates different reactions.

- **Media dependency:** in a crisis (floods, election night) people depend more on media, and so media influence increases.

- **Knowledge gap:** educated, wealthy groups learn from media faster than poorer groups, so the gap widens.

- **Diffusion of innovations:** how a new idea or product spreads: innovators (2.5%) first, then early adopters (13.5%), early majority (34%), late majority (34%) and laggards (16%).

- **McLuhan:** the *form* of the medium shapes society more than the content. *Global village:* electronic media shrink the world into one village. **Hot media** give a lot of detail and need little effort from you (radio, film, print); **cool media** give less detail and need you to participate (TV, telephone, in McLuhan's classification).

### 9.10 In simple words: The story of media effects (useful for analysis questions)

1.  **Strong effects era (1920s to 1940s):** hypodermic needle idea after propaganda in the World Wars and the 1938 radio drama panic.

2.  **Limited effects era (1940s to 1960s):** research like the two-step flow showed personal influence matters and media effects are limited.

3.  **Return of powerful (long-term) effects (1970s onward):** agenda setting, cultivation, spiral of silence.

4.  **Active audience (1970s onward):** uses and gratifications.

5.  **Digital era:** interactive, user-generated, algorithm-driven media, filter bubbles and echo chambers.

### 9.11 In simple words: Four theories of the press, simplified

- **Authoritarian:** the ruler controls the press (licences, censorship). *Example idea:* kings and early colonial regimes.

- **Libertarian:** the press is free; truth wins in a "marketplace of ideas". *Example idea:* liberal democracies with minimal regulation.

- **Social responsibility:** freedom comes with duties to society; professional ethics and codes. *Example idea:* press councils.

- **Soviet communist:** the press serves the party and state.

- **McQuail's additions:** *development media theory* (media should support national development) and *democratic-participant theory* (citizens and communities should have access to media).

### 9.12 In simple words: New / digital media

- **Features:** interactivity, hypertext, user-generated content, 24x7 availability, global reach, convergence (text, audio and video together), and personalisation by algorithms.

- **Filter bubble** (Eli Pariser): algorithms show you only what you already like.

- **Echo chamber:** you hear only people who agree with you, so your beliefs get reinforced.

- **Types of false information:** *misinformation* (false, shared without intent to harm), *disinformation* (false, shared deliberately to deceive) and *malinformation* (true information used to cause harm, e.g., leaking private details).

- **Digital divide:** the gap between those who have access to technology and skills and those who do not. **Media literacy:** the skill of accessing, analysing and creating media responsibly.

### 9.13 In simple words: Development communication and traditional media

- **Development communication** uses communication to support social and economic development (health, farming, education, literacy).

- India examples frequently cited: the **Satellite Instructional Television Experiment (SITE), 1975-76**, which used satellite TV for rural education; the **Kheda Communication Project**; and Doordarshan's farm programme **Krishi Darshan (1967)**.

- **Folk and traditional media** like puppetry, street theatre, folk songs and storytelling are low-cost, local-language and trusted, so they remain useful for health and social campaigns in rural areas.

### 9.14 In simple words: Media regulation in India (quick map)

- **Press Council of India:** print media and journalistic ethics.

- **CBFC (Central Board of Film Certification):** films, under the Cinematograph Act, 1952.

- **Prasar Bharati:** public broadcaster (AIR and Doordarshan).

- **TRAI (1997):** telecom and broadcasting tariff regulation.

- **Cable Television Networks (Regulation) Act, 1995:** regulates cable TV.

- **ASCI (Advertising Standards Council of India):** self-regulation of advertising.

- **NBDSA:** self-regulation for news broadcasters.

- **Information Technology Act, 2000, and the IT Rules, 2021:** digital and social media intermediaries.

- **Article 19(1)(a):** the constitutional basis of press freedom; **Article 19(2):** reasonable restrictions (security of the state, public order, decency, defamation, etc.).

### 10.3 Common confusions to avoid

**Common confusions to avoid:**

- *Paralanguage* is how you say it (tone, pitch); it is **not** verbal content, but it is *vocal*.

- *Proxemics* = space; *chronemics* = time; *haptics* = touch; *kinesics* = body movement; *oculesics* = eyes; *olfactics* = smell.

- *Hypodermic needle* = powerful direct effect; *two-step flow* = effect through opinion leaders; *uses and gratifications* = active audience.

- *Agenda setting* = what to think **about**; *cultivation* = **long-term** shaping of beliefs about reality.

- *Downward* = superior to subordinate; *upward* = subordinate to superior; *horizontal* = same level; *diagonal* = different level and different department.

- *Grapevine* is informal; its **cluster chain** is the most common.

- *Semantic barrier* = language and meaning; *psychological barrier* = emotions and attitudes; *physical barrier* = noise and environment.

- *Intrapersonal* = within oneself; *interpersonal* = two people; *mass* = large, anonymous audience with delayed feedback.

- Mehrabian's rule applies only to **feelings and attitudes** with inconsistent messages.

### 10.4 Strategy for analysis-type MCQs

**Strategy for analysis-type MCQs:**

- In statement-based questions, test each statement independently first, then match with the options.

- In assertion–reason questions, check (1) whether the assertion is true, (2) whether the reason is true, and (3) whether the reason actually explains the assertion.

- In match-the-following questions, lock the pairs you are 100% sure of first and eliminate options.

- Watch for absolute words such as *always, only, never, all*; they often make a statement false.

- In "NOT / EXCEPT" questions, underline the negative and check each option against the rule.

- No negative marking: never leave a question blank.
