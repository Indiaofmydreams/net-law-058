# Constitutional and Administrative Law: Detailed Notes for UGC NET Law (058)

## Chapter 1: Preamble

### 1.1 What Is the Preamble?

The Preamble is the introductory statement of the Constitution setting out its guiding purposes, principles, and philosophy. It is NOT a source of legislative or executive power, but IS an interpretive tool to understand constitutional intent. Adopted November 26, 1949; Constitution came into force January 26, 1950.

### 1.2 Full Text (As Amended)

WE, THE PEOPLE OF INDIA, having solemnly resolved to constitute India into a SOVEREIGN SOCIALIST SECULAR DEMOCRATIC REPUBLIC and to secure to all its citizens: JUSTICE, social, economic and political; LIBERTY of thought, expression, belief, faith and worship; EQUALITY of status and of opportunity; and to promote among them all FRATERNITY assuring the dignity of the individual and the unity and integrity of the Nation; IN OUR CONSTITUENT ASSEMBLY this twenty-sixth day of November, 1949, do HEREBY ADOPT, ENACT AND GIVE TO OURSELVES THIS CONSTITUTION.

### 1.3 Key Concepts Explained

**Sovereign** — India is not subject to any external authority. No foreign power can dictate governance. India can acquire or cede territory. Tested in Berubari Union Case (1960).

**Socialist** — Added by 42nd Amendment, 1976. Mixed economy, not pure communism. State must minimise inequalities of income and status. In Excel Wear v. Union of India (1978), SC clarified 'socialist' does not mean State can nationalise everything.

**Secular** — Added by 42nd Amendment, 1976. India follows POSITIVE secularism — State has no religion of its own but treats all religions equally (Sarva Dharma Sambhava). Distinctly different from Western negative secularism which rigidly excludes religion from public life.

**Democratic** — Sovereignty rests with the people, exercised through elected representatives. Includes adult suffrage (Article 326), free and fair elections, parliamentary form of government.

**Republic** — Head of State (President) is elected, not hereditary. President is indirectly elected through electoral college.

**Justice** — Three dimensions: Social (no discrimination), Economic (equitable distribution), Political (equal political rights). Inspired by Russian Revolution ideals.

**Liberty** — Liberty of thought, expression, belief, faith, worship — linked to Article 19. Not absolute; subject to reasonable restrictions.

**Equality** — Equality of status and opportunity — linked to Articles 14, 15, 16. Includes formal equality (before law) and substantive equality (actual equal treatment). Permits reasonable classification and protective discrimination.

**Fraternity** — Brotherhood among citizens assuring dignity of the individual and unity and integrity of the Nation. The ONLY value in the Preamble without a corresponding justiciable Fundamental Right — it is a moral and social value permeating the whole Constitution.

### 1.4 Is the Preamble Part of the Constitution?

- Berubari Union Case (1960): Preamble is NOT part of the Constitution.

- Kesavananda Bharati v. State of Kerala (1973): OVERRULED Berubari — Preamble IS part of the Constitution; can be amended under Article 368 but basic features cannot be destroyed.

- LIC v. Consumer Education Research Centre (1995): Reaffirmed Preamble is part of the Constitution.

### 1.5 Amendability

Yes — 42nd Amendment (1976) added 'Socialist', 'Secular', 'Integrity'. However, basic structure features (sovereignty, democracy, republic, secularism) cannot be destroyed even by amendment (Kesavananda Bharati Basic Structure Doctrine).

### Memory Capsule: Chapter 1

- **SSSDR**: Sovereign → Socialist → Secular → Democratic → Republic

- **JLEF**: Justice → Liberty → Equality → Fraternity

- Socialist + Secular + Integrity — all three added together by 42nd Amendment, 1976

## Chapter 2: Fundamental Rights (Articles 12–35)

### 2.1 Overview and Significance

Part III (Articles 12–35) contains Fundamental Rights — justiciable rights enforceable by courts against the State. Dr. Ambedkar called Article 32 the "heart and soul" of the Constitution. These rights are available against the State as defined in Article 12. Two exceptions apply against private individuals also: Article 17 (untouchability) and Article 23 (forced labour/trafficking).

### 2.2 Article 12: Definition of 'State'

For Part III purposes, 'State' includes: Government and Parliament of India; Government and Legislature of each State; all local or other authorities within India; all local or other authorities under the control of Government of India.

Key expansion through cases:

- R.D. Shetty v. International Airport Authority (1979): Body that is an instrumentality or agency of Government is 'State' under Article 12.

- Pradeep Kumar Biswas v. IICB (2002): 7-judge bench — 'functional test': pervasive governmental control + financial dependence + public importance of functions.

- Ajay Hasia v. Khalid Mujib (1981): Societies substantially financed and controlled by Government are 'State'.

### 2.3 Article 13: Laws Inconsistent with FRs

**Article 13(1):** Pre-constitutional laws inconsistent with FRs are void to the extent of inconsistency — **Doctrine of Eclipse** applies (law is dormant, revives if FR amended to remove inconsistency — Bhikhaji Narain Dhakras v. State of MP, 1955).

**Article 13(2):** State shall NOT make any law abridging FRs — void ab initio. **Doctrine of Severability** applies — only offending provision severed, rest valid if separable (R.M.D. Chamarbaugwala v. Union of India, 1957).

**Article 13(3):** 'Law' includes ordinances, regulations, bye-laws, rules, notifications, customs, usages. Constitutional amendments are NOT 'law' under Article 13 — subject to Basic Structure doctrine.

**Doctrine of Eclipse:** Pre-constitutional law inconsistent with FR becomes dormant (eclipsed) but not dead. If the FR is subsequently amended to remove the inconsistency, the law automatically revives. Does NOT apply to post-constitutional laws — those are void ab initio.

**Doctrine of Severability:** Only the unconstitutional part of a law is struck down; the rest remains valid if the valid and invalid portions are separable. Test: Would Parliament have enacted the law even without the invalid portion?

**Doctrine of Waiver:** Fundamental Rights cannot be waived — they are given as a matter of public policy and cannot be contracted away (Behram Khurshid Pesikaka v. State of Bombay, 1955).

### 2.4 Right to Equality (Articles 14–18)

**Article 14 — Equality Before Law and Equal Protection of Laws**

Two distinct guarantees: (1) Equality before law — negative concept, no special privileges (from English Rule of Law — Dicey); (2) Equal protection of laws — positive concept, equals to be treated equally (from US 14th Amendment).

Twin tests of reasonable classification:

- The classification must be based on intelligible differentia (clear and understandable basis)

- The differentia must have rational nexus to the object sought to be achieved

Evolution of Article 14 through case law:

- State of West Bengal v. Anwar Ali Sarkar (1952): Twin tests laid down

- E.P. Royappa v. State of Tamil Nadu (1974): Arbitrariness violates Article 14 — equality is antithetic to arbitrariness

- Maneka Gandhi v. Union of India (1978): Article 14 read with Articles 19 and 21 — 'golden triangle'

- Shayara Bano v. Union of India (2017): Triple talaq struck down as manifestly arbitrary under Article 14

**Article 15 — Prohibition of Discrimination**

Article 15(1): State shall not discriminate on grounds ONLY of religion, race, caste, sex, or place of birth. Language and domicile are NOT included.

Article 15(2): No citizen shall face disabilities in access to public places on these grounds alone.

Article 15(3): State may make special provisions for women and children — exception to 15(1).

Article 15(4): State may make special provisions for socially/educationally backward classes or SC/ST — inserted by 1st Amendment (1951) after Champakam Dorairajan case.

Article 15(5): Reservation for backward classes/SC/ST in private educational institutions — 93rd Amendment (2005), upheld in Ashoka Kumar Thakur v. Union of India (2008).

Article 15(6): 10% EWS reservation — 103rd Amendment (2019), upheld in Janhit Abhiyan v. Union of India (2022) by 3:2 majority.

**Article 16 — Equality of Opportunity in Public Employment**

Article 16(4): Reservations for backward classes not adequately represented in State services. 50% ceiling laid down in Indra Sawhney v. Union of India (1992) — the Mandal Case. Creamy layer to be excluded.

Article 16(4-A): Reservation in promotion for SC/ST — 77th Amendment (1995).

Article 16(4-B): Unfilled SC/ST vacancies carried forward — not subject to 50% cap — 81st Amendment (2000).

Article 16(6): 10% EWS reservation in public employment — 103rd Amendment (2019).

**Article 17 — Abolition of Untouchability** Practice forbidden in any form; offence punishable by law. Applies against private individuals too. Protection of Civil Rights Act, 1955 enforces this.

**Article 18 — Abolition of Titles** No title (except military/academic) shall be conferred by the State. Bharat Ratna and Padma awards are NOT 'titles' under Article 18 — Balaji Raghavan v. Union of India (1996).

### 2.5 Right to Freedom (Article 19)

SIX freedoms guaranteed to CITIZENS only:

- **(a) Speech and expression:** Includes freedom of press, right to know, commercial speech, right to silence. Restrictions (Art. 19(2)): sovereignty, integrity, security, friendly relations, public order, decency, morality, contempt, defamation, incitement.

- **(b) Peaceful assembly without arms:** Restrictions (Art. 19(3)): sovereignty, integrity, public order.

- **(c) Form associations/unions:** Includes trade unions, political parties. Restrictions (Art. 19(4)): sovereignty, integrity, public order, morality.

- **(d) Move freely throughout India:** Restrictions (Art. 19(5)): general public interest, protection of Scheduled Tribes.

- **(e) Reside and settle anywhere:** Restrictions (Art. 19(5)): same as above.

- **(g) Practise any profession/occupation/trade/business:** Restrictions (Art. 19(6)): professional qualifications, State monopoly.

Note: Article 19(1)(f) (right to property) was deleted by 44th Amendment, 1978. Now only 6 freedoms.

All restrictions must be: (i) imposed by law, (ii) reasonable, (iii) in relation to specified grounds only.

### 2.6 Right to Life and Personal Liberty (Articles 20–22)

**Article 20 — Triple Protection (non-suspendable even during Emergency):**

1.  No ex post facto criminal law

2.  No double jeopardy (Nemo bis punitur pro eodem delicto)

3.  No compelled self-incrimination

Selvi v. State of Karnataka (2010): Narco-analysis, brain mapping, polygraph without consent violate Article 20(3).

**Article 21 — Right to Life and Personal Liberty (most expansive FR):**

Narrow start: A.K. Gopalan v. State of Madras (1950) — any procedure prescribed by law suffices. Landmark expansion: Maneka Gandhi v. Union of India (1978) — procedure must be just, fair, and reasonable. Substantive due process imported.

Post-Maneka judicially expanded rights under Article 21:

- Right to livelihood (Olga Tellis v. BMC, 1985)

- Right to speedy trial (Hussainara Khatoon, 1979)

- Right to legal aid (M.H. Hoskot v. State of Maharashtra, 1978)

- Right to education — now Article 21-A (Unnikrishnan JP v. State of AP, 1993)

- Right to clean environment (M.C. Mehta v. Union of India, 1987)

- Right to privacy (K.S. Puttaswamy v. Union of India, 2017 — 9-judge bench)

- Right to die with dignity — passive euthanasia (Common Cause v. Union of India, 2018)

- Right to health and medical care (Paschim Banga Khet Mazdoor Samity, 1996)

- Right to shelter (Chameli Singh v. State of UP, 1996)

**Article 21-A — Right to Education:** Inserted by 86th Amendment (2002). Free and compulsory education for children aged 6–14. Implemented by Right of Children to Free and Compulsory Education Act, 2009.

**Article 22 — Protection Against Arrest:** Two parts: (1) Rights of arrested person — inform grounds, right to legal counsel, produce before magistrate within 24 hours; (2) Preventive detention — Advisory Board required for detention beyond 3 months.

### 2.7 Right Against Exploitation (Articles 23–24)

**Article 23:** Prohibits trafficking, begar, and forced labour. Applies against State AND private individuals. Bandhua Mukti Morcha v. Union of India (1984): SC ordered identification, release, and rehabilitation of bonded labourers.

**Article 24:** No child below 14 years in factories, mines, or other hazardous employment. Child Labour (Prohibition and Regulation) Amendment Act, 2016 expanded the prohibition.

### 2.8 Right to Freedom of Religion (Articles 25–28)

**Article 25:** Right to freely profess, practise, and propagate religion — subject to public order, morality, health. State can regulate secular activities associated with religion.

**Article 26:** Religious denomination's right to establish institutions, manage religious affairs, own property.

**Article 27:** No compulsion to pay taxes for promotion of any particular religion. Distinction between 'tax' (compulsory — Art. 27 applies) and 'fee' (for services — Art. 27 does not apply).

**Article 28:** No religious instruction in institutions wholly maintained out of State funds.

### 2.9 Cultural and Educational Rights (Articles 29–30)

**Article 29:** Right of any section of citizens with distinct language/script/culture to conserve the same. No denial of admission to State-maintained/aided institutions on grounds of religion, race, caste, or language.

**Article 30:** All religious and linguistic minorities have right to establish and administer educational institutions of their choice. State can regulate to ensure academic standards. T.M.A. Pai Foundation v. State of Karnataka (2002) — 11-judge bench laid down the law.

### 2.10 Right to Constitutional Remedies (Article 32)

'Heart and soul' of the Constitution (Dr. Ambedkar). Five writs: habeas corpus, mandamus, prohibition, certiorari, quo warranto. Article 32 is itself a Fundamental Right — cannot be suspended except under Article 359 during National Emergency (and even then Arts. 20 and 21 remain enforceable).

## Chapter 3: Fundamental Duties (Article 51-A)

### 3.1 Background

Not in original Constitution. Inserted by 42nd Constitutional Amendment Act, 1976 (Swaran Singh Committee recommendation) — borrowed from USSR Constitution. 86th Amendment (2002) added the 11th duty.

### 3.2 The 11 Fundamental Duties

1.  Abide by the Constitution and respect its ideals, institutions, National Flag, National Anthem

2.  Cherish noble ideals of national freedom struggle

3.  Uphold and protect sovereignty, unity, integrity of India

4.  Defend the country and render national service when called upon

5.  Promote harmony and common brotherhood; renounce practices derogatory to women's dignity

6.  Value and preserve rich heritage of composite culture

7.  Protect and improve natural environment; compassion for living creatures

8.  Develop scientific temper, humanism, spirit of inquiry and reform

9.  Safeguard public property; abjure violence

10. Strive towards excellence in all spheres of individual and collective activity

11. Provide opportunities for education to child/ward between 6–14 years (86th Amendment, 2002)

### 3.3 Key Features

- NON-JUSTICIABLE — no court can directly enforce or punish breach

- Apply to CITIZENS only (not non-citizens)

- Parliament CAN pass laws to enforce duties indirectly — protected from Article 19 challenges (AIIMS Students Union v. AIIMS, 2001)

- Courts use them to balance rights against responsibilities and interpret ambiguous laws

- Verma Committee (J.S. Verma, 1999) noted duties have legal dimensions in appropriate contexts

- Ranganatha Misra Commission (2009) recommended making some duties justiciable — not yet implemented

## Chapter 4: Directive Principles of State Policy (Articles 36–51)

### 4.1 Nature and Significance

Part IV (Articles 36–51) contains DPSPs. Non-justiciable (Article 37 — "shall not be enforceable by any court") but fundamental in the governance of the country. Borrowed from the Irish Constitution (1937). Dr. Ambedkar described them as a 'novel feature.' Aim: to establish a welfare state.

### 4.2 Classification

**Socialistic Directives:**

- Article 38: State to secure social order for welfare of people

- Article 39: Equal right to livelihood (a); equal pay for equal work (d); protection of children from exploitation (f)

- Article 39-A: Equal justice and free legal aid — 42nd Amendment, 1976

- Article 41: Right to work, education, public assistance in unemployment/sickness

- Article 42: Just and humane conditions of work and maternity relief

- Article 43: Living wage for workers

- Article 43-A: Workers' participation in management — 42nd Amendment, 1976

- Article 47: Raise standard of living; prohibition of intoxicating drinks and drugs

**Gandhian Directives:**

- Article 40: Organise village panchayats

- Article 43: Promote cottage industries

- Article 43-B: Promotion of co-operative societies — 97th Amendment, 2011

- Article 46: Promote educational and economic interests of SC, ST, weaker sections

- Article 48: Organise agriculture and animal husbandry; prohibit slaughter of cattle

**Liberal Intellectualist Directives:**

- Article 44: Uniform Civil Code

- Article 45: Early childhood care (amended by 86th Amendment)

- Article 48-A: Protection and improvement of environment — 42nd Amendment, 1976

- Article 49: Protection of monuments

- Article 50: Separation of judiciary from executive

- Article 51: Promotion of international peace

### 4.3 FRs vs. DPSPs: The Constitutional Journey

This is the single most analytically important topic in this Unit.

- **Champakam Dorairajan (1951):** FRs prevail over DPSPs → prompted 1st Amendment (Art. 15(4))

- **Golak Nath (1967):** Parliament cannot amend FRs → DPSPs forever subordinate

- **Kesavananda Bharati (1973):** Parliament can amend FRs but not destroy basic structure → balance restored

- **Minerva Mills (1980):** 42nd Amendment's absolute primacy to DPSPs struck down — harmony between Parts III and IV IS itself a basic feature. Neither can completely override the other.

- **Waman Rao v. Union of India (1981):** Laws giving effect to DPSPs can go in Ninth Schedule — subject to I.R. Coelho (2007) limitation (laws after April 24, 1973 can be challenged if they violate basic structure)

### 4.4 Key DPSPs That Led to Legislation

- Article 39(b)/(c) → Nationalisation of industries, land reform laws

- Article 40 → 73rd and 74th Amendments (Panchayati Raj and Municipal Bodies)

- Article 44 → Debate on Uniform Civil Code (Shah Bano, Sarla Mudgal; Uttarakhand UCC 2024)

- Article 48-A → Environment Protection Act, 1986

- Article 50 → Gradual separation of judiciary from executive through various Acts

## Chapter 5: Union Executive

### 5.1 The President of India

**Constitutional Position:** Executive power of the Union vested in President under Article 53. The President is the constitutional head — acts on the aid and advice of the Council of Ministers (Article 74). Not a rubber stamp but has limited real discretionary powers.

**Qualifications (Article 58):** Citizen of India; at least 35 years; qualified as Lok Sabha member; must not hold office of profit.

**Election (Article 54):** Indirect election through electoral college — elected members of both Houses of Parliament AND elected members of State Legislative Assemblies (including Delhi and Puducherry). Nominated members and members of State Legislative Councils excluded. Single transferable vote system with proportional representation to ensure parity between States.

**Term:** 5 years (Article 56). Eligible for re-election. Only President elected twice: Rajendra Prasad.

**Removal (Article 61):** Impeachment for violation of Constitution. Each House passes a resolution by special majority (2/3 of total membership). The other House investigates and if it resolves by 2/3 majority that charge is sustained, President is removed.

**Powers of the President:**

*Executive:* All executive actions expressed in President's name (Article 77). Appoints PM, Cabinet, Governors, CAG, CEC, CJI and SC/HC judges, Attorney General, Ambassadors.

*Legislative:* Summons, prorogues, dissolves Parliament. Addresses Parliament. Nominates 12 Rajya Sabha members. Gives assent to Bills — cannot withhold after reconsideration (Article 111). Promulgates Ordinances when Parliament not in session (Article 123) — Ordinance ceases within 6 weeks of Parliament's reassembly.

*Judicial:* Pardoning power under Article 72 — pardon, reprieve, respite, remission, commutation. Covers: death sentences, court martial offences, Union law offences. D.C. Wadhwa v. State of Bihar (1987): Re-promulgation of Ordinances without bringing them before legislature is fraud on the Constitution.

*Emergency:* Proclaims National Emergency (Art. 352), President's Rule (Art. 356), Financial Emergency (Art. 360).

**Discretionary Powers:** Limited real discretion: (a) Can refer Cabinet advice back once for reconsideration — must act on reconsidered advice (Article 74(1) proviso — 44th Amendment); (b) Appointment of PM when no clear majority; (c) Dissolution of Lok Sabha if PM's advice appears improper.

**Pardoning Power Comparison:**

President (Article 72): Death sentences ✓ \| Court martial ✓ \| Union law offences ✓ \| State law offences ✗

Governor (Article 161): Death sentences ✗ \| Court martial ✗ \| Union law offences ✗ \| State law offences ✓

### 5.2 Vice President (Articles 63–67)

Ex-officio Chairman of Rajya Sabha (Article 64). Elected by members of both Houses of Parliament — both elected AND nominated members (unlike President's electoral college). Term: 5 years. Removed by Rajya Sabha resolution (simple majority) agreed to by Lok Sabha (Article 67). Acts as President when vacancy arises (Article 65).

### 5.3 Prime Minister and Council of Ministers

**Article 75:** PM appointed by President. Other Ministers appointed by President on PM's advice. Council of Ministers collectively responsible to Lok Sabha (Article 75(3)). A Minister not a member of either House for 6 consecutive months ceases to be Minister.

**Cabinet vs. Council of Ministers:** Cabinet (Cabinet Ministers only) is the inner circle — the real executive. Council of Ministers is the wider body. Collective responsibility means the entire Cabinet must resign if it loses Lok Sabha confidence.

## Chapter 6: State Executive and the Governor

### 6.1 Constitutional Status

Governor is constitutional head of State executive. Article 153: Governor for each State. One person can be Governor of two or more States. Executive power vested in Governor (Article 154) but exercised on aid and advice of Council of Ministers headed by CM.

### 6.2 Appointment, Qualifications, Term

- Appointed by President under Article 155

- Qualifications (Article 157): Citizen of India; at least 35 years

- Conditions (Article 158): Not a member of any legislature; not hold office of profit

- Term (Article 156): 5 years OR during the pleasure of the President — whichever is earlier

### 6.3 Removal

Holds office during pleasure of President (Article 156(1)). B.P. Singhal v. Union of India (2010): Power to remove Governor is not absolute — cannot be exercised arbitrarily, capriciously, or for political reasons.

### 6.4 Powers of Governor

**Executive:** Appoints CM when no clear majority (genuine discretion). Appoints other ministers on CM's advice. Seeks information from CM on administration (Article 167).

**Legislative — Assent to Bills (Article 200):** Four options: (i) give assent; (ii) withhold assent; (iii) return non-Money Bill for reconsideration; (iv) reserve for President's consideration. CRITICAL: If Bill is re-passed by State Legislature, Governor MUST give assent — cannot withhold a second time.

**Presidential consideration (Article 201):** President has WIDER discretion than Governor — not bound to assent even after Bill is re-passed by State Legislature.

**Ordinance-making (Article 213):** When Legislature not in session AND immediate action necessary. Ceases to operate if: (1) not approved within 6 weeks of reassembly; (2) resolution disapproving it passed; (3) Governor revokes it.

**Pardoning Power (Article 161):** State law offences ONLY. CANNOT pardon death sentences (President's exclusive power under Article 72).

**Discretionary Powers (Article 163):**

1.  Appointment of CM when no clear majority

2.  Dismissal of Council of Ministers that has lost majority but refuses to resign

3.  Dissolution of State Assembly (limited by S.R. Bommai)

4.  Reservation of Bills for President's consideration

5.  Seeking information from CM (Article 167)

6.  Recommending President's Rule (Article 356)

**Immunity (Article 361):** Complete immunity from court proceedings during tenure. No criminal proceedings whatsoever. Civil proceedings for personal acts: 2 months' notice required.

### 6.5 Key Cases on Governor

- Shamsher Singh v. State of Punjab (1974): Governor is constitutional head; acts on CoM advice except in matters of express constitutional discretion

- S.R. Bommai v. Union of India (1994): Governor's report under Art. 356 is justiciable; floor of House is sole test of majority; Assembly in suspended animation until Parliament approves

- Rameshwar Prasad v. Union of India (2006): Dissolution of Bihar Assembly by Governor struck down as mala fide

- B.P. Singhal v. Union of India (2010): Removal of Governor cannot be arbitrary or for political reasons

- Nabam Rebia v. Deputy Speaker, Arunachal Pradesh (2016): Governor cannot advance session contrary to CoM advice

## Chapter 7: Parliament

### 7.1 Composition

**Rajya Sabha (Article 80):** Maximum 250 (238 elected + 12 nominated by President). Permanent body — cannot be dissolved. 1/3 members retire every 2 years. Chaired by Vice President.

**Lok Sabha (Article 81):** Maximum 543 elected members (after 104th Amendment, 2020 — abolished Anglo-Indian nominated seats). Direct election on basis of adult suffrage. Normal term: 5 years. Chaired by Speaker.

### 7.2 Money Bill (Article 110)

Deals ONLY with matters in Article 110(1)(a) to (g): taxes, borrowing, Consolidated Fund, appropriation, supplementary expenditure, audit of accounts. Must be introduced ONLY in Lok Sabha. Rajya Sabha can only make recommendations within 14 days. Speaker's certification is final (Article 110(3)).

### 7.3 Parliamentary Privileges (Article 105)

- Freedom of speech in Parliament — no liability for anything said or vote cast

- No arrest in civil cases during session + 40 days before/after

- Courts cannot question Parliamentary proceedings

- Publication of Parliamentary reports protected

Key evolution: P.V. Narasimha Rao v. State (1998) — bribery for vote protected by Art. 105. Sita Soren v. Union of India (2024): 7-judge bench OVERRULED P.V. Narasimha Rao — bribery NOT protected by Article 105(2).

### 7.4 Anti-Defection Law (Tenth Schedule: 52nd Amendment, 1985)

Disqualification if: (1) voluntarily gives up party membership; (2) votes/abstains contrary to party direction without permission and party does not condone within 15 days. 91st Amendment (2003): 'split' exception abolished; only merger of 2/3 members recognised. Kihoto Hollohan v. Zachillhu (1992): Tenth Schedule upheld; Speaker's decision subject to judicial review.

### 7.5 Constitutional Amendment (Article 368)

1.  Simple majority: Creation of new States, changing State names

2.  Special majority (2/3 of present and voting + majority of total): Most provisions

3.  Special majority + Ratification by half of State Legislatures: Federal provisions (Arts. 54, 55, 73, 162, Seventh Schedule, etc.)

## Chapter 8: Union–State Legislative Relations

### 8.1 Three Lists (Seventh Schedule)

**Union List (List I):** Parliament only — Defence, Foreign Affairs, Currency, Banking, Railways, etc. Residuary powers also vest in Parliament (Article 248).

**State List (List II):** State Legislature only — Public Order, Police, Agriculture, Land, Local Government, Public Health, etc.

**Concurrent List (List III):** Both Parliament and State — Education, Criminal Law, Marriage, Contracts, Labour. In repugnancy, Union law prevails (Article 254). Exception: State law with Presidential assent prevails in that State — but Parliament can override.

### 8.2 Key Doctrines

**Pith and Substance:** Courts look at the true nature and character of legislation. If it falls substantially within the legislature's competence, it is valid even if it incidentally trenches on another legislature's field. Case: State of Bombay v. F.N. Balsara (1951).

**Colorable Legislation:** The legislature cannot do indirectly what it cannot do directly. If the substance encroaches on another legislature's domain, it is colorable. Case: K.C. Gajapati Narayan Deo v. State of Orissa (1953).

**Occupied Field:** When Parliament intends to legislate exhaustively on a Concurrent List subject, States cannot legislate on the same subject.

**Article 254 — Repugnancy:** Union law on Concurrent List prevails over State law. Exception: State law with Presidential assent prevails in that State — but Parliament can supersede by subsequent legislation.

### 8.3 When Parliament Legislates on State List

- Article 249: Rajya Sabha resolution by 2/3 majority — national interest

- Article 250: During National Emergency

- Article 252: Parliament legislates for two or more States by consent

- Article 253: To implement international treaties — even State List subjects

- Article 356: During President's Rule — Parliament legislates for the State

## Chapter 9: Judiciary

### 9.1 Supreme Court (Articles 124–147)

**Composition:** CJI + maximum 33 other judges (Parliament can increase). Retirement: 65 years. Salaries charged to Consolidated Fund of India — not subject to vote.

**Appointment (Collegium System):** Three Judges Cases established the collegium (CJI + 4 senior-most SC judges). The NJAC (99th Amendment, 2014) was struck down in 2015 as violating judicial independence — a basic structure element.

**Removal (Article 124(4)):** By address of each House by special majority on grounds of proved misbehaviour or incapacity.

**Jurisdiction:**

- Original (Article 131): Inter-State disputes — exclusive

- Appellate (Articles 132–134): Constitutional, civil, criminal matters

- SLP (Article 136): Discretionary — widest appellate power; from any court or tribunal

- Writ (Article 32): For enforcement of Fundamental Rights

- Advisory (Article 143): President's reference — opinion advisory, not binding

- Court of Record (Article 129): Binding precedents; contempt power

**Article 141:** Law declared by SC is binding on ALL courts in India. SC not bound by its own decisions.

**Article 142:** SC can make any order to do complete justice — extraordinary power used for structural injunctions and unique remedies.

### 9.2 High Courts (Articles 214–231)

- Composition: Chief Justice + other judges. Retirement: 62 years.

- Appointment (Article 217): By President in consultation with CJI, Governor, Chief Justice of HC.

- Writ jurisdiction (Article 226): WIDER than Article 32 — any legal right, not just FRs

- Superintendence (Article 227): Over all subordinate courts and tribunals

- Transfer of constitutional questions (Article 228): Can withdraw cases involving constitutional questions

### 9.3 Collegium System: Three Judges Cases

1.  First Judges Case (S.P. Gupta, 1981): Executive primacy in appointments

2.  Second Judges Case (1993): Collegium — CJI's opinion gets primacy; CJI + 2 senior judges

3.  Third Judges Case (1998): Collegium expanded — CJI + 4 senior-most SC judges

4.  NJAC Case (2015): 99th Amendment struck down — judicial independence is basic structure

### 9.4 PIL and Epistolary Jurisdiction

Evolved by SC (Justice P.N. Bhagwati and Justice V.R. Krishna Iyer) in late 1970s–1980s. Liberalised locus standi — any public-spirited person can approach courts on behalf of those unable to access justice. Letters to court treated as writ petitions. Key cases: Hussainara Khatoon (1979), Bandhua Mukti Morcha (1984), M.C. Mehta (1987), Vishaka (1997), Common Cause (2018).

## Chapter 10: Emergency Provisions

### 10.1 National Emergency (Article 352)

**Grounds:** War, external aggression, or armed rebellion. 'Armed rebellion' replaced 'internal disturbance' by 44th Amendment, 1978 — to prevent misuse like the 1975 Emergency.

**Proclamation:** Written advice of Cabinet (not PM alone — 44th Amendment). Approved by Parliament by special majority within one month. Renewed every 6 months by special majority. NO maximum period — continues indefinitely while renewed.

**Revocation:** President can revoke on own. Lok Sabha alone can pass simple majority resolution to disapprove (Article 352(8)) — the ONLY instance where Lok Sabha can constitutionally act alone.

**Effects during National Emergency:**

- Parliament legislates on State List (Article 250)

- Revenue distribution modified (Article 354)

- Article 19 freedoms automatically suspended (Article 358) — for laws made DURING Emergency only (not pre-existing laws)

- Other FRs can be suspended by Presidential Order (Article 359) — BUT NOT Articles 20 and 21

- Fundamental duty to defend country becomes enforceable

**Historical use:** 1962 (Chinese aggression), 1965 (Indo-Pak war), 1971 (Bangladesh war), 1975–1977 (internal disturbance — the most controversial, last Emergency)

### 10.2 Article 358 vs. Article 359: Critical Distinction

Article 358: Suspends Article 19 rights \| AUTOMATICALLY \| Only war/external aggression \| Only for laws made during Emergency \| Does not affect pre-existing laws

Article 359: Suspends other specified FRs \| Requires Presidential Order \| All types of National Emergency \| Cannot suspend Articles 20 and 21 \| Covers laws and executive action during Emergency

### 10.3 President's Rule: State Emergency (Article 356)

**Grounds:** Constitutional machinery of State has failed. Also: failure to comply with Union directions (Article 365).

**Duration:** 2 months (initial, pending Parliament approval) → 6 months (after approval) → renewed every 6 months → maximum 3 years. Extension beyond 1 year requires: (a) National Emergency in operation; OR (b) Election Commission certifies elections cannot be held.

**Effects:** President assumes State executive functions; Parliament legislates on State List; Governor becomes Centre's agent; Assembly is dissolved or kept in suspended animation.

**S.R. Bommai v. Union of India (1994) — 9-judge bench — KEY PRINCIPLES:**

1.  Art. 356 proclamation is subject to judicial review

2.  Until Parliament approves, Assembly is in 'suspended animation' — NOT dissolved

3.  Secularism is a basic feature — anti-secular State government can be dismissed

4.  Floor of the House is the SOLE test of majority — Governor cannot assess majority elsewhere

5.  Courts can examine whether material before President was relevant and germane

### 10.4 Financial Emergency (Article 360)

**Grounds:** Financial stability or credit of India or any part is threatened.

**Key features:**

- Has NEVER been proclaimed in India's history

- No maximum period — continues until revoked by President

- President can direct reduction of salaries of ALL government servants including SC and HC judges (Article 360(4)(b)) — a unique constitutional provision

- Centre can give directions to States on financial matters during Financial Emergency

## Chapter 11: Temporary, Transitional and Special Provisions (Part XXI)

### 11.1 Overview

Part XXI (Articles 369–392) contains provisions that were transitional at the time of adoption, and special provisions for specific States reflecting their unique historical, cultural, or political circumstances.

### 11.2 Article 370: Special Status of J&K

Granted J&K special autonomous status — most provisions of the Constitution applied to J&K only with Presidential concurrence/consent. Abrogated in August 2019 by Presidential Order CO 272. J&K reorganised into two Union Territories: J&K (with Legislature) and Ladakh (without Legislature). SC upheld the abrogation in Dr. Farooq Abdullah v. Union of India (2023) by 5-judge bench.

### 11.3 Article 371: Special Provisions for States

- Article 371: Maharashtra and Gujarat — Governor has special responsibility for certain regions

- Article 371-A: Nagaland — customary law and practices protected; Acts of Parliament on Naga customary law require State Legislature's request

- Article 371-B: Assam — committee of tribal area members in Legislature

- Article 371-C: Manipur — similar committee

- Article 371-D and E: Andhra Pradesh/Telangana — equitable opportunities in public services

- Article 371-F: Sikkim — protection of rights and existing laws

- Article 371-G: Mizoram — similar to Nagaland

- Article 371-H: Arunachal Pradesh — Governor's special responsibility for law and order

- Article 371-I: Goa — minimum size of Legislative Assembly

- Article 371-J: Hyderabad-Karnataka region — reservation of seats

### 11.4 Union Territories

Administered by President through Administrator/Lieutenant Governor (Articles 239–241). Article 239-A: Parliament can create a legislature for certain UTs. Delhi's unique status: Government of NCT of Delhi Act, 1991 created Delhi Legislative Assembly. Delhi LG vs. Delhi Government dispute repeatedly litigated — Government of NCT of Delhi v. Union of India (2018): Constitutional bench held LG is bound by Cabinet's aid and advice on most matters; Government of NCT of Delhi v. Union of India (2023): SC held Delhi government has control over services (later overridden by constitutional amendment).

## Chapter 12: Election Commission of India

### 12.1 Constitutional Basis

Article 324: Superintendence, direction, and control of preparation of electoral rolls and conduct of all elections to Parliament and State Legislatures vested in Election Commission of India.

### 12.2 Composition and Appointment

CEC + such number of other ECs as President may fix (Article 324(2)). Appointed by President. The Chief Election Commissioner Act, 2023 changed the appointment process — selection committee (PM + Leader of Opposition + Cabinet Minister). This change (removing CJI from selection) is under judicial challenge.

### 12.3 Tenure and Removal

Term: 6 years or 65 years — whichever is earlier. The CEC can be removed ONLY in the same manner as a SC judge — by impeachment process (Article 324(5)). Other ECs can be removed by President on recommendation of CEC. T.N. Seshan v. Union of India (1995): CEC and other ECs have equal powers; CEC has no veto.

### 12.4 Powers of Election Commission

- Preparation and revision of electoral rolls

- Recognition of political parties; allotment of election symbols

- Model Code of Conduct enforcement (not statutory — based on Art. 324 plenary power)

- Setting election schedules; monitoring of elections

- Disqualification of candidates for corrupt practices (on reference from President/Governor)

- Advice on disqualification of MPs/MLAs under Articles 103 and 192

### 12.5 Key Cases

- T.N. Seshan v. Union of India (1995): Equal powers of CEC and ECs; CEC has no veto

- Mohinder Singh Gill v. CEC (1978): Election Commission's superintendence powers are plenary

- Association for Democratic Reforms v. Union of India (2002): Candidates must disclose criminal antecedents, assets, educational qualifications — right to information is part of Art. 19(1)(a)

- Lily Thomas v. Union of India (2013): Convicted MPs/MLAs disqualified from date of conviction — Section 8(4) RPA struck down

## Chapter 13: Administrative Law: Nature and Scope

### 13.1 What Is Administrative Law?

Administrative law is that branch of public law which deals with the organisation, powers, and procedures of administrative authorities, and the legal remedies available to persons adversely affected by public administration. It governs the relationship between individuals and the State in the exercise of governmental power.

**Key definitions:**

- H.W.R. Wade: "The law relating to the control of governmental power"

- Garner: "That branch of public law concerned with the composition, powers and duties of administrative authorities"

- Ivor Jennings: "The law relating to the administration — determines the organisation, powers and duties of administrative authorities"

- K.C. Davis (USA): "Deals with powers and procedures of administrative agencies, including especially judicial review of administrative action"

### 13.2 Reasons for Growth of Administrative Law

The growth of administrative law is directly linked to the transition from a laissez-faire (minimal government) state to a welfare state. Key reasons:

- Welfare State concept: State regulates health, education, housing, environment, labour

- Complexity of modern society: Parliament cannot foresee every situation; delegated legislation fills the gap

- Need for technical expertise: Regulatory bodies (SEBI, TRAI, RBI) require specialist knowledge courts lack

- Speed and flexibility: Administrative adjudication is faster than courts

- Parliamentary time constraints: Parliament cannot deal with all regulatory minutiae

### 13.3 Dicey's Critique and Its Rejection

A.V. Dicey opposed administrative law because he believed it violated the Rule of Law — it created a separate system of law for government officials. Dicey saw the French Droit Administratif system (Conseil d'État) as contrary to English principles of equality before law.

Dicey's three meanings of Rule of Law:

1.  Supremacy of law — no man punished except for breach of established ordinary law

2.  Equality before the law — every man, including officials, subject to ordinary courts

3.  Constitution as consequence of rights declared by ordinary courts

Dicey was later criticised by Ivor Jennings, W.A. Robson, and H.W.R. Wade who showed that French Droit Administratif actually provides MORE protection to citizens against the State than Dicey acknowledged, and that the English common law too has developed substantial administrative law.

### 13.4 Rule of Law in India

Rule of Law is a basic feature of the Indian Constitution (Kesavananda Bharati, 1973; Indira Nehru Gandhi v. Raj Narain, 1975). Constitutional pillars of Rule of Law in India:

- Article 13: No law can violate Fundamental Rights

- Article 14: Equal protection of laws

- Article 21: Procedure must be just, fair, and reasonable (Maneka Gandhi, 1978)

- Articles 32 and 226: Judicial review enforces Rule of Law

- Independence of judiciary (basic structure)

### 13.5 Separation of Powers

India does not have strict Montesquieu-style separation but a functional separation with checks and balances. Ram Jawaya Kapur v. State of Punjab (1955): Indian Constitution does not adopt strict separation of powers but contemplates a system of checks and balances. The judiciary is the watchdog — reviews legislative and executive action.

### 13.6 Delegated Legislation

Legislation made by executive or administrative bodies under authority delegated by Parliament. Justified by: technical nature of subjects, parliamentary time constraints, need for flexibility, local expertise.

**Types:** Rules, Regulations, Bye-laws, Notifications, Schemes.

**Controls:**

1.  Parliamentary control: Laying procedure (affirmative or negative resolution); Committee on Subordinate Legislation in both Houses

2.  Judicial control: Courts strike down if ultra vires, violates FRs, procedurally defective, or unreasonable

3.  Procedural controls: Mandatory consultation, public notice, publication in Official Gazette

**Limits on Delegation — Delhi Laws Act Case (1951):** Parliament cannot delegate the 'essential legislative function' — must lay down policy. Details of implementation only can be delegated. Excessive delegation (abdication of legislative function) is unconstitutional. Sub-delegation requires express or implied permission (delegatus non potest delegare).

**D.C. Wadhwa v. State of Bihar (1987):** Repeated repromulgation of Ordinances without bringing them before the Legislature is a fraud on the Constitution — misuse of delegated legislative power.

### 13.7 Ombudsman and Lokpal

Ombudsman concept originated in Sweden (1809) — an independent official appointed by Parliament to investigate complaints against government officials. India's equivalent: Lokpal (Union level) and Lokayukta (State level).

Lokpal and Lokayuktas Act, 2013:

- Jurisdiction: PM (with restrictions), Ministers, MPs, Group A/B/C/D government servants, and certain NGOs

- Chairperson: Former CJI or SC judge or eminent person with 25+ years' experience

- First Lokpal: Justice Pinaki Chandra Ghose (appointed 2019)

- Complaints against PM excluded if related to external affairs, security, public order

## Chapter 14: Principles of Natural Justice

### 14.1 Foundations

Natural justice consists of procedural requirements that must be observed by courts, tribunals, and administrative bodies before making decisions that adversely affect persons. Rooted in natural law theory — principles so fundamental to justice that they are universally self-evident. Not merely technical procedural rules — a fundamental guarantee of fairness rooted in human dignity.

### 14.2 The Two Principal Rules

**Rule 1 — Nemo debet esse judex in propria sua causa (Rule Against Bias):**

No person shall be a judge in his own cause. The decision-maker must not have any interest — personal, financial, or otherwise — in the outcome. Ensures both actual fairness AND appearance of fairness.

Types of Bias:

1.  Pecuniary bias: ANY financial interest in the outcome — AUTOMATIC disqualification — no need to prove actual influence. Dimes v. Grand Junction Canal (1852): Lord Chancellor held shares; decree set aside.

2.  Personal bias: Friendship, hostility, or other personal relationship with a party.

3.  Subject matter bias: Pre-judgment — the adjudicator has already formed a view before hearing.

4.  Departmental/Institutional bias: The entire department has a pre-determined stance. Gullapalli Nageswara Rao v. A.P. SRTC (1959): Investigator cannot be adjudicator — officer who heard objections cannot sanction the same scheme.

5.  Policy bias: Adjudicator under pressure to follow a government policy predetermining the outcome.

Test for bias in India (A.K. Kraipak v. Union of India, 1970): 'Real danger' or 'Reasonable suspicion' of bias. R v. Sussex Justices (1924): 'Justice must not only be done but must also be seen to be done.'

**Rule 2 — Audi alteram partem (Right to Fair Hearing):**

Hear the other side before deciding against a person. No one should be condemned unheard. Core components:

- Notice: Adequate notice of proceedings and the charge/allegation

- Opportunity to be heard: In writing or orally (oral hearing not always required)

- Right to know the case against: Access to material relied upon by decision-maker

- Opportunity to present evidence and cross-examine: NOT always required — depends on context (Russell v. Duke of Norfolk, 1949)

- Right to legal representation: NOT automatic — depends on statutory provision and nature of proceedings

- Speaking/Reasoned order: Duty to give reasons (Bhagat Raja v. Union of India, 1967)

Key case history:

- Cooper v. Wandsworth Board of Works (1863): House demolished without notice — violated NJ even though no statute required a hearing

- Ridge v. Baldwin (1964) \[HL\]: LANDMARK — NJ applies to decisions affecting rights, not just strictly judicial functions; revived the scope of NJ

- A.K. Kraipak v. Union of India (1970): Boundary between administrative and quasi-judicial is not rigid — NJ applies to administrative functions affecting rights

- Maneka Gandhi v. Union of India (1978): Impounding passport without prior hearing — procedure must be fair; post-decisional hearing is generally inadequate

- Swadeshi Cotton Mills v. Union of India (1981): NJ content is flexible — depends on statutory framework, nature of power, consequences, public interest

**Rule 3 — Speaking Orders (Duty to Give Reasons):** The decision-maker must record reasons so the aggrieved party can challenge the decision effectively. Applies to quasi-judicial orders. Bhagat Raja v. Union of India (1967): This requirement is an element of natural justice for quasi-judicial orders.

### 14.3 When Natural Justice Can Be Excluded

1.  Statutory exclusion — express statutory provision (but cannot violate Articles 14/21 — Maneka Gandhi principle)

2.  Emergency — urgency makes prior hearing impossible (must give post-decisional remedy)

3.  Impracticability — mass action where individual hearing is impractical

4.  Confidentiality/National security — disclosure would prejudice State security

5.  Purely administrative action — policy-making with no individual rights affected

6.  Waiver — the affected party can voluntarily and knowingly waive the right

### 14.4 Legitimate Expectation

A person has a 'legitimate expectation' when a public authority has, by its conduct, promise, or consistent practice, led a person to reasonably expect that a certain procedure will be followed or a benefit will not be withdrawn without an opportunity to be heard.

- Council of Civil Service Unions v. Minister for Civil Service (GCHQ Case, 1985) \[HL\]: PM banned union membership at GCHQ without consulting unions — breach of legitimate expectation of consultation

- Navjyoti Coop. Group Housing Society v. Union of India (1992): First clear Indian recognition — change of policy without notice to affected parties was invalid

- Food Corporation of India v. Kamdhenu Cattle Feed Industries (1993): LE gives right to be heard before adverse action, not necessarily the substantive benefit expected

- Union of India v. Hindustan Development Corporation (1993): LE gives only procedural right — NOT a substantive right to the expected benefit

**Promissory Estoppel vs. Legitimate Expectation:** Promissory Estoppel (Motilal Padampat Sugar Mills v. State of UP, 1979): Government cannot retract a clear representation on which a party has acted to its detriment — equitable doctrine. Legitimate Expectation is broader — based on reasonable expectation from conduct/practice, not necessarily a specific promise — public law doctrine.

## Chapter 15: Judicial Review of Administrative Action: Grounds

### 15.1 What Is Judicial Review?

Judicial review is the power of the judiciary to examine the lawfulness of administrative action. In India, it flows from Articles 32 and 226 (writ jurisdiction) and is a basic feature of the Constitution. It is a constitutional power, not merely a statutory one (L. Chandra Kumar v. Union of India, 1997).

### 15.2 Lord Diplock's Three Grounds: GCHQ Case (1985)

In Council of Civil Service Unions v. Minister for Civil Service (GCHQ Case, 1985), Lord Diplock classified grounds of judicial review into three authoritative categories:

**1. Illegality:** The decision-maker must understand the law regulating the decision-making power and give effect to it. Sub-categories:

- No jurisdiction (acting without authority)

- Excess of jurisdiction

- Failure to exercise jurisdiction

- Error of law (post-Anisminic, 1969 — ANY error of law goes to jurisdiction)

- Mala fide/Improper purpose — exercising power for an unauthorised purpose

- Irrelevant considerations — taking into account matters not authorised by statute

- Failure to consider relevant matters

**2. Irrationality (Wednesbury Unreasonableness):** From Associated Provincial Picture Houses Ltd. v. Wednesbury Corporation \[1948\]. Lord Greene MR: A decision so unreasonable that no reasonable authority could ever have come to it. HIGH threshold — courts do not substitute their own judgment for that of the authority. The court asks: was the decision within the range of reasonable responses?

**3. Procedural Impropriety:** Failure to observe procedural rules prescribed by statute (procedural ultra vires) OR failure to observe rules of natural justice — breach of audi alteram partem and rule against bias.

### 15.3 The Indian Grounds of Judicial Review

Indian courts have developed a comprehensive jurisprudence:

1.  Lack of jurisdiction (no jurisdiction): Administrative authority has no power in the matter at all

2.  Excess of jurisdiction: Authority has power but has exceeded it

3.  Abuse of discretion:

    - Exercise of discretion for improper purpose (colourable exercise)

    - Taking irrelevant considerations into account

    - Ignoring relevant considerations

    - Acting mala fide

    - Arbitrary or unreasonable decision

4.  Error of law: Following Anisminic (1969) — any error of law, not just 'jurisdictional' error in the narrow sense

5.  Error of fact going to jurisdiction

6.  Violation of natural justice: Breach of audi alteram partem or rule against bias

7.  Legitimate expectation: Failure to honour a legitimate expectation

8.  Proportionality: Means used must be proportionate to ends sought

### 15.4 Proportionality

Stricter standard than Wednesbury unreasonableness. Structured inquiry:

1.  Is the objective legitimate?

2.  Are the means suitable for achieving the objective?

3.  Are the means necessary — could less restrictive means achieve the same goal?

4.  Is there a fair balance between individual rights and public interest?

Om Kumar v. Union of India (2001): SC adopted proportionality as a ground of review for punishments imposed on government servants. Modern Dental College v. State of MP (2016) and K.S. Puttaswamy (2017): Proportionality test applied to restrictions on Fundamental Rights.

### 15.5 The Five Writs in Detail

**Habeas Corpus ('you have the body'):** Compels production of detained person before court to examine legality of detention. Most important writ for personal liberty. Wide scope — lies against State AND private individuals. Available even during Emergency (subject to Art. 359 orders — but Arts. 20/21 cannot be suspended).

**Mandamus ('we command'):** Issued to public authority commanding performance of a public duty which has been refused or neglected. Enforces statutory duties, constitutional duties. Does NOT lie against: private contractual obligations; purely discretionary decisions (only compels exercise of discretion, not a particular outcome); judicial authority in purely judicial matters; private individuals.

**Certiorari ('to be certified'):** Issued to inferior tribunal to bring record before superior court for examination and quash if defective. CURATIVE — issued AFTER decision. Grounds: excess of jurisdiction, lack of jurisdiction, violation of NJ, error apparent on the face of record (Syed Yakoob v. K.S. Radhakrishnan, 1964).

**Prohibition:** Issued to inferior tribunal to STOP it proceeding in excess of jurisdiction. PREVENTIVE — issued BEFORE decision is made. If decision already made, certiorari is the appropriate remedy.

**Quo Warranto ('by what authority'):** Issued against a person who has usurped or holds a public office without legal authority. Only lies for public offices of a substantive character created by statute or Constitution. A discretionary writ — not a writ of right (University of Mysore v. C.D. Govinda Rao, 1964).

### 15.6 Administrative Tribunals

Article 323-A (42nd Amendment, 1976) provides for Administrative Tribunals for service disputes. Administrative Tribunals Act, 1985 established the Central Administrative Tribunal (CAT). Article 323-B: Tribunals for other matters (taxation, elections, industrial disputes).

L. Chandra Kumar v. Union of India (1997): Power of High Courts under Articles 226 and 227 is part of the basic structure of the Constitution and CANNOT be excluded. CAT decisions are subject to HC Division Bench review. SC can entertain SLPs from HC decisions on CAT matters.

## Chapter 20: Key Doctrines: Constitutional and Administrative Law

This section compiles ALL major doctrines of Constitutional and Administrative Law in a structured form for quick recall.

### 20.1 Constitutional Doctrines

**Basic Structure Doctrine** Origin: Kesavananda Bharati v. State of Kerala (1973). Parliament can amend the Constitution under Art. 368 but cannot destroy its basic structure. Recognised elements: supremacy of Constitution, republican and democratic form, secular character, separation of powers, federal character, judicial review, free and fair elections, dignity of individual, unity and integrity of nation, parliamentary system, rule of law, equality, social justice, and independence of judiciary.

**Doctrine of Eclipse** Origin: Bhikhaji Narain Dhakras v. State of MP (1955). Pre-constitutional laws inconsistent with Fundamental Rights are not void but dormant — eclipsed. They automatically revive if the constitutional bar (FR) is amended to remove the inconsistency. Does NOT apply to post-constitutional laws (those are void ab initio under Art. 13(2)).

**Doctrine of Severability** Origin: R.M.D. Chamarbaugwala v. Union of India (1957). Only the unconstitutional part of a law is struck down; the rest remains valid if separable and independently workable. Test: Would Parliament have enacted the law even without the invalid portion?

**Doctrine of Colourable Legislation** Origin: K.C. Gajapati Narayan Deo v. State of Orissa (1953). Even where a legislature appears to act within its powers, if in substance it is encroaching upon another legislature's domain, the legislation is 'coloured' or 'disguised' — and is invalid. The label does not matter; the substance does.

**Pith and Substance Doctrine** Origin: Profulla Kumar Mukherjee v. Bank of Khulna (1947, PC); State of Bombay v. F.N. Balsara (1951). If a law incidentally trenches on another legislature's field, courts look at its true nature and character. If it falls substantially within the legislature's competence, it is valid despite incidental encroachment.

**Doctrine of Occupied Field** When Parliament legislates exhaustively on a Concurrent List subject, it occupies the entire field — States cannot legislate on the same subject. Related to Art. 254 repugnancy.

**Doctrine of Pleasure (Article 310)** Government servants hold office during the pleasure of the President/Governor. Subject to Art. 311 procedural safeguards. Does NOT apply to SC/HC judges (who hold during good behaviour) or Constitutional authorities with specific security of tenure.

**Doctrine of Implied Powers** Powers not expressly conferred but necessarily implied from express provisions. Applied to fill lacunae in the Constitution.

**Doctrine of Prospective Overruling** Origin in India: Golak Nath v. State of Punjab (1967) — Subba Rao CJ. When the SC overrules a previous decision, it can declare that the overruling shall operate only prospectively — not affecting past transactions. Prevents injustice from retroactive application of a new legal rule.

**Doctrine of Double Jeopardy (Article 20(2))** No person shall be prosecuted and punished for the same offence more than once. Narrower than the common law autrefois acquit/convict — applies only to prosecution AND punishment, not to mere prosecution. Disciplinary proceedings + criminal proceedings on the same facts do NOT violate Art. 20(2).

**Doctrine of Sovereign Immunity** The State is not liable for acts done in the exercise of sovereign functions. In P. & O. Steam Navigation Co. v. Secretary of State for India (1861), a distinction was drawn between sovereign and non-sovereign functions. Post-Constitution, the SC in Kasturi Lal Ralia Ram Jain v. State of UP (1965) upheld the distinction. Later cases (N. Nagendra Rao & Co. v. State of AP, 1994) significantly eroded it — State cannot claim immunity for tortious acts of its servants.

**Doctrine of Res Judicata in Constitutional Matters** In Devilal Modi v. STO (1964), the SC held that res judicata applies to writ petitions also. A petition dismissed on merits creates res judicata — but a petition dismissed on grounds of alternative remedy does not bar a fresh petition.

### 20.2 Administrative Law Doctrines

**Doctrine of Ultra Vires** An act beyond the powers conferred by the enabling statute is ultra vires and void. Two types: Substantive ultra vires (acting beyond scope) and Procedural ultra vires (not following prescribed procedure). In delegated legislation: mandatory procedural requirements must be followed; directory requirements — their breach may not invalidate the legislation.

**Doctrine of Legitimate Expectation** A person has a legitimate expectation if a public authority has, by conduct, promise, or consistent practice, led them to reasonably expect a certain procedure will be followed or benefit will not be withdrawn without a hearing. Gives procedural right only — not substantive right to the expected benefit (Union of India v. Hindustan Development Corp., 1993). The authority can always override LE for larger public interest, provided it gives an opportunity to be heard.

**Doctrine of Proportionality** The means used by the State to achieve a legitimate aim must be proportionate — not more restrictive of rights than necessary. Four-stage test: (1) legitimate aim; (2) rational connection; (3) necessity (minimum impairment); (4) proportionality stricto sensu (fair balance). Adopted in Om Kumar (2001) for service matters; expanded to FR restrictions in Modern Dental College (2016) and Puttaswamy (2017).

**Doctrine of Wednesbury Unreasonableness** From Associated Provincial Picture Houses v. Wednesbury Corporation (1948). A decision so unreasonable that no reasonable authority could ever have come to it. High threshold — courts do not evaluate the merits of the decision; only ask whether it was within the range of reasonable responses. Slowly being replaced in rights-based cases by proportionality.

**Doctrine of Fairness (Duty to Act Fairly)** Wider than natural justice. Even in cases where formal natural justice rules may not apply, a public authority must act fairly. Maneka Gandhi (1978) expanded the duty of fairness to all State action affecting persons.

**Doctrine of Promissory Estoppel** The government is estopped from going back on a promise or representation on which a citizen has acted to his detriment. Confirmed in Motilal Padampat Sugar Mills (1979). Exceptions: (1) Public interest requires the change; (2) The original promise was made without authority. Differs from legitimate expectation (LE is wider — no specific promise needed).

**Delegatus Non Potest Delegare** A delegate cannot further delegate the power delegated to it unless expressly or impliedly permitted by the parent Act. Prevents uncontrolled sub-delegation of legislative power.

**Doctrine of Excessive Delegation** Parliament cannot abdicate its essential legislative function. It must lay down the policy and the standards — delegation of powers to fill in details is permissible, but delegation of the entire legislative field is unconstitutional. In re Delhi Laws Act (1951): SC held that the essential legislative functions cannot be delegated.

| Proclaimed in India          | 1962, 1965, 1971, 1975 (4 times)                                         | Many times (over 125 times since 1950)         | Never                                 |
| Revocable by Lok Sabha alone | YES — simple majority (Art. 352(8))                                      | No — requires both Houses                      | No                                    |

### 21.4 Article 32 vs. Article 226: Writ Jurisdiction

| Feature                  | Article 32 (Supreme Court)                    | Article 226 (High Court)                                  |
|--------------------------|-----------------------------------------------|-----------------------------------------------------------|
| Purpose                  | Only for enforcement of Fundamental Rights    | For enforcement of any legal right                        |
| Who can file             | Any person whose FR is violated               | Any person whose legal right is violated                  |
| Territorial jurisdiction | Throughout India                              | Within HC's territorial jurisdiction                      |
| Can be suspended         | Art. 359 — during Emergency (not Arts. 20/21) | Cannot be suspended even during Emergency                 |
| Is it itself a FR        | YES — Art. 32(1) is a FR                      | NO — Art. 226 is a constitutional power                   |
| Wider scope              | No — narrower (only FRs)                      | YES — wider (any legal right)                             |
| Discretion               | SC has discretion but usually doesn't refuse  | HC has wider discretion; can refuse if alternative remedy |

### 21.5 Certiorari vs. Prohibition: Comparison

| Feature      | Certiorari                                                    | Prohibition                                           |
|--------------|---------------------------------------------------------------|-------------------------------------------------------|
| Nature       | Curative                                                      | Preventive                                            |
| When issued  | After a decision is made                                      | Before a decision is made                             |
| Purpose      | To bring up record and quash the decision                     | To stop the inferior tribunal from proceeding further |
| Against whom | Inferior court/tribunal/quasi-judicial body                   | Inferior court/tribunal/quasi-judicial body           |
| Grounds      | Excess of jurisdiction, NJ violation, error on face of record | Excess of jurisdiction, lack of jurisdiction          |

## Chapter 23: Important Statutory Provisions (Administrative Law)

### 23.1 Administrative Tribunals Act, 1985

- Establishes Central Administrative Tribunal (CAT) and State Administrative Tribunals

- CAT has jurisdiction over recruitment and service conditions of Central government employees

- CAT decisions are subject to HC writ jurisdiction (L. Chandra Kumar, 1997)

- Chairperson must be a retired SC judge or HC Chief Justice

### 23.2 Right to Information Act, 2005

- Implements Art. 19(1)(a) right to know (S.P. Gupta v. Union of India, 1982)

- Every citizen can request information from public authorities

- Central Information Commission (CIC) and State Information Commissions (SICs) adjudicate

- Exemptions: sovereignty, security, cabinet papers, fiduciary relationships, personal information

- RTI Amendment Act, 2019 — changed tenure and service conditions of CIC and SIC members (controversial)

### 23.3 Lokpal and Lokayuktas Act, 2013

- Establishes Lokpal at Union level

- Jurisdiction: PM (restricted), Ministers, MPs, Group A/B/C/D officials, NGOs receiving government funds

- Selection: A 5-member committee (PM, LS Speaker, LoP in LS, CJI or SC judge nominated by CJI, Eminent Jurist)

- The CEC Act 2023 later changed ECI appointment but Lokpal committee structure remains separate

### 23.4 Prevention of Corruption Act, 1988 (Amended 2018)

- Key amendment: Bribe-giver is now equally culpable (not just bribe-taker)

- Prior sanction for prosecution of public servants required even after retirement

- Reflects Art. 51-A (j) — duty to strive towards excellence without corruption

### 23.5 Protection of Human Rights Act, 1993

- Establishes National Human Rights Commission (NHRC) and State Human Rights Commissions (SHRCs)

- Chairperson: Retired CJI (NHRC); retired HC Chief Justice (SHRC)

- Powers similar to civil court for inquiry; can recommend compensation

- Cannot inquire into matters pending before NHRC or HC

## Chapter 25: Memory Mnemonics: Master Quick Revision

### 25.1 Emergency Articles

- **3-5-6-0**: Article **352** (National) → **353** (Effect) → **354** (Revenue) → **355** (Duty) → **356** (State) → **357** (Legislative) → **358** (Art.19 Auto-suspend) → **359** (Presidential Order) → **360** (Financial) → **365** (Non-compliance)

- **358 = AUTO** (automatic suspension of Art. 19, only war/external aggression)

- **359 = ORDER** (Presidential Order needed; NOT Arts. 20/21)

### 25.2 Grounds for Emergency Revocation

- National Emergency (352): President on own + **Lok Sabha simple majority alone** (Art. 352(8))

- President's Rule (356): President on own + **both Houses** by simple majority

### 25.3 Five Writs: HMCPQ

- **H**abeas Corpus — liberty — most available

- **M**andamus — command — public duty

- **C**ertiorari — quash — after decision (Curative)

- **P**rohibition — prevent — before decision (Preventive)

- **Q**uo Warranto — office holder — most restricted

### 25.4 Basic Structure Elements: SRFDES (Selective)

- **S**upremacy of Constitution

- **R**epublican and Democratic form

- **F**ederal character

- **D**emocracy — free and fair elections

- **E**quality (substantive)

- **S**ecularism

- - Judicial review, Rule of Law, Parliamentary system, Dignity of individual, Unity and Integrity

### 25.5 Governor's Discretionary Powers: CARD

- **C**hief Minister appointment (no clear majority)

- **A**ssembly dissolution (limited by Bommai)

- **R**eservation of Bills for President

- **D**ismissal of CoM that has lost majority

### 25.6 Three Lists: UCS

- **U**nion List — Parliament only — **U**nique to Centre

- **C**oncurrent List — Both — **C**ommon ground

- **S**tate List — States only — **S**tate's own domain

- Residuary → Parliament (Art. 248)

### 25.7 44th Amendment, 1978: Key Changes (AACAR)

- **A**rmed rebellion replaced internal disturbance (Art. 352)

- **A**dvice of Cabinet in writing required for Emergency

- **C**annot suspend Arts. 20 and 21 (made non-suspendable)

- **A**bsolished right to property as FR (Art. 300-A)

- **R**aised special majority requirement for Emergency approval

### 25.8 Articles 20, 21: Non-Suspendable: Trick

- **20 = EDS** — Ex post facto, Double jeopardy, Self-incrimination

- **21 = LIFE** — Life includes all expanded rights (liberty, education, privacy, environment...)

- Both 20 and 21 together = **NEVER suspended** (44th Amendment, 1978)
