# Intellectual Property Rights and Information Technology Law: Detailed Notes for UGC NET Law (058)

## Chapter 0: Exam Pattern, Trends and Study Method

**Exam format (UGC NET Law, Subject Code 58).** Paper 2 has 100 MCQs of 2 marks each (200 marks). Paper 1 (General) and Paper 2 are taken in one continuous 3-hour sitting, and there is no negative marking, so never leave a question blank. The syllabus has 10 units, and Unit IX is one of the most **fact-dense and statute-driven** units. Marks here come from precision: section numbers, years, durations, penalties, institution names and case holdings. Typically this unit contributes somewhere around 8–12 questions (my estimate from past-paper patterns, not an official figure), spread across IPR (about 60–65%) and IT law/cyber law (about 35–40%).

### 0.1 How questions are asked

- **Direct fact:** "The term of a patent in India is…", "The Biological Diversity Act was enacted in…"

- **Multi-statement (I, II, III, IV):** "Which of the statements is/are correct?" This is the commonest style. Examiners plant one subtle error, such as 60 instead of 50 years, or Section 43 instead of Section 66.

- **Match the following:** Cases ↔ principles; Conventions ↔ subjects; Sections ↔ offences; Institutions ↔ locations.

- **Assertion–Reason:** usually on theories (Locke/Hegel/utilitarian), on why Section 3(d) exists, or on the Shreya Singhal reasoning.

- **Chronological order:** Paris → Berne → UCC → Rome → TRIPS → WCT; or the IT Act 2000 → 2008 amendment → Shreya Singhal → DPDP Act.

- **"Which is NOT" / odd one out:** items not patentable under Section 3, offences not in the IT Act, bodies not part of the three-tier biodiversity structure.

### 0.2 Recent trends

1.  Heavy weight on **statutory provisions of the Copyright Act 1957, Patents Act 1970, Trade Marks Act 1999, GI Act 1999, IT Act 2000** and their time periods.

2.  **Landmark case–principle matching**: Novartis (Section 3(d)), Bayer v. Natco (compulsory licence), Shreya Singhal (Section 66A), Puttaswamy (privacy), Amar Nath Sehgal (moral rights), Eastern Book Company (originality), Toyota v. Prius (territoriality), Cadila (deceptive similarity).

3.  **Recent legislative churn is a favourite:** Tribunals Reforms Act 2021 (IPAB abolished); Biological Diversity (Amendment) Act 2023; Digital Personal Data Protection Act 2023 and DPDP Rules 2025 (verify commencement status); Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita, Bharatiya Nagarik Suraksha Sanhita and Bharatiya Sakshya Adhiniyam 2023 (in force 1 July 2024), which change the IPC/CrPC/Evidence Act references in cyber law; Patents (Amendment) Rules 2024; the Promotion and Regulation of Online Gaming Act 2025; WIPO Treaty on IP, Genetic Resources and Associated Traditional Knowledge (May 2024); WIPO Riyadh Design Law Treaty (November 2024).

4.  **Cross-over topics:** GI vs trademark; TK vs patents; intermediary liability under Section 79 IT Act vs copyright infringement; Section 81 IT Act (proviso protecting copyright/patent rights).

5.  **Trap words:** "only", "always", "all", "must", "exclusively". IPR rules usually have exceptions, so extreme words often signal a wrong option.

### 0.3 Study method: the three layers

- *Memory layer:* numbers, years, sections, places. Learn them from the tables and "Quick facts" boxes.

- *Concept layer:* why the rule exists (the theory behind it), and the difference between look-alike concepts (assignment vs licence; infringement vs passing off; digital vs electronic signature).

- *Analysis layer:* apply the rule to a fact pattern, or identify which case supports which principle.

## Chapter 1: Concept and Meaning of Intellectual Property

### 1.1 Meaning

Intellectual property (IP) refers to **creations of the human intellect to which the law grants exclusive, time-bound (or, in some cases, renewable) rights** that allow the creator or owner to exclude others from exploiting the creation without permission. The property is *intangible* (incorporeal): the right is not in the physical object but in the idea-expression, invention, mark or know-how embodied in it. Buying a book gives you ownership of the copy, not the copyright; buying a patented machine does not give you the patent.

The classic definition is in **Article 2(viii) of the WIPO Convention 1967**, which lists IP as rights relating to: literary, artistic and scientific works; performances of performing artists, phonograms and broadcasts; inventions in all fields of human endeavour; scientific discoveries; industrial designs; trademarks, service marks and commercial names and designations; protection against unfair competition; and all other rights resulting from intellectual activity in the industrial, scientific, literary or artistic fields.

**Industrial property vs copyright.** *Industrial property* (patents, trademarks, designs, GIs, unfair competition) is governed internationally by the **Paris Convention**; *copyright and related rights* by the **Berne Convention** and the Rome Convention.

### 1.3 Essential features of IP rights

- **Intangible and incorporeal:** the same creation can be used simultaneously by many people, which is why law must create artificial scarcity.

- **Territorial:** rights exist only in the country that grants them (an Indian patent has no effect in the US). The Paris and Berne principles of national treatment reduce the harshness of this.

- **Exclusive but limited:** limited in time (except trademarks, GIs and trade secrets), in scope (fair dealing, compulsory licences, the Bolar exception) and by public interest.

- **Negative right:** primarily a right to *exclude*, not a right to practise. You may hold a patent on an improvement whose use still infringes someone else's earlier patent.

- **Freely transferable** (assignment, licence, mortgage), except GI rights (Section 24 GI Act) and moral rights.

- **Registration:** mandatory for patents, designs, registered trademarks and GIs; **optional** for copyright (Section 45 registration is only prima facie evidence under Section 48).

### 1.4 Constitutional and institutional framework in India

- **Entry 49, List I (Union List):** "Patents, inventions and designs; copyright; trade-marks and merchandise marks." Parliament has exclusive power over these subjects.

- **Article 300A** (right to property) has been held to include IP. In *Entertainment Network (India) Ltd v. Super Cassette Industries Ltd* (2008, SC), the Court recognised the balance between the copyright owner's property right and the public interest in access.

- **Article 21** (health) and **Article 39(b)** (distribution of material resources) are invoked to justify limits on pharmaceutical patents (the Section 3(d) and compulsory licensing debate).

- **Article 51(c)** and Entry 14, List I: treaty-making and implementing international obligations (TRIPS compliance).

- **Administration:** the Department for Promotion of Industry and Internal Trade (DPIIT, Ministry of Commerce) is the nodal department. The **Office of the Controller General of Patents, Designs and Trade Marks (CGPDTM)**, headquartered in **Mumbai**, oversees the patent offices (Kolkata, Delhi, Mumbai, Chennai), the Trade Marks Registry (Mumbai, with branch offices including Delhi, Kolkata, Chennai and Ahmedabad) and the Geographical Indications Registry (**Chennai**). The Copyright Office and the Registrar of Copyrights are in New Delhi (Department-level administration under DPIIT).

- **National IPR Policy 2016** (slogan *"Creative India; Innovative India"*): seven objectives including IPR awareness, creation of IPRs, legal framework, administration, commercialisation, enforcement and human capital. **CIPAM** (Cell for IPR Promotion and Management) implements it.

- **Dispute forums:** The Intellectual Property Appellate Board (IPAB), established under the Trade Marks Act 1999, was **abolished by the Tribunals Reforms Act 2021**. Appeals formerly heard by IPAB now go to the **High Courts** (Delhi High Court has a dedicated **IP Division**, 2021). Infringement suits go to the District Court/High Court and are commercial disputes under the **Commercial Courts Act 2015**.

### 1.5 Exhaustion of rights (important concept question)

The **doctrine of exhaustion** (first-sale doctrine) says that once the owner sells a product lawfully, the right to control its further resale ends. Three models: *national* (only sales within the country exhaust the right), *regional* (e.g. EU) and *international* (sale anywhere exhausts the right). India follows **international exhaustion** for patents (Section 107A(b), parallel imports) and, through Section 30(3)–(4) of the Trade Marks Act, for trademarks (*Kapil Wadhwa v. Samsung Electronics*, Delhi HC 2012). Parallel imports in copyright are restricted under Section 51(b)(iv) (import of infringing copies). TRIPS Article 6 leaves exhaustion to each member.

## Chapter 2: Theories of Intellectual Property

Theories justify *why* the law should grant exclusive rights over intangible creations. UGC NET asks mainly: **which thinker, which theory, and what is its core idea** (and sometimes which theory best explains a particular doctrine). Learn the table first, then read the explanations.

| Theory                           | Key thinkers                                        | Core idea                                                                                                     | Best explains                                                    |
|----------------------------------|-----------------------------------------------------|---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------|------------------------------------------------------------------|
| Natural rights / Labour theory   | John Locke                                          | A person owns the fruits of their labour; mixing labour with the common creates property                      | Copyright and patents as reward for effort; "sweat of the brow"  |
| Personality theory               | Kant, Hegel (modern: Margaret Radin, Justin Hughes) | Property is an extension of personality; creative works embody the creator's self                             | Moral rights (paternity, integrity); droit d'auteur tradition    |
| Utilitarian / Incentive theory   | Jeremy Bentham, J.S. Mill                           | IP rights give incentive to create and invest; maximises social welfare                                       | Patents, limited-term copyright, TRIPS rationale                 |
| Economic/Public goods theory     | Modern law and economics (Landes & Posner)          | Creations are non-rival and non-excludable, so free-riding destroys incentives unless law creates exclusivity | Why IP exists at all; why terms are limited                      |
| Social planning theory           | William Fisher                                      | IP should be used to build a just and attractive culture                                                      | Fair use, compulsory licences, public interest limits            |
| Prospect theory                  | Edmund Kitch                                        | Patent grant is like a mining claim that lets the patentee coordinate development of a technology             | Early patent filing, scope of claims                             |
| Contract / Social bargain theory | Classical patent theory                             | Inventor discloses the invention; society grants a temporary monopoly in exchange                             | Disclosure requirement, sufficiency of specification, term limit |
| Reward theory                    | Associated with natural-law tradition               | Society owes the creator a reward for benefit conferred                                                       | Remuneration claims, royalties                                   |
| Tragedy of the anticommons       | Michael Heller                                      | Too many overlapping exclusive rights block use of resources                                                  | Patent thickets, licensing bottlenecks                           |

### 2.1 Labour (natural rights) theory: Locke

In his *Second Treatise of Government* (1690), Locke argued that God gave the world to humankind in common, but a person owns his body and therefore his labour. When labour is mixed with a thing in the common, it becomes the labourer's property. Applied to IP, the author who labours to compose a work or the inventor who toils to create an invention deserves ownership. Two limits are famous: the **"enough and as good" proviso** (appropriation is justified only if enough and as good is left in common for others) and the **spoilage limitation**. Critics say ideas are not scarce, so the proviso is hard to apply, and that labour theory cannot justify a *time-limited* right. The theory underlies the old UK "sweat of the brow" standard (*University of London Press v. University Tutorial Press*, 1916), later displaced in India by the "modicum of creativity" standard (*Eastern Book Company v. D.B. Modak*, 2008).

### 2.2 Personality theory: Kant and Hegel

Kant treated a book as an act of speech by the author to the public, an expression of personality that cannot be fully alienated. Hegel argued that property is the embodiment of the will and personality of the owner. Radin's "personhood" version distinguishes *personal* property (closely tied to self-development) from *fungible* property. This theory explains **moral rights**: the rights of paternity and integrity that survive assignment of copyright (Section 57, Copyright Act; Article 6bis Berne). It is dominant in civil-law countries (France, Germany), where author's rights (*droit d'auteur*) are central, while the common-law tradition leans utilitarian.

### 2.3 Utilitarian and economic theories

Bentham's maxim "the greatest happiness of the greatest number" supports IP only where benefits of creativity exceed the costs of monopoly. The economic form is the **incentive theory**: information is a *public good* (non-rival: my use does not diminish yours; non-excludable: hard to stop copying), so without legal exclusivity the creator cannot recover costs and too little will be created. This theory explains why terms are limited, why fair dealing and compulsory licences exist, and why the US Constitution grants Congress power "to promote the progress of science and useful arts." TRIPS Article 7 (objectives: "mutual advantage of producers and users of technological knowledge") is explicitly utilitarian. Criticisms: it rests on assumptions that are hard to measure, and it cannot explain moral rights.

### 2.4 Social planning theory and the commons

Fisher's social planning theory asks that IP be used as a tool to achieve a just culture, giving weight to democratic participation, diversity and access. It underpins **public-interest limits**: Sections 31–31D (compulsory/statutory licences) and Section 52 (fair dealing) of the Copyright Act, and Sections 84 and 92 (compulsory licences) of the Patents Act. The **commons** and **anticommons** arguments warn that over-protection harms follow-on innovation.

### 2.5 Related concepts examiners like

- **Idea–expression dichotomy:** copyright protects expression, not ideas (*R.G. Anand v. Delux Films*, SC 1978).

- **Originality tests:** sweat of the brow (UK, old) vs modicum of creativity (US *Feist v. Rural*, 1991; India *Eastern Book Company*, 2008).

- **Rights vs welfare:** natural-law and personality theories are *rights-based* (deontological); utilitarian and economic theories are *consequence-based*.

- **Public domain:** works whose protection has expired or never existed, free for all to use.

### 3.2 Paris Convention 1883

The Paris Convention is the foundation of industrial property law. Its core principles are:

- **National treatment** (Art. 2): foreigners from member states get the same protection as nationals.

- **Right of priority** (Art. 4): an applicant who first files in one member state has **12 months** (patents, utility models) or **6 months** (industrial designs, trademarks) to file in other member states and claim the first filing date. *Commonly tested: 12 vs 6 months.*

- **Independence of patents** (Art. 4bis): a patent in one country is independent of patents for the same invention elsewhere.

- **Well-known marks** (Art. 6bis): protection against registration or use of marks that conflict with well-known marks even if unregistered.

- Art. 10bis: protection against **unfair competition**; Art. 10: false indications of source.

- Art. 5A: compulsory licence only after 4 years from filing or 3 years from grant (the basis of Section 84(1) of Indian Patents Act: three years from grant).

### 3.3 Berne Convention 1886

Berne rests on **three basic principles**: (1) **national treatment**; (2) **automatic protection** (no formalities such as registration or notice); (3) **independence of protection** (protection does not depend on protection in the country of origin). Other points: minimum term **life of the author + 50 years** (Art. 7); **moral rights** (Art. 6bis); minimum rights of translation, reproduction, public performance, adaptation; the Appendix gives developing countries compulsory licences for translation and reproduction. The latest text is the **Paris Act 1971**. TRIPS (Art. 9) incorporates Berne (Arts. 1–21) **except Article 6bis (moral rights)**. India implements Berne through the Copyright Act 1957 and the International Copyright Order.

### 3.4 Universal Copyright Convention 1952

Sponsored by UNESCO, designed to bring countries that did not join Berne (notably the USA) into a copyright system. It requires only a notice (**©, name of owner, year**) as a formality, and sets a minimum term of life + 25 years. After the US joined Berne (1989), UCC lost importance; Berne prevails over UCC among Berne members.

### 3.5 Rome Convention 1961

Protects **performers** (minimum 20 years), **producers of phonograms** (20 years) and **broadcasting organisations** (20 years) as "neighbouring rights". **India is not a party**, but protects these rights domestically (Sections 37–39A Copyright Act; Section 13 for sound recordings). WPPT 1996 later updated rights of performers and phonogram producers for the digital environment.

### 3.6 TRIPS Agreement 1994

The most comprehensive IP treaty, administered by the **WTO**, binding on all WTO members. **Key features:**

- **Minimum standards** (members may give more protection, Art. 1.1).

- **National treatment** (Art. 3) and **Most Favoured Nation** (Art. 4), a new principle in IP law.

- **Objectives and principles:** Arts. 7 and 8 (public health, nutrition, public interest measures).

- **Copyright (Arts. 9–14):** incorporates Berne (except moral rights); computer programs as literary works (Art. 10); rental rights (Art. 11); term of at least 50 years where not based on life (Art. 12); performers/phonogram producers 50 years; broadcasters 20 years (Art. 14).

- **Trademarks (Arts. 15–21):** registrable subject matter, initial registration and each renewal at least **7 years**; compulsory licensing of trademarks not allowed (Art. 21).

- **GIs (Arts. 22–24):** general protection (Art. 22) and **additional protection for wines and spirits** (Art. 23). India has been seeking extension of Art. 23 to all products.

- **Industrial designs (Arts. 25–26):** at least 10 years.

- **Patents (Arts. 27–34):** patents available for inventions in all fields of technology (Art. 27(1)) that are new, involve an inventive step and are capable of industrial application; exclusions in Art. 27(2)–(3) (including plants and animals other than micro-organisms, and the option of *sui generis* plant variety protection, the basis of India's PPVFR Act); term **20 years from filing** (Art. 33); **compulsory licensing** conditions (Art. 31); burden of proof in process patents (Art. 34).

- **Layout-designs of integrated circuits (Arts. 35–38):** 10 years.

- **Undisclosed information (Art. 39):** protection against unfair competition and test data protection.

- **Enforcement (Part III), dispute settlement** through the WTO DSB.

- **Transition:** developing countries had until 2000 (general), plus a further 5 years (to 2005) for product patents in new fields; least-developed countries have longer transition periods. India introduced **product patents in pharmaceuticals/agrochemicals through the Patents (Amendment) Act 2005**, after the 1999 and 2002 amendments.

**Doha Declaration on TRIPS and Public Health (14 November 2001):** TRIPS "can and should be interpreted and implemented in a manner supportive of WTO members' right to protect public health"; each member has the right to grant compulsory licences and determine what constitutes an emergency. The **30 August 2003 Decision** (Paragraph 6 system) allows export of medicines made under compulsory licence to countries lacking manufacturing capacity; it was made permanent as **Article 31bis** (in force **23 January 2017**). India's counterpart is **Section 92A** of the Patents Act. At the WTO's 12th Ministerial Conference (June 2022), members adopted a **limited TRIPS decision on COVID-19 vaccines** (a temporary waiver-like flexibility for vaccine patents).

### 3.7 WIPO and the WIPO Internet Treaties

**WIPO (World Intellectual Property Organization)** was established by the 1967 Convention (in force 1970) and became a **specialised agency of the UN in 1974**; headquarters **Geneva**. It administers about 26 treaties including Paris, Berne, PCT, Madrid, Hague and Lisbon.

**WCT 1996** protects computer programs and compilations of data (databases) as literary works, grants a **right of distribution, rental and right of communication to the public**, and requires protection against circumvention of **technological protection measures (TPMs)** and removal of **rights management information (RMI)**. **WPPT 1996** gives performers moral rights and economic rights in fixed performances and phonogram producers economic rights. **India's Copyright (Amendment) Act 2012** added Sections 65A (TPMs) and 65B (RMI) to align with these treaties.

### 3.8 Patent Cooperation Treaty (PCT) 1970

One **international application** filed with a receiving office has effect in all designated PCT states. Stages: international phase (search report, written opinion, publication at 18 months, optional international preliminary examination) and **national phase** (entry into each country within **30 months** from priority date; India's period is **31 months**, Rule 20(4)(i)). PCT does not grant an "international patent"; each national office grants the patent. The Indian Patent Office acts as a Receiving Office, International Searching Authority (ISA) and International Preliminary Examining Authority (IPEA).

### 3.9 Madrid System (Trademarks)

The **Madrid Agreement (1891)** and **Madrid Protocol (1989)** create a single international registration administered by WIPO. India joined the **Protocol** (not the Agreement) in **2013**. A **basic application or registration** in the home country is required, and the international registration is dependent on it for **five years** (**central attack**). India uses the **Nice Classification** (45 classes: 1–34 goods, 35–45 services).

### 3.10 Convention on Biological Diversity, Nagoya Protocol, UPOV and ITPGRFA

- **CBD 1992 (Rio Earth Summit)** recognises **national sovereignty over biological resources**, and sets three goals: conservation, sustainable use, and **fair and equitable sharing of benefits** from genetic resources. **Article 8(j)** requires respect for traditional knowledge of indigenous and local communities.

- **Nagoya Protocol (2010)** operationalises access and benefit sharing (ABS). **Cartagena Protocol (2000)** covers biosafety.

- **UPOV** (International Union for Protection of New Varieties of Plants): India is **not a member**; it chose a *sui generis* system under TRIPS Art. 27(3)(b), balancing breeders' and farmers' rights under the PPVFR Act 2001.

- **ITPGRFA (International Treaty on Plant Genetic Resources for Food and Agriculture, 2001):** farmers' rights and a multilateral benefit-sharing system; India is a party.

- **WIPO GRATK Treaty (24 May 2024):** requires patent applicants to disclose the **country of origin or source** of genetic resources and associated traditional knowledge used in claimed inventions. It enters into force after a threshold number of ratifications (verify current ratification count).

### 3.11 Other treaties worth a glance

- **Marrakesh Treaty 2013:** India was the first country to ratify (2014); reflected in **Section 52(1)(zb)** (accessible formats for persons with disabilities).

- **Beijing Treaty on Audiovisual Performances 2012:** performers' rights in audiovisual fixations.

- **Hague Agreement:** international registration of industrial designs. **Riyadh Design Law Treaty (2024)** harmonises design formalities.

- **Lisbon Agreement 1958 and Geneva Act 2015:** appellations of origin and geographical indications.

- **Patent Law Treaty 2000** (formalities harmonisation); India is **not** a party.

- **Budapest Treaty 1977:** a deposit of a micro-organism with an International Depositary Authority satisfies the disclosure requirement.

**Quick facts for revision (Section 3).** Priority: **12 months** (patents) / **6 months** (designs, marks). Berne term: **life + 50**. India's term: **life + 60** (more than Berne). Rome Convention: India **not** a member. PCT national phase: 30 months generally, **31 in India**. Madrid Protocol: India 2013. WCT/WPPT: India 2018. TRIPS in force: **1 January 1995**. TRIPS patent term: **20 years**. UPOV: India **not** a member.

## Chapter 4: Copyright and Neighbouring Rights

### 4.1 Legislative history and framework

The **Copyright Act 1957** (in force 21 January 1958) replaced the Indian Copyright Act 1914 (which extended the UK Copyright Act 1911). It has been amended in 1983, 1984, 1991, 1994, 1999 and, most importantly, in **2012** (in force 21 June 2012; the Copyright Rules 2013 followed). The **Copyright Board** was merged into the IPAB (2017) and, after the **Tribunals Reforms Act 2021**, its functions passed to the High Court / Commercial Court. A **Registrar of Copyrights** maintains the Register (Section 44, 45). **Registration is optional**; the certificate is prima facie evidence (Section 48). Copyright subsists automatically on creation (Berne principle).

### 4.2 Nature of copyright: what is protected

Copyright protects **expression, not ideas, procedures, methods of operation or mathematical concepts** (idea–expression dichotomy: *R.G. Anand v. Delux Films*, 1978; the test of substantial similarity is how the *ordinary observer/lay viewer* sees the two works). Section 13 lists the **classes of work** in which copyright subsists:

1.  **Original literary, dramatic, musical and artistic works**

2.  **Cinematograph films**

3.  **Sound recordings**

Key definitions (Section 2):

- **Literary work (2(o))** includes computer programmes, tables and compilations **including computer databases**.

- **Dramatic work (2(h))** includes recitation, choreographic work or entertainment in dumb show, scenic arrangement (the acting form); it does not include a cinematograph film.

- **Musical work (2(p))**: music and any graphical notation; it does *not* include words or action intended to be sung, spoken or performed with the music (lyrics are a separate literary work).

- **Artistic work (2(c))**: painting, sculpture, drawing (including diagram, map, chart or plan), engraving, photograph, work of architecture, and **any other work of artistic craftsmanship**; the artistic quality of the work is irrelevant for paintings/sculptures/drawings/engravings/photographs.

- **Cinematograph film (2(f))**: any work of visual recording on any medium, including a sound recording accompanying it, and any work produced by video or any similar process.

- **Sound recording (2(xx))**: recording of sounds from which sounds can be produced, regardless of the medium.

- **Author (2(d))**: literary/dramatic – the author; musical – the composer; artistic – the artist; photograph – the photographer; **cinematograph film and sound recording – the producer**; **computer-generated work – the person who causes the work to be created**.

- **Communication to the public (2(ff))**: making a work available to the public by any means of display or diffusion, other than by issuing copies.

**Originality.** India moved from the English "sweat of the brow" standard (*University of London Press v. University Tutorial Press*, 1916) to the "modicum of creativity" standard in **Eastern Book Company v. D.B. Modak (2008, SC)**: copy-edited judgments and headnotes of law reports qualify only if the editorial inputs show a minimum degree of creativity and skill, not mere labour. The US analogue is *Feist Publications v. Rural Telephone* (1991). Photographs, translations, compilations and adaptations are protected as derivative works if original.

**No copyright in:** ideas, titles and slogans (generally, as insufficient), news facts, works of the Government that are statutory (but Government works carry copyright under Section 17(d)), and works not original. A "work" must also be fixed or expressed; copyright does not subsist in unpublished works of foreign authors unless the author is a qualified person (Section 13(2)–(3); International Copyright Order).

### 4.3 Ownership (Section 17)

The **author is the first owner** (Section 17), subject to exceptions in the absence of a contrary agreement:

- **Journalist** employed by a newspaper/magazine under a contract of service: the **proprietor** owns copyright for publication in the paper, but the journalist keeps the right for book publication (Section 17(a)).

- **Commissioned photographs, paintings, portraits, engravings** (for valuable consideration): the **commissioner** (Section 17(b)); cinematograph film made for valuable consideration – the producer is the *author* under Section 2(d)(v).

- **Employment** (contract of service or apprenticeship): the **employer** (Section 17(c)); *contract for service* (independent contractor) leaves ownership with the contractor unless assigned.

- **Government work**: the **Government** (Section 17(d)); **public undertaking** (Section 17(dd)); **international organisation** (Section 17(e)); **public lecture/address**: the person delivering (Section 17(cc)).

- **Authors of literary/musical works in a film:** the 2012 proviso to Section 17 states that nothing in clauses (b) or (c) affects the right of the author in works incorporated in a cinematograph film, other than as part of the film. The producer cannot take over lyricists' and composers' separate rights.

### 4.4 Rights of the owner: economic rights (Section 14)

Section 14 gives **exclusive rights**, which differ by class of work:

- **Literary, dramatic, musical works:** reproduce; issue copies to the public (not already in circulation); perform in public/communicate to the public; make a cinematograph film or sound recording; make translations and adaptations. For **computer programmes**, also sell or give on **commercial rental**.

- **Artistic works:** reproduce (including in 3D from 2D and vice versa); communicate; adapt; issue copies.

- **Cinematograph films:** make a copy (including a photograph of any image); sell/hire; communicate to the public.

- **Sound recordings:** make any other recording; sell/hire; communicate.

**Moral rights (Section 57), Special rights of the author:** independent of the author's economic rights and survive assignment. They comprise:

- **Right of paternity:** claim authorship of the work.

- **Right of integrity:** restrain or claim damages for any **distortion, mutilation, modification or other act** prejudicial to honour or reputation.

They can be enforced by the author or, after death, by the **legal representatives**. Cases: **Amar Nath Sehgal v. Union of India (Delhi HC, 2005)**, destruction/neglect of a bronze mural at Vigyan Bhavan violated the integrity right, with relief granted; **Mannu Bhandari v. Kala Vikas Pictures (Delhi HC, 1987)**, a film adaptation of a novel that mutilated it infringed integrity right (the right is not limited by the assignment). **Performer's moral rights** are in **Section 38B** (2012).

### 4.6 Assignment and licensing

- **Assignment (Sections 18–19):** the owner may assign the existing or future copyright (a *future* work: assignment takes effect on coming into existence). The assignment must be **in writing signed by the assignor**, and identify the work, the rights assigned, the **duration** and the **territorial extent**. If **duration is not stated, it is deemed 5 years**; if **territory is not stated, India**; if the assignee does not exercise the rights **within one year**, the assignment is deemed to **lapse** (unless otherwise specified). The 2012 proviso: authors of literary and musical works in films cannot assign the right to **receive royalties** except to legal heirs or a copyright society.

- **Licence (Sections 30–30A):** the owner can grant exclusive or non-exclusive licences in writing. Licences are not assignments of ownership.

- **Section 19A:** disputes about assignment or licence (Copyright Board earlier; now the High Court under the 2021 Act).

- **Compulsory licences:** **Section 31** (works withheld from the public), **31A** (unpublished or **orphan** works; Indian works where author is dead/unknown/untraceable), **31B** (for **persons with disabilities**, 2012), **31C** (cover versions of sound recordings, after 5 years), **Section 31D** (**statutory licence** for **broadcasting organisations** for literary and musical works and sound recordings, on payment of royalty fixed by the Appellate Board / High Court). *Tips Industries v. Wynk Music (Bombay HC, 2019)* held that Section 31D covers radio and television broadcasts, not internet streaming; the point remains debated, following a DPIIT 2016 memorandum that extended it to internet broadcasting. **Section 32** covers compulsory licence for translation.

- **Copyright societies (Section 33–36A):** associations of authors/owners that collect and distribute royalties: IPRS (Indian Performing Right Society, 1969, music composers/lyricists), PPL (Phonographic Performance Ltd), IRRO (Indian Reprographic Rights Organisation), ISRA (Indian Singers' Rights Association). Section 33: no person may carry on the business of issuing or granting licences in respect of a work without being registered/authorised as a copyright society.

### 4.7 Infringement (Section 51)

**Primary infringement (51(a)):** a person infringes if, without a licence from the owner or the Registrar of Copyrights, he **does anything the exclusive right** to do which is conferred on the owner; or he **permits for profit any place to be used for communication of the work to the public** where such communication constitutes infringement (unless he was unaware and had no reasonable ground to believe it was infringement).

**Secondary infringement (51(b)):** making for sale or hire, selling or letting for hire, distributing for the purpose of trade, exhibiting in public by way of trade, or **importing** infringing copies (except two copies for private use, 2012).

**Test of infringement:** access plus substantial similarity of expression; the *quantity and quality* of what is taken are considered (*R.G. Anand*; *Bobby Art International v. Om Pal Singh Hoon*, SC 1996, "Bandit Queen"). **Reproduction in a different medium** is infringement. **Authorising** infringement is also an infringement (*Super Cassette Industries v. Myspace*, Delhi HC DB 2016: an intermediary with actual knowledge of infringing content may be liable; safe harbour under Section 79 IT Act is limited by Section 81 proviso).

### 4.8 Limitations and exceptions: fair dealing and other free uses (Section 52)

Section 52 lists acts that are **not infringement**. Indian law uses the **closed-list fair dealing model** (unlike the open-ended US fair use). Main items:

- **Fair dealing for private or personal use including research; criticism or review; reporting of current events and current affairs including lectures delivered in public** (52(1)(a)). Not applicable to computer programmes (except as in (aa)). *Cases:* *India TV Independent News Service v. Yashraj Films* (Delhi HC 2013); *Wiley Eastern v. Indian Institute of Management* (Delhi HC 1996).

- **Computer programmes:** making backup copies, adaptation for the lawful use, observation/study/testing, and **reverse engineering for interoperability** (52(1)(aa)–(ad)).

- **Transient or incidental storage** for technological process, and for providing **electronic links, access or integration**, where not expressly prohibited by the right holder (52(1)(b)–(c), 2012).

- **Judicial proceedings**, reports of legislatures and committees; certified copies of public records.

- **Educational:** reproduction by a teacher or pupil in the course of instruction; as questions in an examination; performance in an educational institution (52(1)(i)–(j)). **Chancellor, Masters & Scholars of the University of Oxford v. Rameshwari Photocopy Services (Delhi HC 2016)**, a single judge upheld photocopying of course packs for students as covered by Section 52(1)(i); the Division Bench (2017) set aside the order and remanded; the underlying issue remains in the exam-relevant discussion.

- **Persons with disabilities (52(1)(zb)):** conversion to accessible formats, aligned with the Marrakesh Treaty.

- **Religious ceremonies and amateur club performances (52(1)(za), (z))**, certain architectural works, public art in sites, and **sound recording of literary/musical works** under compulsory conditions (cover versions).

### 4.9 Remedies

**Civil remedies (Chapter XII, Section 55):** **injunction** (including interim, *Anton Piller* order for search and seizure and *Mareva*-type orders, and **John Doe** orders for unknown infringers), **damages**, **account of profits**, **delivery up** of infringing copies and plates (Section 58). **Innocent infringement:** no damages where defendant proves he was unaware and had no reasonable ground to believe copyright subsisted (Section 55(2)), but injunction/account still available. **Jurisdiction (Section 62(2)):** suit may be filed where the plaintiff *actually and voluntarily resides, carries on business or personally works for gain*; see *Indian Performing Right Society v. Sanjay Dalia* (SC 2015). **Section 60:** relief against groundless threats of infringement proceedings.

**Criminal remedies (Chapter XIII):**

- **Section 63:** knowingly infringing or abetting: imprisonment **6 months to 3 years** and fine **₹50,000 to ₹2 lakh**.

- **Section 63A:** enhanced penalty on second or subsequent conviction: imprisonment **1 to 3 years** and fine **₹1 lakh to ₹2 lakh**.

- **Section 63B:** knowing use of an infringing copy of a computer programme: imprisonment **7 days to 3 years**, fine **₹50,000 to ₹2 lakh**.

- **Section 65A:** circumvention of **technological protection measures**: imprisonment up to **2 years** and fine. **Section 65B:** tampering with **rights management information**: up to 2 years and fine.

- **Section 64:** police may seize infringing copies without a warrant (officer not below Sub-Inspector).

- **Section 53:** the Registrar may ban import of infringing copies (Customs Act).

### 4.10 Neighbouring (related) rights

Rights **related to** copyright, protecting those who interpret, record or broadcast works:

- **Broadcast reproduction right (Section 37):** every broadcasting organisation has this right: re-broadcast, record, reproduce or sell/hire the broadcast. **Term 25 years.**

- **Performer's right (Section 38–38B):** a performer (**Section 2(qq)** includes actor, singer, musician, dancer, acrobat, juggler, conjurer, snake charmer, a person delivering a lecture or any other person who makes a performance) has exclusive rights to make a sound/visual recording, reproduce, broadcast and communicate the performance. **Term 50 years.** Section 38A(2): once a performer consents to incorporation of the performance in a cinematograph film, he may not object to its exploitation, but is entitled to royalties for other uses; **Section 38B** gives moral rights (paternity and integrity in respect of performance).

- **Phonogram producers** (sound recording producers) have rights under Section 13/14 as authors of sound recordings (60 years).

- **Section 39:** acts that do not infringe performers' and broadcasters' rights.

- **Cases:** *Fortune Films International v. Dev Anand* (Bombay HC, 1979) on performer's rights; the 2012 Act brought performers' royalties into the statute.

### 4.11 The 2012 Amendment: key points

- Introduced **performers' moral rights** and made rights of performers and broadcasters TRIPS/WPPT-compliant.

- **Authors' right to royalties in films** (non-assignable except to heirs/copyright societies).

- **Section 31B, 52(1)(zb)** for persons with disabilities; **Section 31D** (statutory licence for broadcasting); **Section 31C** (cover versions).

- **Sections 65A and 65B** on TPMs and RMI; **ISP/transient storage** exceptions in Section 52(1)(b)–(c).

- **Sections 19(4)–(10)**: clear time/territory defaults and the one-year non-exercise rule.

- **Section 33**: copyright societies regulated, with owners' governance rights.

### 4.12 Recent trends in copyright

- **Generative AI and copyright:** *ANI Media v. OpenAI* (Delhi HC, filed 2024) on whether training on news content infringes; DPIIT expert committee working papers on AI training and licensing (2025) – *verify current status*. The Act has no "text and data mining" exception.

- **Authorship of AI works:** under Section 2(d)(vi) the author of a computer-generated work is the person who causes it to be created; the Copyright Office's registration of an AI co-authored work (RAGHAV) and its withdrawal remain a discussion point.

- **Dynamic injunctions and website blocking** for piracy: *UTV Software Communication v. 1337X.to* (Delhi HC 2019) allowed injunctions extending to mirror/redirect websites; *Star India v. Moviemaza* continues the line.

- **Personality rights and voice cloning:** Delhi and Bombay High Courts (e.g. *Anil Kapoor v. Simply Life India*, 2023; *Arijit Singh v. Codible Ventures*, Bombay HC 2024) granted injunctions against AI voice/image misuse.

- **Statutory licensing for internet broadcasting** and **OTT royalties for lyricists** remain contested.

**Quick facts for revision (Section 4).** Act: **1957**; big amendment **2012**. Term: **life + 60**; films/sound recordings/photographs **60**; broadcast reproduction **25**; performers **50**. Default assignment: **5 years, India, lapse after 1 year of non-exercise**. Moral rights: **Section 57**. Infringement: **Section 51**; fair dealing: **Section 52**; criminal: **Sections 63–63B** (6 months–3 yrs / ₹50,000–₹2 lakh). Orphan works licence: **31A**. Disability: **31B / 52(1)(zb)**. Jurisdiction: **62(2)**. Copyright societies: **33–36**.

## Chapter 5: Law of Patents

### 5.1 Framework and history

The **Patents Act 1970** (in force 20 April 1972) replaced the Indian Patents and Designs Act 1911. It was modelled on the **Ayyangar Committee Report (1959)** and originally allowed **only process patents** for food, medicines and chemicals (Section 5). TRIPS compliance came in three steps: the **Patents (Amendment) Act 1999** (mailbox facility and exclusive marketing rights), the **2002 Amendment** (term extended to 20 years, Sections 3(p), 25 and TK/biological-material disclosure provisions) and the **2005 Amendment** (**product patents** in all fields of technology; Section 5 omitted; Section 3(d) strengthened; Section 92A added). The **Patents Rules 2003** (amended 2016, 2019, 2021 and **2024**) govern procedure. The **Patents (Amendment) Rules 2024** (March 2024) among other changes relaxed the **Form 27 (working statement)** to a three-financial-year cycle (verify exact current text). A patent is a **statutory monopoly**, not a natural right: *Bishwanath Prasad Radhey Shyam v. Hindustan Metal Industries* (SC 1978) explained that a patent is a reward for the inventor's disclosure, and that the grant of a monopoly must not be broader than the invention.

### 5.2 Meaning and criteria of patentability

- **Invention (Section 2(1)(j)):** a **new product or process involving an inventive step and capable of industrial application**.

- **New (2(1)(l)):** not anticipated by prior publication, prior use or otherwise, anywhere in the world (**absolute novelty**) before the date of filing/priority.

- **Inventive step (2(1)(ja)):** a feature involving **technical advance** compared to existing knowledge, **or** having **economic significance** or both, and making the invention **not obvious to a person skilled in the art** ("PSITA").

- **Capable of industrial application (2(1)(ac)):** the invention is capable of being made or used in an industry.

**Subject matter excluded: Section 3 ("What are not inventions").** This is the **single most tested provision** in patent law. Learn all of 3(a)–(p):

| Clause | Not an invention                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                  |
|--------|---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------|
| 3(a)   | Frivolous inventions or claims contrary to well-established natural laws                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                          |
| 3(b)   | Inventions whose commercial exploitation would be **contrary to public order or morality**, or cause serious prejudice to human, animal, plant life or health, or the environment                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                 |

**Section 4:** inventions relating to **atomic energy** (Atomic Energy Act 1962, Section 20) are not patentable.

**Section 3(d) and Novartis.** *Novartis AG v. Union of India* (SC, 1 April 2013) is the leading case. Novartis sought a patent on the **beta crystalline form of imatinib mesylate** (Glivec) for cancer. The Supreme Court held that the beta crystalline form was a new form of a known substance (imatinib free base, disclosed in the Zimmermann patent) and that the claimed increase in **bioavailability** did not show **enhanced therapeutic efficacy**; the explanation to Section 3(d) requires that "efficacy" means **therapeutic efficacy** for medicines. The Court read 3(d) as a standard setting a **higher threshold for incremental innovations** to prevent **evergreening** (extending monopoly by trivial changes), and held it consistent with TRIPS and Article 21. It also said that **Section 3(d) is not a limit on patentability of true innovations**.

**Computer-related inventions (CRI).** Section 3(k) excludes software **per se**. **CRI Guidelines 2017** (revised) allow patents where the claim shows a **technical effect or technical contribution** and is not merely software. *Ferid Allani v. Union of India* (Delhi HC 2019) held that software with technical effect is patentable; *Microsoft Technology Licensing LLC v. Assistant Controller of Patents* (Delhi HC 2023) applied the same approach.

**Biotechnology.** *Dimminaco AG v. Controller of Patents* (Calcutta HC 2002): a **vaccine containing a live virus** was patentable as the claimed process produced a manufacture, rejecting the Controller's "living organism" refusal. Micro-organisms are patentable (*Diamond v. Chakrabarty*, US 1980, is the global reference; the Budapest Treaty deposit is required where the invention uses a micro-organism not publicly available). Plant varieties are under the **PPVFR Act 2001**.

### 5.3 Who can apply (Section 6) and forms of application

A patent application may be made by: the **true and first inventor**; the **assignee** of the right to apply; or the **legal representative** of a deceased person who was entitled before death. Applications are made in **Form 1** with **Form 2** (specification: **provisional** or **complete**), plus **Form 3** (Section 8: statement and undertaking on foreign filing), **Form 5** (declaration of inventorship, for convention/PCT applications) and **Form 26** (authorisation of patent agent). The **specification** must fully and particularly describe the invention and the best method of performing it known to the applicant (Section 10(4)), and the **claims** define the scope of protection. A **provisional specification** secures a priority date, with the complete specification due **within 12 months** (Section 9).

### 5.4 Procedure for grant: step by step

1.  **Filing** (Section 7) at the appropriate Patent Office (determined by the applicant's/inventor's residence or place of business).

2.  **Publication** (Section 11A) automatically **18 months** from the filing/priority date; **early publication** can be requested (Rule 24A, within about a month).

3.  **Request for examination** (Section 11B) in **Form 18** within **48 months** from the earlier of the priority/filing date (**expedited examination, Rule 24C, Form 18A** for startups, small entities, women applicants, applications where India was the ISA and some others).

4.  **Examination** by an examiner (Section 12): the **First Examination Report (FER)** is issued; the applicant must **comply within 6 months** (extendable by 3 months; total 9 months from FER) (Section 21).

5.  **Search for anticipation by previous publication and prior claiming** (Section 13).

6.  **Pre-grant opposition (Section 25(1)):** any person, after publication and **before grant**, may file a representation. Grounds include: **wrongful obtaining; prior publication; prior claiming; public knowledge/use; obviousness; not an invention or not patentable (Sections 3, 4); insufficient description; failure to disclose Section 8 information; convention-date non-compliance; non-disclosure or wrong mention of source/geographical origin of biological material (25(1)(j)); anticipation by knowledge available within a local or indigenous community in India or elsewhere (25(1)(k)).** (Rule 55: within 6 months of publication or before grant, whichever is later.)

7.  **Grant** (Section 43) and **sealing**: the patent is granted if all requirements are met, entered in the Register, and published in the **Patent Office Journal**. The **date of the patent is the date of filing** (Section 43(4)). Where it is a PCT application, the international filing date.

8.  **Post-grant opposition (Section 25(2)):** by any **person interested**, within **12 months** from the **date of publication of grant**, before an **Opposition Board**, then decided by the Controller. Grounds are the same list as pre-grant (with the wording "person interested" and no "public knowledge/use" difference of note). A **revocation petition (Section 64)** can be filed before the **High Court** or as a counterclaim in an infringement suit.

9.  **Renewal (Section 53(2)):** annual renewal fee from the **second/third year** onwards (the patent lapses on non-payment but may be restored, Sections 60–61).

**Other key provisions**

- **Section 8:** the applicant must furnish information about corresponding foreign applications; failure is a ground for opposition and revocation.

- **Section 16:** **divisional applications** where the application claims more than one invention (before grant).

- **Section 54:** **patent of addition** (an improvement or modification of the main invention); term coterminous with the main patent.

- **Section 35–39:** **secrecy directions** for inventions relevant to defence; **Section 39:** an **Indian resident** must obtain **prior written permission** (or wait **6 weeks**) before filing abroad.

- **Section 138 / Rule 20:** PCT national-phase entry (31 months).

- **Priority:** Convention applications within **12 months** (Section 135).

- **Fees:** reduced for **startups, small entities and educational institutions** (80% rebate for startups since 2021 Rules; 2024 Rules extended educational-institution benefits – verify).

### 5.5 Rights of the patentee (Section 48)

Subject to the Act, a patent grants:

- For a **product patent**: the exclusive right to **prevent third parties without consent from making, using, offering for sale, selling or importing** the product in India.

- For a **process patent**: the exclusive right to prevent others from **using the process**, and from **using, offering for sale, selling or importing the product directly obtained** by the process (Section 48(b)). **Section 104A:** burden of proof shifts to the defendant in process patent suits where the product is new, or where there is a substantial likelihood that the product was made by the patented process and the patentee has been unable to determine the process used.

- **Term (Section 53):** **20 years** from the **date of filing** of the application (for PCT, from the international filing date), for all patents.

- **Assignment and licence:** must be **in writing** and **registered** (Sections 68–70); **co-owners** have rights under Sections 50–51; **Section 70:** a co-owner may work the patent but cannot assign/license without the others' consent (subject to agreement).

- **Working of the patent:** patentees/licensees must file **Form 27** statements on commercial working (Section 146).

### 5.6 Limitations and exceptions to patent rights

- **Government use (Section 47):** the Government may import or make patented products for its own use, for research, or for distribution in dispensaries, hospitals and medical institutions. **Sections 99–103:** **use of inventions for the purposes of Government**, with notice and payment of **royalty/compensation** and the Central Government's power of **acquisition (Section 102)**.

- **Research and experimental use** (Section 47(3)): a patented process/product may be used for research or instruction.

- **Bolar provision (Section 107A(a)):** any act of making, constructing, using, selling or importing a patented invention **solely for uses reasonably related to the development and submission of information required under any law** that regulates manufacture/sale/import of any product (so generics can seek regulatory approval before patent expiry). **107A(b):** **parallel importation** of patented products from a person duly authorised to sell them – international exhaustion.

- **Compulsory licences (Sections 84–92):**

  - **Section 84:** any person interested may apply **after 3 years from the date of grant** on grounds that (a) the **reasonable requirements of the public** have not been satisfied; (b) the invention is **not available at a reasonably affordable price**; (c) the invention is **not worked in the territory of India**. *Bayer Corporation v. Union of India and Natco Pharma* (Controller 2012; IPAB 2013; Bombay HC 2014; SC dismissed SLP 2014) is India's **first (and best-known) compulsory licence**: **sorafenib tosylate (Nexavar)**, for kidney/liver cancer, granted to **Natco** on all three grounds, with royalty of 6% of net sales.

  - **Section 92:** compulsory licence **at any time after grant** in circumstances of **national emergency, extreme urgency** or **public non-commercial use**, by notification of the Central Government.

  - **Section 92A:** compulsory licence for **export of patented pharmaceutical products to countries with insufficient manufacturing capacity** (TRIPS Para 6 system; Art. 31bis).

  - **Section 83:** **general principles** applicable to working of patented inventions: patents are granted to encourage inventions and secure that they are **worked on a commercial scale** in India **without undue delay** and not merely for importation; they should not impede protection of public health and nutrition.

  - **Section 90:** terms and conditions of CL (reasonable royalty, non-exclusive, non-assignable, with the main aim of supply in India).

  - **Section 66:** **revocation of a patent in the public interest** by the Central Government.

- **Patent linkage rejected:** *Bayer Corporation v. Union of India* (Delhi HC 2010) held that the drug regulator cannot refuse marketing approval merely because a patent exists; India does not follow patent linkage.

- **Traditional knowledge protection** (Section 3(p)) and **disclosure of biological source** (Section 10(4)(d)(ii)(A)); **Section 64(1)(p)–(q)** grounds for revocation.

### 5.7 Revocation (Section 64)

On **petition of a person interested or the Central Government** (or counterclaim in an infringement suit) to the **High Court**. Grounds (Section 64(1)) include: patent granted to a person not entitled; **already claimed in an earlier Indian patent (prior claiming)**; **invention published before** the priority date or publicly known/used in India (**prior publication/use**); **obvious** or lacking inventive step; **not an invention** under the Act or **not patentable**; **insufficient/ambiguous description**; wrong **Section 8** information; secrecy directions contravened; **failure to disclose the source of biological material** (64(1)(p)); and anticipation by **traditional knowledge** (64(1)(q)).

### 5.8 Infringement and remedies

- **What is infringement?** Unauthorised making, using, offering for sale, selling or importing the patented product, or using the patented process (and its direct products), **in India**, during the term of the patent. **Test:** the claims are construed purposively; the court looks at the **"pith and marrow"** of the invention (*Raj Prakash v. Mangat Ram Chowdhary*, Delhi HC 1977); equivalents are considered under the doctrine of equivalents/purposive construction (Indian courts have also applied *Catnic*). Infringement and revocation are decided on the claims **as granted**.

- **Jurisdiction (Section 104):** suits lie in the **District Court**; where the defendant counterclaims for revocation, the suit is transferred to the **High Court**. After the Tribunals Reforms Act 2021, appeals from the Controller go to the **High Court** (Section 117A).

- **Reliefs (Section 108):** **injunction** (interim and final), **damages or account of profits** (against the infringer; innocent infringer exception, Section 111), **delivery up**. **Section 106:** relief against **groundless threats of infringement proceedings**. **Section 109:** an **exclusive licensee** may sue (patentee as co-plaintiff); **Section 110:** the right of a licensee/assignee.

- **Standard-essential patents (SEPs):** *Telefonaktiebolaget LM Ericsson v. Lava International* (Delhi HC 2024) awarded approx. **₹244 crore** in damages for SEP infringement of 2G/3G patents, an important recent decision on **FRAND** (fair, reasonable and non-discriminatory) licensing.

- **Offences (Chapter XX):** falsification of Register (Section 123), unauthorised claim of patent rights (Section 120), wrongful claim of being a patent agent, breach of secrecy directions (Section 118), refusal to supply information; with penalties of imprisonment (up to 6 months in some provisions) and fines, though many criminal provisions were **decriminalised or reduced** under the **Jan Vishwas (Amendment of Provisions) Act 2023** (verify provisions).

### 5.9 Recent trends in patent law

- **IPAB abolition (2021):** appeals now lie to the **High Court**; the Delhi High Court's **IP Division** deals with Patent Office appeals.

- **Section 3(d) litigation continues** (e.g. disputes on drugs such as risdiplam and linagliptin).

- **Standard-essential patents** and **FRAND damages** (Ericsson v. Lava, 2024).

- **Increasing use of the 2021–2024 Rules** to reduce fees and timelines (startups, educational institutions, expedited examination).

- **Patent grants** by the Indian Patent Office have risen sharply since 2020 (CGPDTM annual reports).

- **Genome/AI inventions and CRI Guidelines;** **disclosure of origin** under the WIPO GRATK Treaty (2024) and Section 10(4)(d)(ii)(A).

- **Patents (Amendment) Rules 2024:** Form 27 once in three years (verify), changes to Form 3 timeline and divisional applications.

**Quick facts for revision (Section 5).** Act: **1970** (in force 1972). Term: **20 years from filing**. Publication: **18 months**. RFE: **48 months**. FER reply: **6 + 3 months**. Pre-grant opposition: **Section 25(1)**; post-grant: **25(2)** within **12 months** of publication of grant. Revocation: **Section 64**. Compulsory licence: **Section 84** (after **3 years**), **Section 92** (emergency, any time), **Section 92A** (export). Bolar: **107A**. Product patents: **2005**. Not inventions: **Section 3**; atomic energy: **Section 4**. Novartis: **2013** (3(d), "therapeutic efficacy"). First CL: **Natco (Bayer's sorafenib), 2012**. Burden of proof in process patents: **Section 104A**. Foreign filing permission: **Section 39 (6 weeks)**.

## Chapter 6: Law of Trademarks

### 6.1 Framework

The **Trade Marks Act 1999** (in force **15 September 2003**) replaced the Trade and Merchandise Marks Act 1958 and brought Indian law in line with TRIPS. The **Trade Marks Rules 2017** (in force 6 March 2017) replaced the 2002 Rules and streamlined forms (single **Form TM-A** for all applications, e-filing fee concessions). The **Registrar of Trade Marks** works from the Trade Marks Registry (HQ **Mumbai**; offices at Delhi, Kolkata, Chennai, Ahmedabad). Classification follows the **Nice Classification: 45 classes** (classes 1–34 goods; 35–45 services). Functions of a trademark: **indication of origin, guarantee of quality, advertising and investment function**.

**Definitions (Section 2(1)):**

- **Trade mark (zb):** a mark **capable of being represented graphically** and **capable of distinguishing** the goods or services of one person from those of others, and may include the **shape of goods, their packaging and combination of colours**. (*Sound marks* can be represented graphically through musical notation, and the 2017 Rules allow an MP3 file; the first Indian sound mark was the **Yahoo! yodel** (2008).)

- **Mark (m):** includes a device, brand, heading, label, ticket, name, signature, word, letter, numeral, shape of goods, packaging or combination of colours, or any combination.

- **Well-known trade mark (zg):** a mark that has become so to the substantial segment of the public which uses the goods/receives the services that its use on other goods/services would likely be taken as indicating a connection with the proprietor.

- **Certification trade mark (e)**, **collective mark (g)**, **service (z)**, **permitted use (r)**, **deceptively similar (h)** (a mark that so nearly resembles another as to be likely to deceive or cause confusion).

Note: **defensive registration** (Section 47, TMMA 1958) **was abolished** by the 1999 Act; protection of well-known marks in dissimilar goods replaces it.

### 6.3 Grounds of refusal

**Absolute grounds (Section 9):** a mark shall not be registered if it:

- is **devoid of any distinctive character** (not capable of distinguishing);

- consists exclusively of marks or indications that may serve to designate the **kind, quality, quantity, intended purpose, values, geographical origin, time of production** or other characteristics of goods or services (**descriptive**);

- consists exclusively of marks which have become **customary in the current language** or in bona fide and established practices of trade (**generic**);

- is of such nature as to **deceive the public or cause confusion**; is likely to **hurt the religious susceptibilities** of any class; contains **scandalous or obscene** matter; or is **prohibited under the Emblems and Names (Prevention of Improper Use) Act 1950**;

- **Section 9(3):** a mark that consists exclusively of the **shape of goods resulting from the nature of the goods**, or **necessary to obtain a technical result**, or **which gives substantial value to the goods**.

- **Proviso to 9(1): acquired distinctiveness** (a mark that has become distinctive through use before the application date, or is a well-known mark, is registrable). **Spectrum of distinctiveness:** generic \< descriptive \< suggestive \< arbitrary \< fanciful (coined) marks; fanciful/arbitrary marks get the strongest protection.

**Relative grounds (Section 11):**

- Registration is refused where, because of **identity or similarity with an earlier trade mark** and **identity or similarity of goods/services**, there exists a **likelihood of confusion**, including likelihood of association (11(1)).

- **Section 11(2):** a mark identical or similar to an **earlier mark with a reputation in India** shall not be registered for **dissimilar goods** where use would take unfair advantage of, or be detrimental to, the distinctive character or repute of the earlier mark.

- **Section 11(3):** prohibited where use is liable to be prevented by the law of **passing off** or **copyright**.

- **Section 11(6)–(9):** factors in determining a **well-known mark**: knowledge in the relevant sector of the public (including as a result of promotion), duration, extent and geographical area of use, promotion, registration or applications, record of successful enforcement. **Section 11(9):** it is **not necessary** that the mark has been used in India, registered in India, or is well known to the public at large in India.

- **Section 12:** **honest concurrent use** and special circumstances allow registration of identical or similar marks.

### 6.4 Procedure for registration

1.  **Search** the register (optional but advisable) and **file the application** (Section 18): by a person claiming to be the proprietor of a mark used or **proposed to be used**; one application for a single class (multi-class permitted in a single application now).

2.  **Examination** by the Registrar (Section 19, 20): objections raised by an **examination report**; the applicant replies and may be heard.

3.  **Acceptance and advertisement** in the **Trade Marks Journal** (Section 20).

4.  **Opposition (Section 21):** any person may oppose within **three months** from the advertisement, extendable by **one month** (maximum 4 months); notice of opposition in **Form TM-O**; counterstatement within two months.

5.  **Registration (Section 23):** if no opposition or opposition fails, the mark is registered and a certificate issued. **The date of registration is the date of application.**

6.  **Duration and renewal (Section 25):** registration is for **10 years** and **renewable for 10-year periods indefinitely**; renewal is due before expiry; a lapsed mark can be **restored** with surcharge (within one year after expiry, Section 25(3)).

7.  **Appeal (Section 91):** appeals from the Registrar's orders lie to the **High Court** (earlier the IPAB, abolished in 2021).

8.  **Removal for non-use (Section 47):** a registration can be removed on the ground that the mark was **registered without a bona fide intention** to use it, and has not been used for a continuous period of **5 years and 3 months** from the date of registration. **Section 57:** rectification of the register.

**Assignment, transmission and use by others:**

- **Section 37–45:** a registered/unregistered trademark is **assignable with or without goodwill** (Section 38); an assignment creating **overlapping exclusive rights** in different places is restricted (Sections 40–42).

- **Registered users (Sections 48–55) and permitted use:** the use of a mark by a **registered user** or **permitted user** is deemed use by the proprietor (licensing with quality control).

- **Section 28:** registration gives the **exclusive right to use** the mark in relation to the goods/services and to obtain relief for infringement.

- **Section 31:** registration is **prima facie evidence** of validity; **Section 32:** protection of registration on ground of acquired distinctiveness; **Section 33:** **acquiescence** for **five years** bars the proprietor of an earlier mark from objecting to later use (unless registration was applied for in bad faith).

### 6.5 Infringement (Section 29)

Infringement is a **statutory wrong**: a person who, not being a registered proprietor or permitted user, **uses in the course of trade a mark identical or deceptively similar** to the registered mark, in a way likely to be taken as use as a trademark, infringes. Under Section 29:

- **29(1):** the mark is identical/deceptively similar **and** goods/services are those for which the mark is registered.

- **29(2):** (a) the mark is **identical** and goods are **similar**; (b) the mark is **similar** and goods are **identical**; (c) both are similar; in each case with **likelihood of confusion**.

- **29(3):** the court **presumes confusion** where the mark and goods are **both identical**.

- **29(4):** **dilution**: infringement even for **dissimilar goods** if the registered mark has a **reputation in India** and use takes unfair advantage of, or is detrimental to, the distinctive character or repute of the mark.

- **29(5):** use of the registered mark as a **trade name or part of a trade name** of the defendant's business.

- **29(6)–(9):** forms of "use" (affixing, selling, importing, using on business papers, advertising, **spoken use**); infringement by advertising that takes unfair advantage or is contrary to honest practices.

**Limits on the effect of registration (Section 30):** use of the mark that is **in accordance with honest practices** in industrial or commercial matters is not infringement, including: use of one's own name or place of business; indications of kind, quality, quantity, geographical origin (**descriptive/nominative fair use**); use to indicate the intended purpose of goods (spare parts); **exhaustion** of rights after lawful sale (Section 30(3)–(4), subject to impairment of condition of goods); and **Section 34**: **prior user** rights (continuous use from before the registration/use of the registered proprietor).

**Tests for deceptive similarity** (Indian case law):

- **Amritdhara Pharmacy v. Satya Deo Gupta (SC 1963):** the test is how the mark would strike a person of **average intelligence and imperfect recollection**; the marks are compared as wholes ("Amritdhara" vs "Lakshmandhara").

- **Corn Products Refining Co. v. Shangrila Food Products (SC 1960):** "Gluvita" vs "Glucovita"; overall structural and phonetic similarity.

- **Parle Products v. J.P. & Co. (SC 1972):** the question is whether a person **acquainted with the original** would be deceived; **Cadila Health Care v. Cadila Pharmaceuticals (SC 2001):** in pharma cases, courts apply stricter standards (nature of the goods, the consumers, the risk of harm); factors include nature of marks, degree of resemblance, nature of goods, class of purchasers, mode of purchase and surrounding circumstances.

- **Ruston & Hornsby v. Zamindara Engineering (SC 1969):** infringement is a **statutory** remedy, **passing off** is a common-law remedy, and the plaintiff in an infringement action need not prove actual deception where the marks are identical.

- **Territoriality:** **Toyota Jidosha Kabushiki Kaisha v. Prius Auto Industries (SC 2017)** reaffirmed the **territoriality principle** (reputation in India is required; a foreign mark's spillover reputation must be proved in India) and distinguished earlier cases like **N.R. Dongre v. Whirlpool** and **Milmet Oftho Industries v. Allergan (SC 2004)**, where **transborder reputation** was accepted.

- **Dilution:** **Daimler Benz v. Hybo Hindustan (Delhi HC 1994)** ("Benz" on underwear); **Tata Sons v. Manoj Dodia (Delhi HC 2011)** on well-known marks and dilution.

### 6.6 Passing off

Passing off is a **common-law tort** preserved by **Section 27(2)** (no action lies for infringement of an *unregistered* trade mark, but the right of action for passing off is unaffected). It protects **goodwill**. **Classical trinity (Reckitt & Colman v. Borden, "Jif Lemon", House of Lords 1990):** (1) **goodwill or reputation** in the plaintiff's goods/services; (2) **misrepresentation** by the defendant (leading or likely to lead the public to believe the goods are the plaintiff's); (3) **damage** (or likelihood) to the plaintiff. Indian cases: **Laxmikant V. Patel v. Chetanbhat Shah (SC 2002)** (injunction against passing off even without proof of actual damage; honest adoption is not a defence); **Cadbury India v. Neeraj Food Products (Delhi HC 2007)**; **S. Syed Mohideen v. P. Sulochana Bai (SC 2015)** (registration is not a defence to passing off by a prior user).

|                            | **Infringement**                                                            | **Passing off**                                          |
|----------------------------|-----------------------------------------------------------------------------|----------------------------------------------------------|
| Source                     | Statute (Trade Marks Act, Section 29)                                       | Common law tort (Section 27(2) preserves it)             |
| Mark                       | Registered mark                                                             | Registered or unregistered; goodwill is the basis        |
| Proof                      | Use of identical/deceptively similar mark; confusion presumed in some cases | Goodwill + misrepresentation + damage                    |
| Defence of honest adoption | Limited                                                                     | Not a defence                                            |
| Forum                      | District Court / High Court (Section 134)                                   | Same court as the suit may combine both causes of action |

### 6.7 Remedies

- **Civil (Section 135):** **injunction** (including **interim injunction, Anton Piller (search/seizure)** and **John Doe orders**), **damages or account of profits**, **delivery up** of infringing labels and marks for destruction. **Section 134:** the suit shall be instituted in a **District Court not inferior** to the court having jurisdiction, and the plaintiff can sue where **he actually and voluntarily resides, carries on business or personally works for gain** (the plaintiff-friendly jurisdiction clause). **Section 142:** relief against groundless threats.

- **Criminal (Chapter XIII):**

  - **Section 103:** applying a **false trademark** or false trade description: imprisonment **6 months to 3 years** and fine **₹50,000 to ₹2 lakh**.

  - **Section 104:** selling goods with false trade marks; **Section 105:** enhanced penalty for repeat offences; **Section 107:** falsely representing a trade mark as registered (up to **3 years** or fine, as amended).

  - **Section 115(4):** police officers of the rank of **Deputy Superintendent of Police or above** may search and seize without a warrant after obtaining the Registrar's opinion on facts involved.

- **Offences by companies:** **Section 114**; **Customs:** the Registrar may stop importation of infringing goods (Section 11 of the Customs Act read with IPR Rules 2007).

### 6.8 Recent trends in trademark law

- **Madrid Protocol (2013)** has increased international filings; **sound mark and shape mark** registrations are growing.

- **E-commerce intermediaries:** **Christian Louboutin v. Nakul Bajaj (Delhi HC 2018)** and **Amazon Seller Services v. Modicare (Delhi HC DB 2020)** discuss the "active role" of marketplaces and safe harbour under Section 79 IT Act in trademark cases.

- **Well-known marks:** Registrar's list under **Rule 124** and judicial declarations (Delhi High Court has declared many marks well known).

- **Keyword advertising and meta-tags** (*Google India v. Vishwa Bharti*; *Consim Info v. Google India*, Madras HC 2013) and **domain-name disputes** (INDRP, .IN registry).

- **Dilution of famous marks** in unrelated goods; **trade dress** disputes (shape and packaging).

- **Abolition of IPAB**: High Courts hear all trademark appeals.

Non-use removal: **5 years 3 months**. Absolute grounds: **Section 9**; relative grounds: **Section 11**. Infringement: **Section 29**; limits: **Section 30**; passing off preserved: **Section 27(2)**. Appeals: **Section 91** (High Court). Suit jurisdiction: **Section 134**. Penalties: **Sections 103–107** (6 months–3 years; ₹50,000–₹2 lakh). Certification marks: **Sections 69–78**; collective marks: **61–68**. Well-known mark: **Section 2(1)(zg)**.

## Chapter 7: Geographical Indications

### 7.1 Framework and definition

The **Geographical Indications of Goods (Registration and Protection) Act 1999** (in force **15 September 2003**; GI Rules 2002) is India's *sui generis* GI law, enacted to comply with **TRIPS Articles 22–24**. The **Geographical Indications Registry** is at **Chennai**, under the Controller General of Patents, Designs and Trade Marks (who is also the **Registrar of GIs**). The **first Indian GI** was **Darjeeling Tea** (registered 2004–05).

**Definition (Section 2(1)(e)):** an indication which identifies such goods as **agricultural goods, natural goods or manufactured goods** as originating, or manufactured in the **territory of a country, or a region or locality in that territory**, where a **given quality, reputation or other characteristic** of such goods is **essentially attributable to its geographical origin**, and in the case where such goods are **manufactured goods**, one of the activities of either the **production, processing or preparation** takes place in such territory, region or locality.

### 7.2 Essentials and registration

- **Who may apply (Section 11):** any **association of persons or producers**, or any **organisation or authority established by or under any law** representing the interests of the producers of the goods concerned. An individual cannot apply.

- **Register (Section 6):** **Part A** (registered geographical indications) and **Part B** (**authorised users**). A producer of the goods can apply to be registered as an authorised user (Section 17).

- **Procedure:** application; preliminary scrutiny; **Examination by a Consultative Group of Experts**; show-cause notice; **publication** in the **GI Journal**; opposition (within **three months**, extendable); registration.

- **Duration (Section 18):** registration is for **10 years**, **renewable indefinitely** (like trademarks).

- **Grounds of refusal (Section 9):** a GI is not registrable if its use would **deceive or confuse**; is **contrary to law**; contains **scandalous or obscene** matter; may **hurt religious susceptibilities**; would otherwise be **disentitled to protection in a court**; is a **generic name** or has ceased to be protected, or has fallen into disuse, in its country of origin; or, although literally true as to origin, **falsely represents** to the public that the goods originate elsewhere.

- **GI logo and tagline** ("Geographical Indication of India"), introduced to promote GI-tagged products.

### 7.3 Rights, infringement and remedies

- **Section 21:** registration gives authorised users the **exclusive right to the use** of the GI and the right to obtain **relief for infringement**. **Section 20:** **no action** lies for infringement of an *unregistered* GI, though passing off remains available.

- **Infringement (Section 22):** (a) use of any means in the designation or presentation of goods that **indicates or suggests** that the goods **originate in a geographical area other than the true place of origin** in a manner that **misleads** the public; (b) use that constitutes an act of **unfair competition including passing off**; (c) use of another GI **even where the true origin is indicated**, or where the GI is used in **translation** or accompanied by expressions like "**kind**", "**style**", "**imitation**" (thus India gives wine-and-spirits type protection broadly).

- **Section 24:** a right to a GI **cannot be assigned, transmitted, licensed, pledged or mortgaged**; on the death of an authorised user, the right devolves on the successor in title. *This is the major difference from trademarks.*

- **Offences:** **falsifying a GI**, **falsely applying a GI to goods** and **selling goods with false GIs** are punishable by imprisonment of **6 months to 3 years** and fine of **₹50,000 to ₹2 lakh** (offences chapter of the Act).

- **Appeals:** formerly to the IPAB; now to the **High Court** after the Tribunals Reforms Act 2021.

### 7.4 GI vs trademark vs certification mark (high-yield comparison)

| Feature       | Geographical Indication                                          | Trademark                                                |
|---------------|------------------------------------------------------------------|----------------------------------------------------------|
| Nature        | **Collective/community** right tied to a place                   | **Individual** (or collective) property of an enterprise |
| Who owns      | Association of producers/authority; users are *authorised users* | Proprietor                                               |
| Link          | **Quality or reputation attributable to geographical origin**    | Distinctiveness; no link to place needed                 |
| Transfer      | **Not assignable or licensable (Section 24)**                    | Assignable and licensable                                |
| Term          | 10 years, renewable                                              | 10 years, renewable                                      |
| Treaty basis  | TRIPS 22–24; Lisbon/Geneva Act; Paris Art. 10                    | TRIPS 15–21; Madrid; Paris                               |
| Generic names | Cannot be registered (Section 9)                                 | Cannot be registered if generic                          |

### 7.5 Case laws, examples and international aspects

- **Tea Board India v. ITC Ltd (Calcutta HC 2011):** Darjeeling tea (certification mark and GI); ITC's "Darjeeling Lounge" use at its hotels was challenged. Treat it as the standard Darjeeling GI/certification-mark dispute; confirm the exact holding in the judgment before relying on it for analysis questions.

- **Examples:** Darjeeling Tea, Basmati rice (GI registered 2016; the pending EU application and Pakistan's competing claim are recurring current-affairs points), Kancheepuram silk, Banarasi sarees, Pochampally Ikat, Mysore silk, Kashmir pashmina, Alphonso mango, Nagpur orange, Tirupati laddu, Kullu shawls, Chanderi sarees. **Rasgulla** dispute: West Bengal's *Banglar Rasogolla* (GI 2017) and Odisha's *Odisha Rasagola* (GI 2019).

- **International:** TRIPS **Art. 22** (general protection: prevent misleading use and unfair competition), **Art. 23** (**additional protection** for wines and spirits, even where the true origin is stated or used with "kind"/"type"); **Art. 24** (exceptions: generic terms, prior use); **Lisbon Agreement 1958** and **Geneva Act 2015** (appellations of origin and GIs); **Madrid Agreement 1891** (false/deceptive indications of source); **Paris Convention Art. 10**.

- **Policy:** more than **600 GIs** have been registered in India (verify latest figure); DPIIT and state governments promote GI registration along with **"One District One Product"**; **NABARD and APEDA** support GI-based agribusiness.

**Quick facts for revision (Section 7).** Act: **1999** (in force **2003**); Registry: **Chennai**; First GI: **Darjeeling tea**; Term: **10 years, renewable**; Definition: **Section 2(1)(e)**; Applicants: **Section 11** (associations of producers); Prohibited GIs: **Section 9**; Infringement: **Section 22**; No assignment: **Section 24**; TRIPS: **Arts. 22–24**; Additional protection: **wines and spirits (Art. 23)**.

## Chapter 8: Biodiversity and Traditional Knowledge

### 8.1 Why this topic exists: the IPR–biodiversity link

Biological resources and the knowledge of communities about them are valuable inputs for pharmaceuticals, agriculture and cosmetics. Before the **Convention on Biological Diversity (CBD) 1992**, genetic resources were treated as the "common heritage of mankind" and freely accessible; patents on products derived from them were granted without sharing benefits. The CBD changed this by recognising **sovereign rights of states over biological resources** (Article 15) and requiring **prior informed consent (PIC)** and **mutually agreed terms (MAT)** for access, and **fair and equitable benefit sharing**. **Biopiracy** means unauthorised commercial exploitation of biological resources or associated traditional knowledge (TK), usually through patents granted to foreign companies/institutions without consent or benefit sharing.

### 8.2 The Biological Diversity Act 2002 (BD Act)

Enacted to implement the CBD (to which India is a party since 1994). The **Biological Diversity Rules 2004** supplement the Act. The BD Act sets up a **three-tier institutional structure**:

| Tier     | Body                                                       | Location / key feature                                                                                                               |
|----------|------------------------------------------------------------|--------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------|
| National | **National Biodiversity Authority (NBA)** (Section 8)      | **Chennai**; statutory body established in 2003; regulates access and benefit sharing; advises Government                            |
| State    | **State Biodiversity Boards (SBBs)** (Section 22)          | Regulates commercial utilisation by Indian citizens/companies; advises state governments                                             |
| Local    | **Biodiversity Management Committees (BMCs)** (Section 41) | Constituted by local bodies; prepare **People's Biodiversity Registers (PBRs)** documenting local biological resources and knowledge |

**Key provisions**

- **Section 3:** **foreign nationals, non-resident Indians and foreign-controlled companies/organisations** (non-Indian participation in share capital or management) require **prior approval of the NBA** to obtain any biological resource occurring in India, or knowledge associated with it, for research, commercial utilisation, bio-survey or bio-utilisation.

- **Section 4:** prior approval of NBA for **transfer of results of research** relating to biological resources occurring in/obtained from India to any foreigner.

- **Section 6:** approval of NBA is required before applying for **any IPR** in or outside India for an invention based on biological resources obtained from India (the original Act allowed approval after acceptance of an Indian patent application but before sealing). **Section 6(3):** NBA may impose **benefit-sharing conditions** or **royalties**.

- **Section 7:** **Indian citizens/companies** must give **prior intimation to the SBB** before obtaining biological resources for **commercial utilisation** (not for local people and **vaids/hakims** who have been practising indigenous medicine, and exemptions for normally traded commodities).

- **Section 18, 21:** functions of NBA; **benefit sharing** may be **monetary or non-monetary**: joint ownership of IPR, technology transfer, research and development, capacity building, royalties.

- **Section 36:** Government to develop **national strategies for conservation**; **Section 37:** declaration of **Biodiversity Heritage Sites (BHS)** by the State Governments in consultation with local bodies; **Section 38:** notification of **threatened species**; **Section 36(5):** Central Government protects **traditional knowledge** of local people.

- **Section 41:** BMCs; **Section 55** (as originally enacted): penalties for contravention of Sections 3, 4 and 6: imprisonment up to **5 years**, or fine up to **₹10 lakh**, or both.

**Biological Diversity (Amendment) Act 2023.** Passed by Parliament in July–August 2023 and brought into force in phases. Main changes:

- **Decriminalisation:** imprisonment replaced by **monetary penalties** for most offences (penalties ranging from about ₹1 lakh up to ₹50 lakh, higher for serious breaches; verify the exact figures); adjudication by an **Adjudicating Officer**.

- **Exemptions** for **codified traditional knowledge**, **cultivated medicinal plants**, and **AYUSH practitioners** and local communities (including the earlier exemption of vaids/hakims); and **fast-track approvals** for research, patent applications and transfer of research results.

- **Prior approval for IPR** now required **before grant** (not before application) – aimed at easing patent filings (verify exact stage).

- **Benefit-sharing** now tied to **commercial utilisation**; Indian companies (including those with limited foreign shareholding) treated more favourably; foreign-owned entities remain regulated.

- Wider definition of "**access**" and addition of **"codified TK"**, and requirement for the BMCs and Local Bodies to be consulted.

### 8.3 Protection of Plant Varieties and Farmers' Rights (PPVFR) Act 2001

Enacted under **TRIPS Art. 27(3)(b)** option for a *sui generis* system, rather than joining UPOV.

- **Authority:** **Protection of Plant Varieties and Farmers' Rights Authority (PPVFRA)**, New Delhi; **Registry**; **Plant Varieties Protection Appellate Tribunal**.

- **Registrable varieties:** **new, distinct, uniform and stable (NDUS)**; **extant varieties** and **farmers' varieties** are also registrable. Varieties with **genetic use restriction technologies ("terminator")** are not registrable.

- **Term (Section 24(6)):** **15 years** from the date of registration for most crops; **18 years** for **trees and vines**; 15 years from the date of notification for extant varieties.

- **Breeders' rights:** exclusive right to produce, sell, market, distribute, import or export the variety.

- **Farmers' rights (Section 39):** a farmer may **save, use, sow, resow, exchange, share or sell** his farm produce including seed of a protected variety, but **cannot sell branded seed** of a protected variety. **Also:** farmers are not required to pay fee in proceedings; **Further:** **registration of farmers' varieties** and **recognition and reward** through the **Gene Fund**; **Section 26** and the **National Gene Fund** for **benefit sharing**; **Liability:** **compensation** if the variety fails to perform as claimed.

- **Compulsory licensing** of protected varieties is possible after 3 years if the seed isn't available to the public at a reasonable price (Section 47).

- **Related case/current affairs:** *PepsiCo India Holdings v. Registrar/PPV&FR Authority* (Delhi HC; FL-2027 potato variety – breeders' rights vs. farmers' rights, litigation 2021–2024; verify latest status). **Monsanto Technology v. Nuziveedu Seeds** (SC 2019): Bt cotton patents; Section 3(j) Patents Act and the interface with PPVFR.

### 8.4 Traditional knowledge (TK)

**Meaning.** TK is knowledge, know-how, skills and practices developed, sustained and passed from generation to generation within a community, often forming part of its cultural or spiritual identity. It includes **agricultural, medicinal (Ayurveda, Siddha, Unani, Yoga), ecological knowledge, and traditional cultural expressions (folklore)**. It is typically **collective, oral, cumulative and not novel in the patent-law sense** (it is already in the public domain, so the patent system tends to fail to protect it and invites misappropriation).

**Biopiracy and landmark cases (very high yield)**

- **Turmeric (haldi) patent:** US Patent No. 5,401,504 (1995) was granted to the **University of Mississippi Medical Center** for use of turmeric in wound healing. India's **CSIR** challenged it with **ancient Sanskrit texts and a 1953 JAMA paper**; the USPTO **revoked the patent in 1997** – the first successful challenge by a developing country to a biopiracy patent.

- **Neem patent:** European Patent No. 436,257 (granted 1994) to the **US Department of Agriculture and W.R. Grace & Co.** for a fungicidal neem-oil method; opposed in Europe by Indian and international organisations (including **Vandana Shiva, the Research Foundation for Science, Technology and Ecology and the European Greens**); **revoked by the EPO in 2000** and the revocation was upheld on appeal in **2005** for lack of inventive step (no novelty because of prior use of neem in India).

- **Basmati rice:** **RiceTec Inc.** obtained **US Patent No. 5,663,484 (1997)** claiming Basmati lines and grains; after India's challenge (via APEDA/Government) the USPTO **narrowed the claims (2001)**, and RiceTec withdrew the Basmati-named claims; this led to Basmati's **GI registration in India (2016)**.

- Others: **Hoodia** (San people, South Africa) and **Kava**, **Ayahuasca**, **Hoodia** are international examples of benefit-sharing disputes.

**India's legal measures to protect TK**

1.  **Traditional Knowledge Digital Library (TKDL)**: established in **2001** by **CSIR** and the **Department of AYUSH**, a searchable database of Indian systems of medicine (Ayurveda, Unani, Siddha, Yoga, Sowa-Rigpa). Information is classified using the **Traditional Knowledge Resource Classification (TKRC)**, modelled on the International Patent Classification, and made available in **English, French, German, Japanese and Spanish**. Access agreements allow **patent examiners** at the **EPO, USPTO, JPO, UKIPO and other offices** to search TKDL as **prior art**, preventing erroneous patents.

2.  **Patents Act (2002 Amendment):** **Section 3(p)** (TK is not an invention); **Section 10(4)(d)(ii)(A)** (disclosure of the **source and geographical origin** of biological material); **Section 25(1)(j)–(k) and 25(2)(j)–(k)** (pre-grant and post-grant opposition on non-disclosure and on anticipation by TK); **Section 64(1)(p)–(q)** (revocation).

3.  **Biological Diversity Act 2002:** **PIC/MAT**, benefit sharing (Sections 3, 6, 21), **BMCs and People's Biodiversity Registers** (to document local knowledge and prevent misappropriation), **Section 36(5)**.

4.  **GI Act 1999:** protects collective indigenous products linked to place (handlooms, handicrafts).

5.  **Copyright:** folklore as **traditional cultural expressions** is only indirectly protected (works whose authors are unknown; the Copyright Act has no *sui generis* TCE right, although Sections 22–23 deal with anonymous works).

6.  **PPVFR Act 2001:** farmers' varieties and farmers' rights.

**International developments**

- **CBD Article 8(j)** (respect and maintain TK, innovations and practices of indigenous and local communities and promote their wider application with their approval and involvement, with equitable benefit sharing) and **Nagoya Protocol 2010** (access to genetic resources and **associated TK**, benefit sharing, compliance measures).

- **WIPO IGC** (Intergovernmental Committee on IP and Genetic Resources, Traditional Knowledge and Folklore, established **2000**): led to the **WIPO Treaty on Intellectual Property, Genetic Resources and Associated Traditional Knowledge, adopted 24 May 2024**, the first WIPO treaty directly addressing TK. It requires patent applicants to **disclose the country of origin or source** of genetic resources, and the **Indigenous Peoples or local community** providing associated TK. Enters into force after ratification by **15 contracting parties** (verify current status).

- **TRIPS Council** debate on a **mandatory disclosure of origin** (proposed amendment to TRIPS Art. 29bis, supported by India, Brazil and others).

- **ITPGRFA 2001:** a **multilateral system** for access and benefit sharing for plant genetic resources for food and agriculture, and **farmers' rights**.

**Analysis points (for conceptual MCQs).** (i) TK is not patentable because it lacks **novelty** (public domain) and an identifiable inventor. (ii) The **defensive** approach (databases like TKDL preventing wrongful patents) is distinct from **positive** protection (granting rights to communities). (iii) **Disclosure of origin** is a linking mechanism between patent law and the CBD. (iv) The 2023 amendment's **exemption for codified TK** is criticised for weakening community benefit sharing.

**Quick facts for revision (Section 8).** BD Act: **2002**; NBA: **Chennai (2003)**; SBBs; BMCs; PBR; Section **3** (foreigners), **4** (transfer), **6** (IPR), **7** (Indian users), **41** (BMC). Amendment: **2023**. PPVFR Act: **2001**; term **15/18 years**; Section **39** farmers' rights. TKDL: **2001**; CSIR + AYUSH. Turmeric: US patent revoked **1997**; Neem: EPO revoked **2000** (upheld 2005); Basmati: RiceTec **1997** claims narrowed **2001**. CBD: **1992**; Nagoya: **2010**; WIPO GRATK Treaty: **2024**.

## Chapter 9: Information Technology Law: Digital Signature, E-Governance and Electronic Records

### 9.1 Background and objectives of the IT Act 2000

The **Information Technology Act 2000** (Act 21 of 2000) was enacted on **9 June 2000** (assent) and **came into force on 17 October 2000**. It is based on the **UNCITRAL Model Law on Electronic Commerce 1996** (adopted by UN General Assembly Resolution 51/162 of 30 January 1997) and was amended by the **Information Technology (Amendment) Act 2008** (in force **27 October 2009**), which introduced **electronic signatures** (technology neutral), **Section 66A–66F, 67A–67C, 69A–69B, 70A–70B**, **Section 43A**, **Section 79 (intermediary)** and the **"reasonable security practices"** regime.

**Objectives** (preamble): to provide **legal recognition for transactions carried out by electronic data interchange and other means of electronic communication** ("electronic commerce"), to **facilitate electronic filing of documents with Government agencies**, and to **amend the IPC, the Indian Evidence Act 1872, the Bankers' Books Evidence Act 1891 and the RBI Act 1934**.

**Extent and application (Section 1):** extends to the **whole of India** and, **Section 75**, applies also to any offence or contravention committed **outside India** by any person if the act involves a **computer, computer system or computer network located in India**. **Section 1(4) + First Schedule:** the Act does **not apply** to: (a) a **negotiable instrument other than a cheque**; (b) a **power of attorney**; (c) a **trust**; (d) a **will**; (e) a **contract for the sale or conveyance of immovable property**.

**Important definitions (Section 2(1)):** **access (a)**; **addressee (b)**; **adjudicating officer (c)**; **affixing electronic signature (d)**; **asymmetric crypto system (f)**; **Certifying Authority (g)**; **computer (i)**; **computer network (j)**; **computer resource (k)**; **computer system (l)**; **Controller (m)**; **cyber cafe (na)**; **cyber security (nb)**; **data (o)**; **digital signature (p)**; **Digital Signature Certificate (q)**; **electronic form (r)**; **electronic record (t)**; **electronic signature (ta)**; **electronic signature certificate (tb)**; **information (v)**; **intermediary (w)** (any person who on behalf of another receives, stores or transmits that record or provides any service with respect to it, including telecom service providers, network service providers, ISPs, web-hosting service providers, search engines, online payment sites, online-auction sites, online marketplaces and cyber cafes); **key pair (x)**; **originator (za)**; **private key (zc)**; **public key (zd)**; **secure system (ze)**; **security procedure (zf)**; **subscriber (zg)**.

### 9.2 Digital signature (Section 3) and electronic signature (Section 3A)

**Digital signature (Section 2(1)(p), Section 3):** authentication of any electronic record by a **subscriber** by means of an **electronic method or procedure** in accordance with Section 3. A digital signature is created using an **asymmetric crypto system and a hash function**: the subscriber's **private key** transforms (encrypts) the **hash (message digest)** of the record; the **public key** (certified in a **Digital Signature Certificate**) is used by the recipient to **verify** both **authenticity** (who signed) and **integrity** (record unchanged). It is *technology-specific* (PKI-based).

**Electronic signature (Section 2(1)(ta), Section 3A, inserted by the 2008 Amendment):** authentication of any electronic record by a subscriber by means of the **electronic technique specified in the Second Schedule**, and includes a digital signature. It is **technology neutral** and reflects the **UNCITRAL Model Law on Electronic Signatures 2001**. **Conditions for a reliable electronic signature (Section 3A(2)):** (a) the signature creation data are, within the context in which they are used, **linked to the signatory and to no other person**; (b) the data were, at the time of signing, **under the exclusive control of the signatory**; (c) any **alteration to the electronic signature after affixing is detectable**; and (d) any alteration to the **information authenticated after affixing is detectable**. The **Second Schedule** was amended in **2015** to include **e-KYC/Aadhaar-based e-Sign** ("Aadhaar e-Sign").

| Aspect       | Digital signature (Sec. 3)                                  | Electronic signature (Sec. 3A)                                        |
|--------------|-------------------------------------------------------------|-----------------------------------------------------------------------|
| Nature       | Technology-specific (**asymmetric crypto + hash function**) | **Technology neutral** (techniques in Second Schedule)                |
| Certificate  | **Digital Signature Certificate** by a Certifying Authority | **Electronic Signature Certificate** where applicable                 |
| Introduced   | 2000 (original Act)                                         | 2008 Amendment (the original Act's term was only "digital signature") |
| Examples     | PKI-based USB-token DSC (Class 1/2/3)                       | Aadhaar-based e-Sign, OTP-based signatures                            |
| Relationship | A **species** of electronic signature                       | The **genus** that includes digital signature                         |

**Legal recognition:** **Section 4:** where any law requires information to be in writing/typewritten/printed, the requirement is satisfied if it is in **electronic form** and accessible for subsequent reference. **Section 5:** where any law requires information or a document to be **authenticated by affixing a signature**, the requirement is satisfied by a **digital/electronic signature**. **Section 10A (2008):** **contracts formed through electronic means** are valid and enforceable (proposals, acceptance and revocation expressed in electronic form). **Sections 11–13:** attribution, acknowledgement and the **time and place of dispatch and receipt of electronic records** (dispatch occurs when the record enters a computer resource outside the control of the originator; receipt occurs when it enters the designated computer resource; the place of dispatch/receipt is the **place of business** of the originator/addressee). **Sections 14–16:** **secure electronic records and secure electronic signatures**, and the **security procedure** prescribed by the Central Government.

### 9.3 Regulation of Certifying Authorities (Chapters VI–VII)

- **Controller of Certifying Authorities (CCA) (Section 17):** appointed by the Central Government (under MeitY); functions in **Section 18** (supervising CAs, certifying public keys, laying down standards and conditions, **specifying qualifications of CAs, maintaining the Repository**, resolving conflicts of interest). The CCA operates the **Root Certifying Authority of India (RCAI)**, the **root of trust** for the Indian PKI.

- **Licence (Sections 21–24):** a CA must hold a **licence** from the Controller (application with CPS, security documents, fees); licence **valid for a prescribed period** (renewable) and **suspended or revoked** (Sections 25–26) for contravention. **Section 19:** **recognition of foreign Certifying Authorities** (by the Controller with Central Government approval).

- **Duties of Certifying Authorities (Sections 30–34):** use hardware/software and procedures **secure from intrusion and misuse**; provide a reasonable level of reliability; adhere to **security procedures**; ensure compliance with the Act and rules (**Section 31**); **display the licence** (Section 32); **surrender the licence** on suspension/revocation (Section 33); **disclose** the CPS, certificate, revocation notice, and any event materially affecting its operation (Section 34).

- **Digital Signature Certificate (Chapter VII):** **Section 35:** CA issues DSC on application (with fee and a certification practice statement) after satisfying itself that the applicant holds the private key and the corresponding public key; **Section 36:** **representations upon issuance** (the CA certifies it has complied with the Act, the subscriber holds the private key, etc.); **Section 37:** **suspension** of DSC (on request, or in public interest, for not more than 15 days without hearing); **Section 38:** **revocation** of DSC (on request, death, dissolution of company, or when a material fact is false); **Section 39:** notice of suspension or revocation.

### 9.4 Duties of subscribers (Chapter VIII, Sections 40–42)

- **Section 40:** **Generating key pair:** where any DSC the public key of which is to be listed in the certificate has been accepted by the subscriber, the subscriber must generate the key pair by applying the **security procedure**. **Section 40A (2008):** **duties of subscribers of Electronic Signature Certificate**: to comply with the prescribed requirements and exercise **due diligence and reasonable care** to retain control of the **signature creation data**.

- **Section 41:** **Acceptance of Digital Signature Certificate:** the subscriber **accepts** a DSC if he **publishes or authorises its publication** to one or more persons or in a repository, or otherwise demonstrates approval. By acceptance, the subscriber **certifies** to all who reasonably rely on it that (a) he **holds the private key** corresponding to the public key listed; (b) **all representations** made to the CA and all material relevant to the certificate are **true**; and (c) all information in the certificate that is within his knowledge is **true**.

- **Section 42:** **Control of private key:** every subscriber shall **exercise reasonable care to retain control of the private key** corresponding to the public key listed in his DSC and **take all steps to prevent its disclosure** to a person not authorised to affix his digital signature. If the private key has been **compromised**, he must **communicate the same without any delay** to the Certifying Authority in the manner specified by regulations. **He is liable until he has informed the CA.**

- **Penalties linked to subscriber/CA conduct:** **Section 71** (misrepresentation to the Controller/CA: up to **2 years** or fine up to **₹1 lakh** or both); **Section 73** (publishing a DSC with false particulars; up to 2 years or fine up to ₹1 lakh); **Section 74** (publication for fraudulent purpose: up to 2 years or fine up to ₹1 lakh).

### 9.5 Electronic records and electronic governance

**Electronic record (2(1)(t)):** data, record or data generated, image or sound stored, received or sent in an **electronic form or micro film or computer-generated micro fiche**.

- **Section 7: Retention of electronic records:** where a law requires documents/records/information to be retained, the requirement is met if the electronic record is **accessible for later reference**, retained in the **format in which it was originally generated, sent or received** (or a format that accurately represents it), and the **details of origin, destination and date/time of despatch or receipt** are retained. **Section 7A:** **audit of documents** maintained in electronic form.

- **Section 8:** **publication** of rules, regulations and notifications in the **Electronic Gazette** (legally valid, if published also in the official Gazette in the prescribed manner).

- **Section 6: Use of electronic records and electronic signatures in Government and its agencies:** the **appropriate Government may** (permissive, not mandatory) allow filing of forms/applications, issue of licences/permits, receipt/payment of money in electronic form. **Section 6A (2008):** **delivery of services** by **service providers** (e.g. through **Common Service Centres**) on behalf of the Government. **Section 9:** **no right to insist** that any Ministry/Department/Government agency must accept, issue, create, retain or preserve documents in electronic form.

- **Section 10:** power of the Central Government to make rules on digital signatures (type, manner, format, procedure, control of processes). **Section 87:** rule-making power.

- **Electronic evidence:** the **Indian Evidence Act 1872** (amended by the IT Act): Section 3 ("evidence" includes electronic records), **Section 65B** (admissibility of electronic records with a certificate). Under the **Bharatiya Sakshya Adhiniyam 2023** (in force **1 July 2024**), the corresponding provision is **Section 63** (certificate in **Part A and Part B**, including **hash value** and expert's certification). **Anvar P.V. v. P.K. Basheer (SC 2014)** held the certificate **mandatory** for secondary electronic evidence; **Shafhi Mohammad v. State of H.P. (2018)** relaxed it; **Arjun Panditrao Khotkar v. Kailash Kushanrao Gorantyal (SC 2020)** overruled Shafhi and restored the **mandatory certificate** requirement. **Section 79A IT Act:** Central Government may notify **Examiner of Electronic Evidence**.

- **E-governance in India:** **National e-Governance Plan (NeGP)** (2006), **Digital India (2015)**, **e-Courts**, **Aadhaar**, **DigiLocker**, **UMANG**, **e-Sign**. Types: **G2C, G2B, G2G, G2E**. **Justice K.S. Puttaswamy v. Union of India (2017, 9-judge bench)** recognised the **right to privacy** as a **fundamental right under Article 21**; **Puttaswamy (Aadhaar) (2018)** upheld the Aadhaar Act (with Section 57 struck down and limited private use).

- **Data protection:** **Section 43A** (compensation for failure to protect sensitive personal data) and the **IT (Reasonable Security Practices and Procedures and Sensitive Personal Data or Information) Rules 2011** are being replaced by the **Digital Personal Data Protection Act 2023** (assent 11 August 2023), which makes **consent** the principal basis for processing digital personal data, creates **Data Fiduciary / Data Principal / Data Protection Board of India**, and provides penalties up to **₹250 crore**; the **DPDP Rules 2025** (notified November 2025) bring the Act into force in phases (verify current commencement status). Section 44(2) of the DPDP Act omits Section 43A of the IT Act.

### 9.6 Intermediaries and Rules

- **Section 79 (safe harbour):** an intermediary is **not liable** for third-party information, data or communication link made available or hosted by it if (a) its function is limited to providing **access to a communication system**; (b) it does **not initiate** the transmission, **select the receiver** or **select or modify** the information; and (c) it observes **due diligence** and the Central Government's guidelines. The protection is lost if the intermediary **conspires, abets, aids or induces** the unlawful act, or on **receiving actual knowledge** (or being notified by the appropriate Government) fails to **expeditiously remove or disable access**.

- **Shreya Singhal v. Union of India (SC, 24 March 2015):** the Court **read down Section 79(3)(b) and the Intermediary Guidelines Rules 2011 (Rule 3(4))**: **"actual knowledge"** means a **court order** or notification by the **appropriate Government** relating to Article 19(2) matters. (It also struck down Section 66A and upheld Section 69A with the Blocking Rules 2009.)

- **Information Technology (Intermediary Guidelines and Digital Media Ethics Code) Rules 2021** (notified 25 February 2021; amended 2022 and 2023): due diligence, **significant social media intermediaries (SSMIs)** (**5 million registered users**) must appoint a **Chief Compliance Officer, Nodal Contact Person and Resident Grievance Officer** (resident in India), enable **first originator** identification (Rule 4(2)) for messaging services, and comply with a **three-tier grievance mechanism** for digital media/OTT platforms; **Grievance Appellate Committees** (from 2022).

- **Promotion and Regulation of Online Gaming Act 2025** (assent 22 August 2025): prohibits **online money games**, creates a regulatory authority; promotes e-sports (verify implementing rules).

**Quick facts for revision (Section 9).** IT Act: **2000** (in force **17 Oct 2000**); Amendment **2008** (in force **27 Oct 2009**). Model Law: **UNCITRAL 1996**. Digital signature: **Sections 2(1)(p), 3**; electronic signature: **3A**; legal recognition: **4, 5**; contracts: **10A**; retention: **7**; e-Gazette: **8**; e-governance: **6, 6A**; CCA: **Section 17**; CA duties: **30–34**; DSC: **35–39**; Subscriber duties: **40–42**; **First Schedule exclusions (5 items)**; Act applies abroad: **Section 75**; **Shreya Singhal (2015)**.

## Chapter 10: Cyber Crimes, Penalties and Adjudication

### 10.1 Concept and classification of cyber crime

The IT Act does not define "cyber crime" as such. Conceptually, a cyber crime is an **unlawful act where a computer or communication device is the target, the tool, or the incidental medium** of the offence. Common classifications: (i) **crimes against individuals** (cyber stalking, identity theft, online harassment, voyeurism, sextortion, defamation); (ii) **crimes against property** (hacking, data theft, ransomware, phishing, online banking fraud, software piracy); (iii) **crimes against organisations/government** (cyber terrorism, attacks on **critical information infrastructure**, unauthorised access to protected systems); (iv) **crimes against society** (obscenity, child sexual abuse material, hate speech, fake news). The Act draws a distinction between **civil contraventions** (Sections 43–47: compensation/penalty, adjudicated by an Adjudicating Officer) and **criminal offences** (Sections 65–78: prosecuted in a criminal court).

### 10.2 Civil liability: penalties and compensation (Chapter IX, Sections 43–47)

**Section 43** (penalty and compensation for damage to computer, computer system, etc.): if any person **without permission of the owner or any other person in charge** of a computer, computer system or computer network does any of the following, he is liable to pay **damages by way of compensation to the person so affected**:

- \(a\) **accesses or secures access** to the computer/system/network/resource;

- \(b\) **downloads, copies or extracts** any data, database or information, including information held or stored in any removable storage medium;

- \(c\) introduces or causes to be introduced any **computer contaminant or computer virus**;

- \(d\) **damages** or causes damage to any computer, system, network, data, database or programmes;

- \(e\) **disrupts** or causes disruption;

- \(f\) **denies or causes denial of access** to any authorised person (denial-of-service);

- \(g\) **provides any assistance** to any person to facilitate access in contravention of the Act;

- \(h\) **charges the services availed of by a person to the account of another person** by tampering or manipulating;

- \(i\) **destroys, deletes or alters** any information residing in a computer resource, or **diminishes its value or utility**, or affects it injuriously by any means;

- \(j\) **steals, conceals, destroys or alters any computer source code** used for a computer resource with an intention to cause damage.

(Since the 2008 Amendment there is **no cap** on compensation under Section 43; the ₹1 crore cap was removed.)

- **Section 43A (2008):** a **body corporate** possessing, dealing or handling **sensitive personal data or information** in a computer resource that it owns, controls or operates, which is **negligent in implementing and maintaining reasonable security practices and procedures**, is liable to pay damages by way of compensation to the person affected (the 2011 SPDI Rules prescribe standards, e.g. ISO/IEC 27001). This provision is to be **omitted by the DPDP Act 2023** upon commencement (Section 44(2) of the DPDP Act).

- **Section 44:** penalty for failure to **furnish information, return or books**: up to **₹1.5 lakh** for failure to furnish document/return/report to the Controller or CA; **₹5,000 per day** for failure to file return within the time; **₹10,000 per day** for failure to maintain books of account/records.

- **Section 45 (residuary penalty):** contravention of any rule/regulation for which no penalty is provided: compensation not exceeding **₹25,000** to the affected person (or penalty not exceeding ₹25,000).

- **Section 47:** factors the adjudicating officer considers: **amount of gain of unfair advantage** (where quantifiable), **amount of loss caused** to any person, and the **repetitive nature of the default**.

### 10.3 Adjudication machinery (Sections 46, 48, 57, 61–62)

- **Section 46:** the Central Government appoints an **Adjudicating Officer (AO)** for contraventions under Chapter IX. The AO must be of the rank of **Director in the Government of India or equivalent (State Government: Secretary of IT Department)**, and must possess **IT and legal experience**. **Section 46(1A): the AO's jurisdiction extends to claims for injury or damage not exceeding ₹5 crore**; above that, the **civil court** has jurisdiction. The AO exercises the powers of a **civil court** (summons, discovery, evidence) and proceedings are deemed judicial proceedings.

- **Section 48 (Cyber Appellate Tribunal) and Section 57:** an appeal from the AO's order lies to the **Cyber Appellate Tribunal (CAT)**, within **45 days**. The CAT was **merged into the Telecom Disputes Settlement and Appellate Tribunal (TDSAT)** by the **Finance Act 2017**; **TDSAT** now hears these appeals.

- **Section 62:** appeal to the **High Court** from the Tribunal's decision within **60 days** on any question of fact or law.

- **Section 61:** **civil court jurisdiction is barred** in matters within the AO's/CAT's powers.

- **Section 64:** recovery of penalty as **arrears of land revenue**.

- **Section 63:** **compounding of contraventions** by the Controller/AO.

- **Illustrative adjudication:** **Umashankar Sivasubramanian v. ICICI Bank (Tamil Nadu AO, 2010)**: first major adjudication under Section 46, a phishing victim was awarded compensation, finding the bank negligent in security.

| **72**        | **Breach of confidentiality and privacy** by persons with powers under the Act                                    | Up to **2 years** or fine up to ₹1 lakh or both                                                                       |
| **72A**       | **Disclosure of information in breach of lawful contract** (2008)                                                 | Up to **3 years** or fine up to **₹5 lakh** or both                                                                   |

| **84B / 84C** | **Abetment** / **attempt**                                                                                        | Punished with the offence; attempt: up to **half of the longest term**                                                |

**Other key criminal-procedure provisions**

- **Section 75:** **extra-territorial operation**: offences committed outside India by any person if the act involves a computer, system or network located in India.

- **Section 76:** **confiscation** of computer, system, floppies, CDs or any accessory in respect of which the Act has been contravened.

- **Section 77:** compensation, penalties or confiscation do **not preclude other punishments** under any other law.

- **Section 77A:** **compounding of offences** by a competent court (except offences for which punishment of **life or imprisonment exceeding 3 years** is provided, for repeat offenders, or offences affecting the **socio-economic conditions of the country** or committed against a **child below 18 or a woman**).

- **Section 77B:** offences punishable with imprisonment of **3 years and above** are **cognizable** and offences punishable with imprisonment of **3 years** are **bailable**.

- **Section 78:** investigation by a police officer **not below the rank of Inspector**.

- **Section 80:** a police officer **not below Inspector** (or other authorised officer) may **enter any public place, search and arrest without warrant** any person reasonably suspected of having committed or being about to commit an offence under the Act.

- **Section 81:** **overriding effect** of the Act, with the **proviso (2008)** that nothing in the Act restricts rights of any person to exercise any right under the **Copyright Act 1957 or the Patents Act 1970**.

- **Section 85:** offences by **companies** (liability of persons in charge).

### 10.5 Surveillance, blocking and critical infrastructure

- **Section 69:** power to **issue directions for interception, monitoring or decryption** by the Central/State Government (the competent authority) **in the interest of sovereignty or integrity of India, defence, security of the State, friendly relations with foreign states, public order, or for preventing incitement to the commission of any cognizable offence**, or for investigation of any offence. Subject to the **IT (Procedure and Safeguards for Interception, Monitoring and Decryption of Information) Rules 2009**. (Compare **Section 5(2) of the Indian Telegraph Act** and **PUCL v. Union of India (1997)**, which set safeguards for telephone tapping.)

- **Section 69A:** power to **block public access** to information on grounds similar to Article 19(2); **Blocking Rules 2009** (reviewing committee; procedure). **Upheld** in *Shreya Singhal* as it has procedural safeguards and reasons recorded in writing.

- **Section 69B:** monitoring and collection of **traffic data** to enhance **cyber security**.

- **Section 70:** declaration of any computer resource that directly or indirectly affects the facility of **Critical Information Infrastructure (CII)** as a **protected system**; **Section 70A:** the **National Nodal Agency** for CII protection (the **NCIIPC**, established 2014 under the NTRO); **Section 70B:** **Indian Computer Emergency Response Team (CERT-In)** as the national agency for **incident response**; **CERT-In Directions of 28 April 2022** require reporting of cyber incidents within **6 hours**, maintenance of logs for **180 days**, and synchronisation of clocks with NIC/NPL servers.

- **Other institutions:** **Indian Cyber Crime Coordination Centre (I4C)** under the MHA (2018), the **National Cyber Crime Reporting Portal** (cybercrime.gov.in) and helpline **1930**; **National Cyber Security Policy 2013**.

### 10.8 Recent developments and trends (cyber law)

- **BNS/BNSS/BSA 2023** (in force 1 July 2024): the new codes recognise **electronic records** as documents and evidence, e-FIR, electronic summons, audio-video recording of searches, and include **organised crime** (including cyber crime) in BNS Section 111.

- **DPDP Act 2023 and DPDP Rules 2025:** a new data-protection regime replacing Section 43A (verify commencement dates for each phase).

- **CERT-In Directions 2022:** 6-hour incident reporting, 180-day log retention.

- **IT Rules 2021 (as amended):** SSMI obligations, grievance redressal, Grievance Appellate Committees (2022), and the **2023 amendments for online gaming**, followed by the **Promotion and Regulation of Online Gaming Act 2025**.

- **Deepfakes and synthetic media:** MeitY advisories (2023–24) and proposals to require labelling of AI-generated content under the IT Rules (verify whether notified); courts have granted **personality-rights** injunctions.

- **Digital arrest scams, UPI fraud, sextortion:** growing prosecutions under Sections 66C, 66D, 66E, 67, and BNS 318/319; **I4C** and portal **1930**.

- **International:** the **Budapest Convention on Cybercrime (2001)**: India is **not** a party; the **UN Convention against Cybercrime** was adopted by the UN General Assembly in **December 2024** (signature opened in 2025; verify India's position).

- **Proposed Digital India Act** (to replace the IT Act 2000) has been discussed but, as of the last reliable information, **not enacted** (verify).

**Quick facts for revision (Section 10).** Section **43** (civil, compensation) vs **66** (criminal, "dishonestly or fraudulently"). **43A**: body corporate; **46**: AO (≤ **₹5 crore**); appeal to **TDSAT within 45 days**; **High Court within 60 days**. **65**: 3 yrs/₹2 lakh; **66**: 3 yrs/₹5 lakh; **66C/66D**: 3 yrs + ₹1 lakh; **66E**: 3 yrs/₹2 lakh; **66F**: life; **67**: 3 yrs/₹5 lakh then 5 yrs/₹10 lakh; **67A/67B**: 5 yrs/₹10 lakh then 7 yrs; **69/69A**: 7 yrs; **70**: 10 yrs; **72A**: 3 yrs/₹5 lakh. **66A struck down (2015)**. **Section 77B**: 3 years and above cognizable; exactly 3 years bailable. **Investigating officer: Inspector (Section 78)**.

### 11.5 Common traps to avoid in MCQs

1.  **Berne minimum is life + 50; India gives life + 60.** Do not mix them.

2.  **Section 3(d) (new form of known substance) is different from Section 3(e) (mere admixture)** and from **3(k) (software per se)**.

3.  **Pre-grant opposition (Section 25(1)) can be filed by *any person*; post-grant (25(2)) by a *person interested*** within 12 months of publication of grant.

4.  **Compulsory licence under Section 84 is available only after 3 years**, whereas Section 92 can be invoked at any time.

5.  **GI rights cannot be assigned or licensed (Section 24); trademarks can.** GI: association of producers; no individual applicant.

6.  **Moral rights (Section 57) survive assignment**; performer's moral rights are in **Section 38B**.

7.  **Digital signature is a type of electronic signature** under the IT Act; **Section 3A is technology neutral**.

8.  **Section 43 is civil (compensation); Section 66 is criminal** and requires dishonest or fraudulent intention.

9.  **Section 66A is not law** (struck down); **Section 69A is valid**; **Section 79(3)(b)** requires court/government order.

10. **India is not a member of** the **Rome Convention, UPOV, Budapest Convention on Cybercrime, Patent Law Treaty** (check each before answering).

11. **IPAB no longer exists:** appeals go to the High Court; **CAT is merged into TDSAT**.

12. **Biodiversity approvals:** Section 3 (foreigners/foreign-controlled entities, **NBA**), Section 7 (Indian users, **SBB** intimation). Do not swap NBA and SBB.

13. **The Information Technology Act does not apply** to negotiable instruments (other than cheques), powers of attorney, trusts, wills, and contracts for the sale/conveyance of immovable property.

14. **TRIPS vs Berne:** TRIPS incorporates Berne **except moral rights (Art. 6bis)**.
