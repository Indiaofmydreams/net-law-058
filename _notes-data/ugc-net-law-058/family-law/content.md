# Family Law: Detailed Notes for UGC NET Law (058)

## Chapter 0: Exam Pattern, Recent Trends and How to Use These Notes

**Bottom line:** Unit VII is one of ten units in Paper 2 (Law), so expect roughly 8–12 of the 100 questions from Family Law. Every question is an MCQ worth 2 marks, there is no negative marking, and Papers 1 and 2 are attempted together in one 3-hour sitting (150 questions, 300 marks). Family Law rewards three things: exact section numbers and time limits, landmark case–principle pairs, and 2023–2026 developments. Recent NET papers lean on ‘current’ law, and your revised syllabus now names live-in relationships and the Uniform Civil Code outright, so both are fair game.

These notes follow the ten syllabus topics in order. Each topic has (1) a concept core in plain paragraphs, (2) bullet lists of provisions and conditions, (3) tables for case laws and comparisons, (4) a ‘Recent trend’ box, and (5) ‘MCQ traps’. The last section is a revision toolkit: master case table, numbers and dates, and one-liners. Items marked **(verify)** are recent developments taken from news and legal-portal reports (up to October 2026); confirm the exact title or holding on the Supreme Court website or LiveLaw before relying on them for a numerical or ‘who decided’ question.

### 0.2 Where the marks usually sit (approximate, not an official breakup)

- **Highest density:** Muslim law (nikah, talaq forms, mahr, hiba, wasiyat, inheritance, wakf) and Hindu marriage and divorce (Sections 5, 7, 9–13B, 23–25 HMA).

- **Steady:** maintenance (Section 125 CrPC / Section 144 BNSS, Shah Bano line, HAMA), adoption and guardianship (HAMA, HMGA, JJ Act, Guardians and Wards Act), Hindu succession (Sections 6, 8, 14, 15 HSA).

- **Rising:** live-in relationships, UCC, foreign decrees, Article 142 and irretrievable breakdown, Waqf law, same-sex marriage (Supriyo).

- **Rarely but not never:** Parsi and Christian statutes (grounds, mutual-consent waiting periods), Jewish law, Special Marriage Act details.

### 0.3 Recent trend analysis (what the last 3–4 years suggest)

1.  **Constitutional lens on personal law.** Questions ask which Article a judgment used (Shayara Bano: Article 14 ‘manifest arbitrariness’; Hadiya and Nandakumar: Article 21 choice of partner; Supriyo: no fundamental right to marry).

2.  **Judge-made divorce law.** Article 142 divorces for irretrievable breakdown (Shilpa Sailesh, 2023) and the waiver of the 6-month cooling-off period (Amardeep Singh, 2017) are now standard framework questions.

3.  **New criminal codes.** Old IPC/CrPC/Evidence Act numbers are being replaced by BNS/BNSS/BSA numbers: bigamy BNS 82, cruelty BNS 85, dowry death BNS 80, maintenance BNSS 144, presumption of legitimacy BSA 116. Know both numbers.

4.  **Women’s property and maintenance.** Vineeta Sharma (2020), Rajnesh v Neha (2020), Mohd. Abdul Samad (2024) and stridhan rulings recur.

5.  **State-level codification.** Uttarakhand (2024) and Gujarat (2026) UCCs, Goa’s Civil Code, and the ‘can a State enact a UCC?’ debate.

6.  **Waqf.** Waqf (Amendment) Act 2025 and the Supreme Court’s interim order of 15 September 2025 are almost certain current-affairs material.

## Chapter 1: Sources and Schools of Family Law

**Core idea:** India has no single family law. Each religious community is governed by its own personal law, partly codified and partly uncodified, under the constitutional umbrella of Entry 5 of the Concurrent List (marriage and divorce; infants and minors; adoption; wills, intestacy and succession; joint family and partition; all matters for which parties were subject to personal law immediately before the Constitution). Because it is a Concurrent List entry, both Parliament and State legislatures can legislate, subject to Article 254 (repugnancy and Presidential assent). That is why a State UCC is constitutionally arguable.

### 1.1 Hindu law

**Who is a Hindu (Section 2 HMA 1955; same pattern in HSA, HAMA, HMGA).** The Acts apply to: (a) Hindus by religion in any form or development, including Virashaivas, Lingayats and followers of the Brahmo, Prarthana or Arya Samaj; (b) Buddhists, Jains and Sikhs; (c) any person domiciled in the territories to which the Act extends who is not a Muslim, Christian, Parsi or Jew by religion, unless it is proved that he would not have been governed by Hindu law or custom but for the Act. Children of Hindu parents (legitimate or illegitimate) and children of a Hindu parent brought up as Hindu, and converts or reconverts, are covered. Scheduled Tribes are excluded unless the Central Government notifies otherwise (Section 2(2)). Perumal Nadar v Ponnuswami (1970) and Yagnapurushdasji v Muldas (1966) are the usual cases on who counts as a Hindu.

**Sources of Hindu law**

**Section 4 HMA:** the Act overrides any text, rule or interpretation of Hindu law, custom or usage, and any other law in force, to the extent it is inconsistent with the Act.

**Schools (applicable to joint family, partition and some succession matters)**

- **Mitakshara** (all India except Bengal and Assam): sub-schools Benares (Uttar Pradesh, Bihar parts), Mithila (North Bihar), Maharashtra or Bombay (Vyavahara Mayukha is dominant here), Dravida or Madras (south India). Interest by birth; survivorship; coparcenary of male lineal descendants (daughters added by 2005 amendment to Section 6 HSA); inheritance by proximity of blood (nearest-in-blood rule); son cannot be disinherited of ancestral property by father’s gift.

- **Dayabhaga** (Bengal and Assam): founded on the doctrine of religious efficacy or spiritual benefit (pinda offering); right arises only on father’s death, so no birth-right; no survivorship, sons hold as tenants in common; father is absolute owner of ancestral property.

### 1.2 Muslim law

**Primary sources:** (1) the **Quran** (word of God; revealed through Prophet Muhammad; only a small fraction of verses are strictly legal); (2) **Sunnah or Ahadis** (traditions of the Prophet: Sunnat-ul-fail (acts), Sunnat-ul-qawl (sayings), Sunnat-ul-taqrir (tacit approval); Hadis may be mutawatir (continuous), mashhur (well known) or ahad (isolated)); (3) **Ijma** (consensus of jurists; Ijma of companions, of jurists, of the community); (4) **Qiyas** (analogical deduction).

**Secondary sources:** custom (urf) not opposed to the Quran; judicial precedents (Privy Council and Indian courts); legislation; equity or istihsan (juristic preference, used by Hanafis) and istislah (public interest, Malikis).

**Muslim Personal Law (Shariat) Application Act, 1937.** Section 2: in intestate succession, special property of females (inherited or contractual), marriage, dissolution (talaq, ila, zihar, lian, khula, mubarat), maintenance, dower, guardianship, gifts, trusts and trust properties, and wakfs, the rule of decision for Muslims is Muslim personal law, notwithstanding custom. The original Act excluded **adoption, wills and legacies** unless a person made a declaration under Section 3 (the Act’s 1943 amendment dealt with agricultural land). This is why a Muslim’s will and adoption are not ‘Shariat Act’ matters and why the JJ Act applies to adoption.

### 1.3 Christian, Parsi and Jewish law

- **Christians:** Indian Christian Marriage Act 1872; Indian Divorce Act 1869 (amended 2001); Indian Succession Act 1925; Guardians and Wards Act 1890; plus customary and canon-law elements. English common law doctrines applied through justice, equity and good conscience.

- **Parsis:** Parsi Marriage and Divorce Act 1936 (amended 1988); Indian Succession Act 1925 (special rules in Part V Chapter III); customs of Parsi Panchayat; marriage must be by ‘Ashirvad’ ceremony before a priest and two witnesses.

- **Jews:** largely uncodified (Cochin and Bene Israel communities); Jewish customs, Indian Succession Act, and the Special Marriage Act for civil marriage.

- **Anglo-Indians, tribals:** tribal customs retained in the Fifth and Sixth Schedules and Article 371A and similar provisions; Section 2(2) HSA and Section 2(2) HMA exclude Scheduled Tribes.

### 1.4 Constitutional framework

- **Article 13:** are personal laws ‘laws in force’? State of Bombay v Narasu Appa Mali (1952) held personal law is outside ‘law’ for Article 13; this is still criticised and was not decided in Shayara Bano (2017), where the majority tested the 1937 Act’s recognition of talaq-e-biddat under Article 14.

- **Articles 14, 15, 21:** equality, non-discrimination, personal liberty and privacy; Article 15(3) allows special provisions for women and children.

- **Article 25–26:** freedom of religion; ‘essential religious practice’ doctrine (Shirur Mutt, 1954).

- **Article 44:** UCC as a Directive Principle (see topic 10).

- **Entry 5, List III** (above).

- **Articles 141 and 142:** precedent binding; ‘complete justice’ power used for divorces.

### Memory Capsule: Chapter 1

- Dayabhaga is the school of Bengal and Assam, not Mithila.

- ‘Mitakshara’ is a commentary on Yajnavalkya Smriti; ‘Dayabhaga’ is a digest, not a commentary on one Smriti.

- The Shariat Act, 1937 does not cover wills, adoption (original text) or agricultural land (original text).

- Ijma and Qiyas are independent sources; istihsan is Hanafi, not Shafi’i.

- Fatwa is not law (Vishwa Lochan Madan v Union of India, 2014: Sharia courts and fatwas have no legal sanction).

## Chapter 2: Marriage and Dissolution of Marriage

**Core idea:** Hindu marriage is traditionally a sacrament (religious, indissoluble, for lives to come) but the Hindu Marriage Act 1955 introduced contract-like features (divorce, registration, consent). Muslim marriage is a civil contract (nikah) with religious sanctity. Christian marriage is a sacrament in church law and a statutory contract in the Indian Christian Marriage Act 1872. The Special Marriage Act 1954 offers a secular civil marriage open to all.

### 2.1 Hindu marriage (HMA 1955)

**Section 5: conditions for a valid marriage**

1.  **Monogamy:** neither party has a spouse living at the time (5(i)).

2.  **Sound mind:** at the time of marriage neither party is incapable of giving valid consent through unsoundness of mind, or though capable of consent is suffering from a mental disorder of such kind or extent as to be unfit for marriage and procreation of children, or has been subject to recurrent attacks of insanity (5(ii), as amended by the Marriage Laws (Amendment) Act 1999; earlier ‘idiot or lunatic’).

3.  **Age:** bridegroom 21, bride 18 (5(iii)). Penalty under Section 18 HMA is now dominated by the Prohibition of Child Marriage Act 2006 (PCMA).

4.  **Prohibited degrees:** not within degrees of prohibited relationship unless custom permits (5(iv)); defined in Section 3(g).

5.  **Sapinda:** not sapindas of each other unless custom permits (5(v)); sapinda relationship extends up to five degrees in the line of ascent through the father and three through the mother (Section 3(f)).

6.  Clause (vi) (guardian’s consent) was omitted in 1978.

**Section 7: ceremonies.** A Hindu marriage may be solemnised in accordance with the customary rites and ceremonies of either party. Where the rites include the *saptapadi* (seven steps taken jointly before the sacred fire), the marriage becomes complete and binding when the seventh step is taken. Case law: *Bhaurao Shankar Lokhande v State of Maharashtra* (1965: ‘solemnise’ means to celebrate with proper ceremonies; Hindu marriage needs essential rites); *Priya Bala Ghosh v Suresh Chandra Ghosh* (1971). In *Dolly Rani v Manish Kumar Chanchal* (2024) the Supreme Court held that a Hindu marriage without the requisite ceremonies is not valid merely because a certificate was issued, and a registration under Section 8 does not by itself confer validity (it is evidence of marriage).

**Section 8: registration.** State Governments may make rules for registration of Hindu marriages; failure to register does not invalidate the marriage (8(5)). *Seema v Ashwani Kumar* (2006) directed compulsory registration of marriages of all persons of all religions in India.

**Section 9–18** (remedies, covered in topic 3). **Void and voidable marriages:**

| Type                  | Section | Grounds                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                     | Who may petition and limits                                                                                                                                                                                                             |
|-----------------------|---------|-----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------|-----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------|
| Void (void ab initio) | 11      | Bigamy (5(i)); within prohibited degrees (5(iv)); sapindas (5(v))                                                                                                                                                                                                           | Either party (even a third party in some cases); no time limit; decree of nullity is declaratory                                                                                                                                        |
| Voidable              | 12      | \(a\) Respondent’s impotence leading to non-consummation; (b) breach of 5(ii) (mental disorder); (c) consent obtained by force or fraud (as to nature of the ceremony or any material fact about the respondent); (d) respondent was pregnant by another person at marriage | Only the aggrieved party. Section 12(2): fraud or force petitions within 1 year of discovery or cessation of force and no cohabitation afterward; pregnancy ground within 1 year of marriage and no marital intercourse since discovery |

Not a ground for nullity: underage marriage (it is not void under HMA; it may be voidable under PCMA at the child’s option).

**Section 16: legitimacy of children.** Children of void marriages (under Section 11) and of annulled voidable marriages are legitimate. Section 16(3): they have rights only to the property of their parents, not to any other relative’s property. *Parayankandiyal Eravath Kanapravan Kalliani Amma v K. Devi* (1996) said property rights of such children are confined to the parents’ self-acquired property; *Jinia Keotin v Kumar Sitaram Manjhi* (2003) similar; *Revanasiddappa v Mallikarjun* (2023, three-judge bench) held such children are entitled to a share in the parents’ property, self-acquired and ancestral, to the extent of the parents’ share, even in a Hindu joint family.

**Bigamy.** Section 17 HMA: punishable under IPC 494 and 495, now BNS Section 82. *Sarla Mudgal v Union of India* (1995) and *Lily Thomas v Union of India* (2000): conversion to Islam to contract a second marriage does not dissolve the first Hindu marriage; the second marriage is void and an offence. *Yamunabai Anantrao Adhav v Anantrao* (1988): a woman in a bigamous (void) marriage cannot claim maintenance under Section 125 CrPC; later, *Badshah v Urmila Badshah Godse* (2014) tempered this where the wife entered marriage in good faith (verify exact holding before using in an examination).

### 2.2 Special Marriage Act 1954 (SMA)

- **Section 4:** conditions: monogamy, sound mind (same test as HMA), age 21/18, not within prohibited degrees (unless custom permits for both).

- **Notice:** 30 days’ notice of intended marriage to the Marriage Officer (Section 5), objections within 30 days (Section 7), solemnisation within 3 months of notice (Section 14). Allahabad High Court in *Safiya Sultana v State of UP* (2021) held that publication of notice is optional and directory at the option of the parties (verify current practice).

- **Section 15–18:** registration of marriages celebrated in other forms; Section 19: the marriage under the Act of a member of an undivided family who professes the Hindu, Buddhist, Sikh or Jain religion is deemed to sever him from that family.

- **Section 21:** succession governed by Indian Succession Act 1925 (except for Hindus, Buddhists, Sikhs and Jains who are governed by Section 21A: succession under the Hindu Succession Act).

- **Divorce:** Section 27 (fault grounds), Section 28 (mutual consent: 1 year separation), Section 29 (no petition within 1 year of marriage); Section 36–37 alimony; Section 38 custody.

- **Relevance:** inter-religious and inter-caste couples; protected by Article 21 (*Lata Singh v State of UP*, 2006; *Shakti Vahini v Union of India*, 2018; *Shafin Jahan v Asokan*, 2018: right to marry a person of choice).

### 2.3 Muslim marriage (nikah)

**Nature:** a civil contract (*aqd*) with religious sanctity. **Essentials:** (1) proposal (ijab) by one party and acceptance (qubul) by the other in the same meeting; (2) free consent; (3) capacity: sound mind and puberty (presumed at 15 under Hanafi law; majority at 18 under the Indian Majority Act; marriage of a Muslim girl who has attained puberty has been upheld by some High Courts, but the interaction with PCMA 2006 is contested and the Supreme Court issued enforcement guidelines for PCMA in 2024 without settling it: verify); (4) two male Sunni witnesses (or one male and two females); Shia law does not require witnesses; (5) mahr is a consequence, not a condition of validity.

**Classification (Sunni):** valid (sahih), irregular (fasid), void (batil). Shia: valid or void only.

| Prohibition | Examples                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                        | Effect                      |
|-------------|---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------|-----------------------------|
| Absolute    | Consanguinity (mother, daughter, sister, aunt, niece); affinity (wife’s mother or daughter after consummation); fosterage (milk relations)                                                                                                                                                                                                                                      | Void                        |
| Relative    | Unlawful conjunction (two sisters at once); fifth wife (void in Sunni; temporary bar until one of the four is divorced in some views); marriage during iddat (irregular in Sunni; void in Shia); absence of witnesses (irregular, Hanafi); difference of religion (Muslim man with kitabia is irregular; Muslim woman with non-Muslim void); marriage during pilgrimage (ihram) | Irregular or void as stated |

**Muta (temporary marriage)** is recognised in Ithna Ashari Shia law only: fixed period, fixed mahr, no maintenance, no mutual inheritance, issue legitimate; iddat is two menstrual courses (about 45 days) after expiry; for widow, four months and ten days.

**Polygamy:** up to four wives at one time, with equal treatment; courts treat polygamy as not an ‘essential’ practice for Article 25 purposes in some decisions; Uttarakhand UCC Act 2024 bans it for residents (see topic 10).

**Legitimacy and presumption.** Child born at least six months after marriage and within two years (Hanafi), four years (Shafi’i) or ten months (Shia) after dissolution or death is presumed legitimate. Section 112 Indian Evidence Act (now Section 116 BSA 2023): birth during marriage or within 280 days after dissolution while the mother is unmarried is conclusive proof of legitimacy.

**Shayara Bano v Union of India (2017).** Five-judge bench held talaq-e-biddat unconstitutional by 3:2. Justices Nariman and Lalit: manifestly arbitrary, violates Article 14 and is not protected by Article 25; Justice Kurian Joseph: against the Quran, hence not part of Shariat; Chief Justice Khehar and Justice Nazeer (dissent): practice is protected personal law under Article 25 but Parliament should legislate. Earlier: *Shamim Ara v State of UP* (2002) held talaq must be for a reasonable cause, preceded by an attempt at reconciliation, and proved in court; a talaq pleaded in a written statement is not itself a valid pronouncement.

**Muslim Women (Protection of Rights on Marriage) Act 2019.** Talaq-e-biddat void and illegal (Section 3); imprisonment up to three years and fine (Section 4); offence cognizable only on information from the married woman or her blood or marriage relatives (Section 7); compoundable by the Magistrate on the woman’s application; bail only after hearing the wife; subsistence allowance (Section 5); custody of minor children (Section 6). Applies only to pronouncement of talaq-e-biddat; other forms remain valid.

**Dissolution of Muslim Marriages Act 1939, Section 2** grounds for a wife:

1.  Whereabouts of husband not known for four years.

2.  Failure of husband to maintain her for two years.

3.  Husband sentenced to imprisonment for seven years or more.

4.  Husband failed to perform marital obligations for three years without reasonable cause.

5.  Husband impotent at marriage and continuing to be so.

6.  Husband insane for two years or suffering from virulent venereal disease (leprosy was removed by the Personal Laws (Amendment) Act 2019: verify).

7.  Repudiation of marriage (option of puberty) by a girl married before 15 and who repudiates before 18, marriage not consummated.

8.  Cruelty: habitual assault or making life miserable; associating with women of evil repute; forcing to lead an immoral life; disposing of her property or preventing her from exercising legal rights; obstructing religious practice; unequal treatment of co-wives.

9.  Any other ground recognised as valid for dissolution under Muslim law.

Section 4: wife’s renunciation of Islam or conversion does not by itself dissolve her marriage (she may seek dissolution on other grounds if she was a Muslim by birth who reverts); Section 5 protects her dower rights.

### 2.5 Christian marriage

**Indian Christian Marriage Act 1872:** applies where one or both parties are Christian. **Section 4–5:** marriage solemnised by a clergyman of Church of England, Church of Scotland, a minister of another Church licensed, or a Marriage Registrar; **Section 60:** conditions (monogamy, age 21/18, consent of the father or guardian if either is a minor); **time:** between 6 a.m. and 7 p.m. (Section 9 with exceptions); notice and registration provisions. **Indian Divorce Act 1869:** Section 10 grounds after the 2001 amendment: adultery, conversion, unsound mind for two years, venereal disease, wilful refusal to consummate, desertion for two years, cruelty (introduced 2001), and parity between husband and wife; mutual consent divorce under Section 10A after two years of separation (not one). Nullity under Section 19 (impotence, prohibited degrees, prior spouse, unsoundness of mind).

### 2.6 Parsi marriage

**Parsi Marriage and Divorce Act 1936:** ‘Ashirvad’ ceremony performed by a Parsi priest in the presence of two Parsi witnesses (Section 3); prohibited degrees (Schedule I); monogamy (a bigamous Parsi marriage is void and punishable); age 21/18; registration (Section 6). **Divorce under Section 32**: wilful refusal to consummate within one year; unsound mind; pregnancy by another; adultery, bigamy, rape or unnatural offence; hurt; cruelty; desertion; imprisonment for seven years; non-compliance with decree of restitution; conversion; **Section 32B** mutual consent after one year separation. Courts are special Parsi Chief Matrimonial Courts and District Matrimonial Courts (with delegates assisting).

## Chapter 3: Matrimonial Remedies: Divorce and Theories of Divorce

**Core idea:** The Hindu Marriage Act gives six matrimonial reliefs: restitution of conjugal rights (Section 9), judicial separation (Section 10), nullity (Sections 11–12), divorce (Section 13), divorce by mutual consent (Section 13B) and ancillary reliefs (Sections 24–27: maintenance, alimony, custody, property). The philosophy has moved from the **fault** theory (guilty spouse) to **consent** and, through Article 142, towards **irretrievable breakdown**.

### 3.1 Theories of divorce

### 3.2 Restitution of conjugal rights (Section 9 HMA)

- **Conditions:** either spouse has, without reasonable excuse, withdrawn from the society of the other; the petitioner may apply to the district court for restitution; Section 9(2): the burden of proving reasonable excuse is on the person who has withdrawn.

- **Constitutionality:** *T. Sareetha v T. Venkata Subbaiah* (AP High Court, 1983) struck it down as a violation of privacy, dignity and bodily integrity under Article 21; *Harvinder Kaur v Harmander Singh Choudhry* (Delhi High Court, 1984) upheld it, saying the remedy aims at cohabitation, not sexual intercourse; *Saroj Rani v Sudarshan Kumar Chadha* (Supreme Court, 1984) upheld Section 9 as a measure to preserve marriage, not violating Articles 14 or 21.

- **Consequence:** a decree is enforced by attachment of property (Order XXI Rule 32 CPC), not by compelling cohabitation. No restitution within one year ends in a ground for divorce: Section 13(1A)(ii).

- **Pending challenge:** Ojaswa Pathak v Union of India (a writ challenging Section 9) was pending before the Supreme Court at last report (verify).

### 3.3 Judicial separation (Section 10 HMA)

Available on any of the Section 13(1) grounds and, for a wife, the Section 13(2) grounds. Effects: no obligation to cohabit, but the marital status continues; the decree can be rescinded by the court (Section 10(2)). After one year without resumption of cohabitation, either party may seek divorce under Section 13(1A)(i) (reduced from two years in 1976).

**Section 13(1A):** either party can petition for divorce if there has been (i) no resumption of cohabitation for one year or more after a decree of judicial separation, or (ii) no restitution of conjugal rights for one year or more after a decree. **Section 13(2): grounds only for wife:** (i) husband had another wife alive at the commencement of the Act (pre-1955 bigamy); (ii) husband guilty of rape, sodomy or bestiality after marriage; (iii) a decree or order of maintenance (Section 18 HAMA or Section 125 CrPC) passed against husband and no cohabitation for one year or more thereafter; (iv) repudiation: marriage solemnised before she was 15 and she repudiated it after 15 and before 18. In 2026 the Supreme Court (CJI Surya Kant and Justice Joymalya Bagchi) dismissed Jitender Singh v Union of India (W.P.(C) 460/2026), a PIL by a man seeking gender parity in Section 13(2)(iii), saying special provisions for women are constitutionally permitted (reported; verify).

### 3.5 Bars and defences (Section 23 HMA)

The court grants relief only if: (a) the petitioner is not taking advantage of his or her own wrong (the bar of ‘own wrong’); (b) the petitioner has not been accessory to or connived at the act complained of; (bb) no condonation (forgiveness with restoration to former marital status; revives if the condoned offence repeats); (c) not collusive; (d) no unnecessary or improper delay; (e) no other legal ground for refusing relief. Section 23(2): court must try to reconcile where possible. Section 14: no divorce petition within one year of marriage unless exceptional hardship or exceptional depravity. Section 15: remarriage after decree allowed once the time for appeal lapses or the appeal is dismissed (Section 28). Section 21B: trial day-to-day; Section 21A: transfer of petitions; Section 19: jurisdiction: place of marriage, where respondent resides, where parties last resided together, or where the wife resides (inserted 2003).

### 3.6 Mutual consent divorce (Section 13B HMA)

- **13B(1):** a joint petition on the ground that they have been living separately for one year or more, have not been able to live together and have mutually agreed to dissolve the marriage.

- **13B(2):** the second motion not earlier than six months and not later than eighteen months after the petition; if the petition is not withdrawn, the court after inquiry grants a decree.

- **Consent must continue until decree:** Sureshta Devi v Om Prakash (1991); the Supreme Court in Hitesh Bhatnagar v Deepa Bhatnagar (2011) reaffirmed that one party can withdraw consent before the decree; Anil Kumar Jain v Maya Jain (2009) held that only the Supreme Court, under Article 142, could waive the statutory period, a rigid view later softened by Amardeep Singh (2017).

- **Cooling-off period is directory:** *Amardeep Singh v Harveen Kaur* (2017): it can be waived by the Family Court if the statutory one-year separation is over, efforts at mediation have failed, the parties have settled alimony and custody, and waiting would only prolong agony. *Shilpa Sailesh v Varun Sreenivasan* (2023, five judges) also approved this.

- **Recent:** A Supreme Court bench in S v State of Odisha (Justices Nagarathna and Mahadevan, 28 September 2026, reported) invoked Article 142 to grant divorce by mutual consent, awarded alimony and quashed 18 pending civil and criminal cases between the parties, observing that Section 13B does not limit its power (verify; 2026 INSC 1060 appears in a report).

### 3.7 Article 142 and irretrievable breakdown

- **Shilpa Sailesh v Varun Sreenivasan (2023)**: Constitution Bench of Justices Kaul, Khanna, Oka, Vikram Nath and Maheshwari. Held: the Supreme Court may dissolve a marriage on the ground of irretrievable breakdown under Article 142(1) to do ‘complete justice’, even though it is not a statutory ground; it is not a matter of right, but a discretion to be exercised with care; it may also quash related criminal proceedings; it can waive the 6-month period; the Court laid down factors such as period of separation (a long separation, often six years or more, is relevant), efforts at reconciliation, nature of allegations, number of proceedings, the interest of children and financial security of the wife, but refused to fix a rigid rule. High Courts cannot use Article 142; family courts cannot treat irretrievable breakdown as a ground.

- **Earlier cases:** *Naveen Kohli v Neelu Kohli* (2006) recommended amendment; *Vishnu Dutt Sharma v Manju Sharma* (2009) said courts cannot add a ground; *Samar Ghosh* listed long separation as indicating irretrievable breakdown.

- **2026 applications (reported; verify):** *Neha Lal v Abhishek Kumar* (2026 INSC 73, 20 January 2026): marriage dissolved over the husband’s objection after only 65 days of cohabitation and over ten years of separation with more than forty proceedings; judgment of 2 June 2026 dissolving a marriage of two government doctors separated for fifteen years (‘stale and frozen’ marriages); 27 May 2026 (Justices Vikram Nath and Sandeep Mehta) dissolved an Army officer and doctor’s marriage with large permanent alimony.

### 3.8 Ancillary reliefs

- **Section 24:** interim maintenance and expenses of proceedings to the spouse with no independent income sufficient for support (either wife or husband); to be decided within 60 days of service.

- **Section 25:** permanent alimony and maintenance (gross sum or monthly), modifiable if circumstances change; it ceases on remarriage or unchastity (wife) or if the husband has sex outside marriage (husband).

- **Section 26:** custody, maintenance and education of minor children; welfare is paramount.

- **Section 27:** disposal of property presented at or about the time of marriage that belongs jointly to both spouses.

- *Rajnesh v Neha* (2020) mandates an affidavit of disclosure of assets and liabilities in all maintenance cases; interim maintenance from the date of application; no fixed formula (reaffirmed in 2026 decisions: verify).

- *Anamika Jain* (28 January 2026, Section 25 HMA): a working wife can still be entitled to alimony; maintenance enhanced from Rs 15,000 to Rs 30,000 per month (verify).

### Memory Capsule: Chapter 3

- Desertion period is two years under HMA (Section 13(1)(ib)); judicial separation or restitution non-compliance is one year under Section 13(1A).

- Section 13(2) grounds are for the wife only; Section 13(1) grounds are for both.

- Conversion is a ground for the other spouse to seek divorce; it does not dissolve the marriage automatically.

- Section 13B requires a joint petition; Section 14 bar (one year) applies to Section 13, and Section 13B has its own one-year separation condition.

- ‘Irretrievable breakdown’ is NOT a statutory ground under HMA; the Supreme Court uses Article 142 (not High Courts).

- Cruelty burden of proof: preponderance, not ‘beyond reasonable doubt’ (Dastane).

## Chapter 4: Changing Dimensions of Marriage: Live-in Relationships

**Core idea:** There is still no central statute on live-in relationships. Indian law protects them piecemeal: courts treat them as lawful between consenting adults (Article 21), presume marriage after long cohabitation (Section 114 Evidence Act, now Section 119 BSA), give some remedies under the Protection of Women from Domestic Violence Act 2005 (DV Act), and recognise the legitimacy of children. State UCCs (Uttarakhand 2024; Gujarat 2026) are the first statutes to make live-in registration compulsory.

### 4.1 Legal position in short

- **Not an offence:** S. Khushboo v Kanniammal (2010): living together is part of the right to life; premarital sex and live-in relationships are not criminal. Lata Singh v State of UP (2006): consenting adults may live together.

- **Presumption of marriage:** Where a man and a woman have lived together as husband and wife for a long period, the law presumes a valid marriage: Gokal Chand v Parvin Kumari (1952); Badri Prasad v Dy. Director of Consolidation (1978): a strong presumption in favour of marriage; S.P.S. Balasubramanyam v Suruttayan (1993): cohabitation as husband and wife raises a presumption of marriage and the children are legitimate; Tulsa v Durghatiya (2008); Dhannulal v Ganeshram (2015). The presumption is rebuttable and cannot be raised for a ‘walk-in, walk-out’ relationship.

- **Maintenance:** Chanmuniya v Virendra Kumar Singh Kushwaha (2010) read ‘wife’ in Section 125 CrPC widely to include a woman in a long relationship of marriage-like nature; referred a question to a larger bench (whether a strict proof of marriage is needed). Lalita Toppo v State of Jharkhand (2018) held a live-in partner entitled to maintenance under the DV Act (not necessarily under Section 125) and noted the DV Act protects ‘relationships in the nature of marriage’.

- **Domestic Violence Act 2005:** Section 2(f): ‘domestic relationship’ includes a relationship between two persons who live or have lived together in a shared household when they are related by consanguinity, marriage, **a relationship in the nature of marriage**, adoption or joint family. Reliefs: Section 18 (protection orders), 19 (residence orders), 20 (monetary relief), 21 (custody), 22 (compensation).

## Chapter 5: Recognition of Foreign Decrees on Marriage and Divorce

**Core idea:** There is no special statute on foreign matrimonial decrees. Their recognition is governed by **Section 13 of the Code of Civil Procedure 1908** (conclusiveness of foreign judgments), read with Sections 14 and 44A CPC, and interpreted for matrimonial causes by **Y. Narasimha Rao v Y. Venkata Lakshmi (1991)**. The aim is to prevent forum shopping and the ‘quickie’ divorces obtained abroad against an Indian spouse (often a wife left behind in India).

### 5.1 Statutory framework

- **Section 2(5) and 2(6) CPC:** ‘foreign court’ and ‘foreign judgment’.

- **Section 13 CPC:** a foreign judgment is conclusive as to any matter directly adjudicated between the same parties, **except** where: (a) it has not been pronounced by a court of competent jurisdiction; (b) it has not been given on the merits of the case; (c) it appears on the face of the proceedings to be founded on an incorrect view of international law or a refusal to recognise the law of India where applicable; (d) the proceedings in which it was obtained are opposed to natural justice; (e) it has been obtained by fraud; (f) it sustains a claim founded on a breach of any law in force in India. Memory aid: **C-M-I-N-F-B** (Competence, Merits, International law, Natural justice, Fraud, Breach of Indian law).

- **Section 14 CPC:** presumption that a foreign judgment was pronounced by a court of competent jurisdiction (rebuttable).

- **Section 44A CPC:** a decree of a superior court of a ‘reciprocating territory’ (notified by the Central Government; e.g., the United Kingdom, Singapore, Bangladesh, UAE, Hong Kong, New Zealand, Trinidad and Tobago, Papua New Guinea, Fiji) is executable in India as if passed by an Indian District Court. The United States is **not** a reciprocating territory, so a US decree has to be sued on as a foreign judgment.

- **Foreign Marriage Act 1969:** applies to marriages of Indian citizens abroad solemnised before a Marriage Officer, and to registration of foreign marriages; its provisions also cover validity and jurisdiction.

- **Section 19 HMA and Section 31 SMA:** Indian courts’ jurisdiction in matrimonial causes (place of marriage; where the respondent resides; where parties last resided together; where the wife resides).

### 5.2 Y. Narasimha Rao v Y. Venkata Lakshmi (1991)

The Supreme Court held that a foreign matrimonial judgment will be recognised in India only if:

1.  **Jurisdiction:** the foreign court had jurisdiction according to the law under which the parties were married (the matrimonial law of the parties); the jurisdiction assumed by the foreign court and the ground on which relief was granted must accord with the Indian matrimonial law (for Hindus, the HMA).

2.  **Grounds:** the ground for relief must be available under that law.

3.  **Submission:** the respondent must have been duly served, **voluntarily and effectively submitted** to the jurisdiction and contested on merits.

4.  **Consent:** if the decree rests on the respondent’s consent, the consent must be freely given, unequivocal and without fraud or mistake.

The Court rejected the rule that residence of the petitioner alone confers jurisdiction. Domicile and residence of the respondent matter; mere short stay of the petitioner in the foreign country is not enough. Ex parte decrees, or decrees on grounds (such as irretrievable breakdown) unknown to Indian personal law, are not recognised unless the respondent consented and submitted.

### 5.4 Applying the rule: examples

- **US ‘no-fault’ divorce decree against an Indian wife who did not appear:** not recognised (ex parte, ground not recognised, no submission).

- **Decree obtained by consent of both, after both appeared and contested:** recognised if consent is real.

- **Decree from a court in the country where both parties are domiciled and married, on grounds also valid under the personal law:** likely recognised.

- **Muslim talaq pronounced abroad:** analysed according to the personal law and the 2019 Act when talaq-e-biddat is involved.

### Memory Capsule: Chapter 5

- Section 13 CPC lists six exceptions: (a)–(f). ‘Conclusive’ is the default.

- Section 44A applies only to decrees from reciprocating territories; the US and Canada are not (UK and Singapore are).

- Narasimha Rao looks to the **respondent’s** residence and submission; the petitioner’s short stay is not enough.

- Foreign decree on a ground unknown to HMA (irretrievable breakdown) is not binding on a Hindu spouse unless the spouse submitted.

### Analysis Angle: Chapter 5

**Recent trend:** In 2026 the Supreme Court has combined non-recognition of foreign no-fault decrees with the exercise of Article 142 to give Indian divorces, so that the wife is not left without relief and parallel litigation ends. ## Chapter 6: Maintenance, Dower (Mahr) and Stridhan

**Core idea:** Maintenance is available through several overlapping routes: the personal law (Hindu Adoptions and Maintenance Act 1956; Muslim law and the 1986 Act), the matrimonial statutes (Sections 24–25 HMA and equivalents), the secular criminal-procedure provision (Section 125 CrPC, now Section 144 BNSS), and the DV Act 2005. Dower and stridhan are the wife’s own property rights.

### 6.1 Maintenance under Hindu law (HAMA 1956)

- **Interim and permanent alimony in matrimonial cases:** Section 24 (pendente lite; either spouse; decided within 60 days of service of notice); Section 25 (permanent alimony; at the time of or after the decree; may be varied). No fixed formula; the Supreme Court says the amount depends on status, needs, income and conduct (Rajnesh v Neha, 2020; Vinny Parmvir Parmar v Parmvir Parmar, 2011; Kiran Jyot Maini v Anish Pramod Patel, 2024).

- **Rajnesh v Neha (2020):** all courts must require an affidavit of assets and liabilities; interim maintenance runs from the date of application; overlapping claims under different laws must be disclosed and adjusted; execution can be done through civil and criminal means. The Supreme Court in 2026 decisions on alimony (Deepa Joshi, Ruchika Rathi, Anju Jain; reported) repeated that there is no fixed formula and that the one-time settlement (‘clean break’) approach can be used with Article 142 (verify).

### 6.2 Section 125 CrPC / Section 144 BNSS

- **Who can claim:** wife (including a divorced wife who has not remarried), legitimate or illegitimate minor children, major children with physical or mental abnormality or injury, and father or mother, **if unable to maintain themselves**, from a person with sufficient means who neglects or refuses to maintain them.

- **Ceiling:** the earlier Rs 500 limit was removed in 2001; there is no ceiling now.

- **Disqualification of wife (125(4)):** living in adultery; refusing without sufficient reason to live with husband; living separately by mutual consent.

- **Procedure:** Magistrate First Class; interim maintenance allowed; Sections 126–128 CrPC: procedure, alteration and enforcement; **BNSS:** Section 144 (maintenance), Section 145 (procedure), Section 146 (alteration), Section 147 (enforcement). The Supreme Court (Gaurav Joshi, 16 April 2026, reported) held that EMIs and loan commitments cannot defeat a maintenance obligation (verify).

- **Secular and religion-neutral:** Bai Tahira v Ali Hussain Fissalli (1979), Fuzlunbi v K. Khader Vali (1980), Shah Bano (1985), Mohd. Abdul Samad v State of Telangana (2024).

- *Vijaya Manohar Arbat v Kashirao Rajaram Sawai* (1987): a married daughter with means has a duty to maintain her parents under Section 125.

### 6.3 Maintenance under Muslim law and the 1986 and 2019 Acts

- **During marriage:** husband must maintain (nafaqa: food, clothing, lodging) even if the wife has means; the wife loses it if she is disobedient (nashiza) without lawful reason.

- **After divorce (classical law):** only during iddat (three months or three menstrual courses; until delivery if pregnant; for a widow four months and ten days).

- **Shah Bano (Mohd. Ahmed Khan v Shah Bano Begum, 1985, Constitution Bench, Chandrachud CJ):** Section 125 CrPC applies to Muslim women; mahr is not a sum payable ‘on divorce’ in lieu of maintenance; maintenance of Rs 179.20 per month. The case also urged a UCC.

- **Muslim Women (Protection of Rights on Divorce) Act 1986:** Section 3: reasonable and fair provision and maintenance within the iddat period, mahr and property; Section 4: if no relatives, the State Waqf Board pays; Section 5: option of Section 125 by joint affidavit.

- **Danial Latifi v Union of India (2001):** upheld the 1986 Act but read it down: ‘reasonable and fair provision’ means a lump sum covering her future needs and may extend beyond iddat; Section 125 remains available.

- **Iqbal Bano v State of UP (2007), Shabana Bano v Imran Khan (2010):** divorced Muslim woman can claim under Section 125 even after iddat if she has not remarried and is unable to maintain herself.

- **Mohd. Abdul Samad v State of Telangana (10 July 2024):** Justices Nagarathna and Masih: a Muslim woman, including one illegally divorced by triple talaq, can seek maintenance under Section 125 CrPC; the remedy is in addition to the 1986 Act and the 2019 Act’s subsistence allowance; Section 125 is secular and religion-neutral. The All India Muslim Personal Law Board criticised it.

- **2019 Act, Section 5:** subsistence allowance for the woman subjected to talaq-e-biddat, fixed by a Magistrate.

### 6.4 Other laws and cross-cutting rules

- **Christians:** Divorce Act 1869 Sections 36 (alimony pendente lite) and 37 (permanent alimony); Section 125 CrPC.

- **Parsis:** Parsi Act Sections 39 (pendente lite) and 40 (permanent alimony).

- **SMA:** Sections 36–37.

- **DV Act 2005:** monetary relief (Section 20), residence orders (Section 19). *Satish Chander Ahuja v Sneha Ahuja* (2020) overruled *S.R. Batra v Taruna Batra* (2006): a woman can claim residence in a house owned by in-laws if she has lived there in a domestic relationship; *Hiral P. Harsora v Kusum Narottamdas Harsora* (2016): ‘adult male’ struck from definition of respondent (women can be respondents); *Prabha Tyagi v Kamlesh Devi* (2022): actual residence at the time of filing not required.

- **Maintenance and Welfare of Parents and Senior Citizens Act 2007:** tribunal-based maintenance for parents and senior citizens.

- **Dowry Prohibition Act 1961:** Section 3 (giving or taking dowry: at least five years’ imprisonment and fine at least Rs 15,000 or the value of the dowry, whichever is more), Section 4 (demanding), Section 6 (dowry to be held in trust for the woman); BNS Section 80 (dowry death: minimum seven years); BSA Section 118 (presumption of dowry death).

### 6.5 Dower (mahr)

**Definition:** a sum of money or other property which the wife is entitled to receive from the husband in consideration of the marriage (Hedaya; Mulla). Mahmood J. in *Abdul Kadir v Salima* (1886): dower is not the price of consideration but an obligation imposed by law on the husband as a mark of respect for the wife.

| Classification             | Meaning                                                                                                                                                                               |
|----------------------------|---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------|
| Specified (mahr-i-musamma) | Fixed by agreement before, at or after marriage                                                                                                                                       |
| Proper (mahr-i-misl)       | Not specified: assessed by reference to the dower of paternal female relatives of the wife (her father’s family: sisters, aunts), her own personal qualities and her husband’s status |
| Prompt (muajjal)           | Payable on demand; wife can refuse cohabitation until it is paid; if marriage is not consummated she can refuse sexual relations                                                      |
| Deferred (muwajjal)        | Payable on dissolution of marriage by death or divorce; it is not payable earlier unless agreed                                                                                       |

**Amount:** minimum under Hanafi law is 10 dirhams; Maliki 3 dirhams; Shia and Shafi’i: no fixed minimum. No maximum. **If marriage is dissolved before consummation:** half of the specified dower is due; if none was specified, a consolatory gift (mutah). **Dower is a debt:** the wife is an unsecured creditor of the husband’s estate ranking equally with other creditors; heirs are liable only to the extent of the estate in their hands, in proportion to their shares. **Widow’s right of retention:** if she is lawfully in possession of the husband’s property, she can retain it until her dower is paid, but she does not acquire title (*Maina Bibi v Chaudhri Vakil Ahmad*, PC 1924; *Kapore Chand v Kadar Unnisa*, SC 1953). **Remission:** wife can remit (hiba-i-mahr) with free consent. **Dower and the 1986 Act:** mahr is to be paid at divorce in addition to maintenance (Section 3).

**Dower vs dowry:** dower is the wife’s legal right in Muslim law and is not prohibited; dowry is illegal under the Dowry Prohibition Act 1961.

### 6.6 Stridhan

**Meaning:** a woman’s absolute property over which she has full right of ownership and disposal. In classical Hindu law (Manu, Yajnavalkya, Narada, Katyayana) stridhan covers: gifts before the nuptial fire (adhyagni), at the bridal procession (adhyavahanika), gifts of affection (pritidatta, from in-laws), gifts at the time of leave-taking or obeisance (padavandanika), gifts from relatives (saudayika), subsequent gifts (anvadheyaka), and (in some texts) bridal price (sulka). The Mitakshara gives the widest meaning (including property by inheritance, partition, purchase, and her own earnings), the Dayabhaga narrower; the Bombay school (Vyavahara Mayukha) is in between.

- **Section 14 HSA:** any property possessed by a female Hindu, whether acquired before or after the Act, is held as full owner and not as limited owner; ‘property’ includes inheritance, devise, partition, maintenance or arrears, gift, own skill or exertion, purchase, prescription, and her stridhan. Section 14(2): property acquired by gift, will, instrument, decree or award which prescribes a restricted estate is excepted. *V. Tulasamma v Sesha Reddy* (1977): a widow’s right of maintenance converts a limited estate into an absolute estate under Section 14(1); Section 14(2) is a narrow exception. *Eramma v Verrupanna* (1966), *Bai Vajia v Thakorbhai Chelabhai* (1979): possession is a requirement.

- **Husband’s position:** he has no right over stridhan during marriage except in an emergency, and must restore it (*Pratibha Rani v Suraj Kumar*, 1985: stridhan is the wife’s sole property; refusal to return it is criminal breach of trust under Section 406 IPC, now BNS Section 316). *Rashmi Kumar v Mahesh Kumar Bhada* (1997) reaffirms. The Supreme Court in 2024–25 judgments (reported; for instance *Maya Gopinathan v Anoop S.B.*, verify) again held that stridhan is exclusively the woman’s and that the husband and in-laws hold it only in trust.

- **Section 27 HMA:** court may pass orders about property presented at or about the time of marriage which belongs jointly to both parties.

- **Succession to stridhan:** Section 15 HSA (see topic 8): the pattern depends on the source (parents’ or husband’s family).

- **Christians and Parsis:** Married Women’s Property Act 1874 protects a married woman’s separate property (not applicable to Hindus, Muslims, Buddhists, Sikhs and Jains).

### Memory Capsule: Chapter 6

- Section 125 CrPC ceiling of Rs 500 was removed in 2001.

- Section 144 BNSS corresponds to Section 125 CrPC; Section 144 CrPC is the old prohibitory order provision (do not confuse).

- Dower is a debt, not ‘consideration’ in the contractual sense; the widow’s lien is not ownership.

- Section 14(2) HSA is an exception; Section 14(1) is the general rule (Tulasamma).

- Shah Bano is a Section 125 case, and the 1986 Act partly overrode it; Danial Latifi upheld the 1986 Act as interpreted.

### Analysis Angle: Chapter 6

**Recent trend:** Court decisions in 2024–2026 emphasise (a) full disclosure of income and assets, (b) maintenance as a means of dignity (not a bargaining tool), (c) no maintenance cap under Section 144 BNSS, (d) a wife’s qualification does not by itself bar alimony, and (e) stridhan as an enforceable right.

## Chapter 7: Adoption, Guardianship and Acknowledgement

**Core idea:** Only Hindus (including Buddhists, Jains and Sikhs) have a codified adoption law with the effect of severing the child from the birth family: the Hindu Adoptions and Maintenance Act 1956 (HAMA). For everyone else, adoption is through the secular **Juvenile Justice (Care and Protection of Children) Act 2015** (JJ Act), as upheld in *Shabnam Hashmi v Union of India* (2014). Muslim law does not recognise adoption but has guardianship (wilayat), custody (hizanat) and acknowledgement (iqrar). Guardianship is governed by the Hindu Minority and Guardianship Act 1956 (HMGA) and the secular Guardians and Wards Act 1890 (GWA).

- **Cases:** *Lakshmi Kant Pandey v Union of India* (1984): guidelines for inter-country adoption and welfare of the child; CARA arose from it.

### 7.2 Adoption under the JJ Act 2015 and Adoption Regulations 2022

- **Eligibility (Section 57):** prospective adoptive parents (PAPs) must be physically fit, financially sound, mentally alert and highly motivated; a married couple must have two years of stable marital relationship and the consent of both spouses; a single woman can adopt a child of any sex; a single man cannot adopt a girl child. A person with three or more children is not eligible except for special-needs children and relatives’ children (as in CARA rules).

- **Authority:** CARA (Central Adoption Resource Authority) is a statutory body under the Ministry of Women and Child Development; the Child Care Institutions, Specialised Adoption Agencies and District Child Protection Units are the machinery; the Adoption Regulations 2022 (framed under Section 68) govern process; after the 2021 amendment, District Magistrates pass adoption orders.

- **Section 2(2):** adoption means the process through which the adopted child is permanently separated from biological parents and becomes the lawful child of the adoptive parents with all rights, privileges and responsibilities.

- **Inter-country adoption:** India ratified the 1993 Hague Convention on Inter-country Adoption in 2003; CARA is the Central Authority.

- **Shabnam Hashmi v Union of India (2014):** three-judge bench held that the right to adopt under the JJ Act is available to all, irrespective of religion, and that personal law cannot override this enabling law; the Court refused to declare adoption as a fundamental right. Muslims can therefore adopt under the JJ Act; personal law continues to apply to those who prefer it. In *Shazia Aman Khan v State of Orissa* (2024) the Supreme Court distinguished custody and guardianship from adoption in a Muslim family context (verify). Madras High Court (2025) held that the JJ Act prevails over Muslim personal law for registration of adoption of an intra-family child (reported; verify).

- **Muslim law:** no adoption (Quran 33:4–5: adopted child is not a real son); the nearest concepts are **kafala** (guardianship and sponsorship), custody and acknowledgement.

### 7.3 Guardianship under Hindu law: HMGA 1956

- **Section 4:** minor is a person under 18; ‘guardian’ includes natural guardian, testamentary guardian, guardian appointed by court and guardian by court of wards; ‘natural guardian’ is a guardian in Section 6.

- **Section 6 (natural guardians):** (a) boy or unmarried girl: the father, and after him the mother, but the custody of a minor who has not completed **five years** shall ordinarily be with the mother; (b) illegitimate boy or unmarried girl: the mother and after her the father; (c) a married girl: the husband. **No** person is entitled to act as natural guardian if he has ceased to be a Hindu or has renounced the world.

- **Gita Hariharan v Reserve Bank of India (1999):** ‘after’ in Section 6(a) means ‘in the absence of’ for the father (due to his disinterest or incapacity); the mother may act during the father’s lifetime; avoided striking down the section by constitutional reading. *Jijabai Vithalrao Gajre v Pathankhan* (1970) is the earlier view of the father’s ‘absence’. *ABC v State (NCT of Delhi)* (2015): an unwed mother can be the sole guardian of her child without disclosing the father’s name.

- **Section 7:** natural guardianship of adopted son: adoptive father, then adoptive mother.

- **Section 8: powers.** The natural guardian may do all acts necessary or reasonable and proper for the benefit of the minor or for the realisation, protection or benefit of the minor’s estate, but cannot bind the minor by a personal covenant. **Without prior permission of the court** he cannot (a) mortgage, charge, or transfer by sale, gift, exchange or otherwise any immovable property of the minor; (b) lease for more than five years or for a term extending beyond a year after the minor attains majority. Alienation in breach is **voidable** at the minor’s instance (Section 8(3)); permission is given only for necessity or evident advantage (Section 8(4)).

- **Section 9:** testamentary guardian (the father or mother can appoint by will); **Section 10:** minor cannot be guardian; **Section 11:** de facto guardian cannot deal with the minor’s property; **Section 12:** no guardian for undivided interest in joint family property managed by karta; **Section 13:** welfare of the minor is the paramount consideration; **Section 5:** overriding effect.

- **Custody cases:** *Nil Ratan Kundu v Abhijit Kundu* (2008) and *Gaurav Nagpal v Sumedha Nagpal* (2009): welfare is paramount, not legal right; *Roxann Sharma v Arun Sharma* (2015): custody of a child under five with the mother; *Vandana Shiva v Jayanta Bandhopadhyaya* (2005). Orders under Section 26 HMA, Section 38 SMA and Section 41–43 Divorce Act apply in matrimonial proceedings.

### 7.4 Guardians and Wards Act 1890 (secular)

- **Section 4(1):** minor under 18 (Indian Majority Act 1875: 21 if a guardian is appointed by the court or the court of wards takes charge).

- **Section 7:** District Court may appoint or declare a guardian; **Section 9:** jurisdiction of the court where the minor ordinarily resides.

- **Section 17:** the court must consider the welfare of the minor, with reference to age, sex and religion; character and capacity of the proposed guardian; nearness of kin; wishes of the deceased parents; and existing or previous relations with the minor or the property.

- **Section 19:** no guardian can be appointed for a minor whose father is alive and not unfit; **Section 25:** return of ward to guardian custody; **Section 29:** a guardian appointed by court cannot mortgage, charge, sell, gift or lease for more than five years without court permission; **Section 30:** alienation in breach is voidable; **Section 47:** appeals.

### 7.6 Acknowledgement (iqrar-i-nasab) and legitimacy

- **Muslim law:** acknowledgement of paternity is a declaration by a man that a child is his legitimate child. Conditions: (a) the parentage of the child must be unknown (not known to be the child of another man); (b) the age difference must be such that the acknowledger could be the father (traditionally, he must be at least 12½ years older than the acknowledged); (c) the child must not be known as the offspring of zina or of another man; (d) the child must not be the child of a woman whom the acknowledger could not have lawfully married at the time of conception; (e) if the acknowledged is an adult, he or she must confirm; (f) the acknowledger must be of sound mind and majority. Effect: legitimacy, inheritance rights, and a presumption that cannot be revoked once made (*Ashrufood Dowlah v Hyder Hossein Khan*, PC 1867; *Habibur Rahman Chowdhury v Altaf Ali Chowdhury*, PC 1921: acknowledgement of legitimacy does not apply where illegitimacy is apparent).

- **Presumption in other laws:** Section 112 Evidence Act (now Section 116 BSA 2023): birth during marriage or within 280 days after its dissolution, mother remaining unmarried, is conclusive proof of legitimacy unless non-access is proved (*Goutam Kundu v State of West Bengal*, 1993: DNA test cannot be ordered routinely; *Nandlal Wasudeo Badwaik v Lata Nandlal Badwaik*, 2014: scientific evidence prevails over presumption, in the facts; *Aparna Ajinkya Firodia v Ajinkya Arun Firodia*, 2023: DNA only in exceptional cases).

- **Hindu law:** the HAMA adoption route replaces earlier ‘dattaka’ and ‘kritrima’ forms; there is no separate concept of acknowledgement, but Section 16 HMA and presumption of marriage secure legitimacy.

### Memory Capsule: Chapter 7

- Section 6 HMGA: the **mother** is ordinarily entitled to custody of a child under **five** years, but that is not a rule of absolute entitlement.

- Section 8(2) HMGA: **the need for prior court permission covers immovable property only; dealings in movables are tested by the necessity or benefit of the minor under Section 8(1)**.

- Under HAMA datta homam is **not** essential; actual giving and taking is.

- Hindu male adopting a girl or Hindu female adopting a boy: the age gap must be at least twenty-one years.

- A single man **cannot** adopt a girl child under the JJ Act regime.

## Chapter 8: Succession and Inheritance

**Core idea:** Intestate succession is governed by the personal law of the deceased: the Hindu Succession Act 1956 (HSA) for Hindus, Buddhists, Jains and Sikhs; uncodified Muslim law (Hanafi, Shia); the Indian Succession Act 1925 (ISA) for Christians, Jews and, with special rules, Parsis. Testamentary succession is covered in topic 9.

### 8.1 Hindu Succession Act 1956

**Application (Section 2):** Hindus in the extended sense (as in HMA); not Scheduled Tribes unless notified; not Muslims, Christians, Parsis or Jews. **Overriding effect (Section 4):** HSA overrides any text or custom on matters it deals with.

**Mitakshara coparcenary and Section 6 (as amended in 2005)**

- Before 2005, only male members were coparceners. The Hindu Succession (Amendment) Act 2005 (in force from **9 September 2005**) made the daughter a coparcener **by birth** ‘in the same manner as the son’, with the same rights and liabilities (Section 6(1)).

- Section 6(3): on the death of a Hindu after the amendment, his interest in coparcenary property devolves by testamentary or intestate succession (not by survivorship), on the basis of **notional partition**; daughter gets the same share as a son; the share of a pre-deceased child goes to his or her surviving child or grandchild.

- Section 6(1) proviso and 6(5): not applicable to a partition effected before **20 December 2004** (a registered deed or a court decree).

- **Cases:** *Prakash v Phulavati* (2015): amendment prospective: only living daughters of living coparceners on 9 September 2005. *Danamma @ Suman Surpur v Amar* (2018): daughters entitled even if father died before 2005 (partly contrary). **Vineeta Sharma v Rakesh Sharma (11 August 2020, three-judge bench of Justices Arun Mishra, S. Abdul Nazeer and M.R. Shah):** the right of a daughter is by birth; it is not necessary that the father was alive on 9 September 2005; the provision is ‘retroactive’ in the sense that it applies to rights arising from birth; oral partition and unregistered family settlements cannot defeat the right unless proved by public documents or a court decree; overruled Prakash and part of Danamma. *Ganduri Koteshwaramma v Chakiri Yanadi* (2011): benefit of the amendment extended to pending partition suits before final decree. *Arunachala Gounder v Ponnusamy* (2022): a Hindu male’s self-acquired property devolves by succession; if he dies intestate without a male heir the daughter inherits, even for deaths before 1956, and the daughter’s right is superior to collaterals.

- Dayabhaga: sons do not acquire an interest by birth; coparcenary in the Mitakshara sense does not exist, so Section 6 mostly concerns Mitakshara.

- **Notional partition and Gurupad Khandappa Magdum v Hirabai (1978):** the interest of a deceased coparcener is determined by assuming a partition immediately before death; widow’s share added.

- **Repealed by 2005:** Section 4(2) (agricultural holdings), Section 23 (daughter’s limited right in dwelling house), Section 24 (certain widows’ disqualification on remarriage).

**Heirs of a Hindu male (Sections 8–13)**

- **Distribution among Class I (Section 10):** Rule 1: widow (or widows together) takes one share; Rule 2: surviving sons, daughters and the mother one share each; Rule 3: the heirs of a predeceased son or daughter take together one share; Rule 4: within each branch, the division is per stirpes.

- **Other sections:** Section 14 (female’s absolute ownership), 15–16 (female’s property), 18 (full blood preferred over half blood), 19 (per capita; tenants in common), 20 (child in womb is treated as born), 21 (simultaneous deaths: the younger is presumed to survive), 22 (preferential right to acquire the property of the intestate), 25 (murderer disqualified), 26 (descendants of a convert disqualified unless Hindus at the time of opening succession), 27 (disqualified person treated as predeceased), 28 (disease or defect is not a disqualification), 29 (escheat), 30 (testamentary succession: a Hindu may dispose of his coparcenary interest by will).

**Property of a Hindu female (Sections 15–16)**

- **15(1):** order: (a) sons, daughters (including children of predeceased children) and husband together; (b) heirs of the husband; (c) mother and father; (d) heirs of the father; (e) heirs of the mother.

- **15(2):** exceptions (the ‘source’ rule): (a) property inherited from her father or mother: if no son, daughter or their children, goes to the father’s heirs; (b) property inherited from her husband or father-in-law: if no son, daughter or their children, goes to the husband’s heirs.

- **Section 16:** rules for distribution among heirs.

- **Pending challenge:** the Supreme Court is hearing petitions challenging Sections 15 and 16 as discriminatory against women; the Court indicated it would proceed cautiously (reported; verify status).

### 8.2 Muslim law of inheritance

**Principles:** inheritance arises only on death; no birth-right; no primogeniture; no distinction between self-acquired and ancestral property; no right of representation in Sunni law (nearer in degree excludes the remoter; a grandchild whose father predeceased is excluded by the deceased’s son; the Shia school allows a form of representation); the estate first pays funeral expenses, then debts, then bequests up to one-third, and the balance is divided among heirs; the female heir gets half of the male heir’s share in the same degree.

**Sunni (Hanafi) heirs:** (1) **Sharers (Quranic heirs, ashab al-furud):** husband, wife, daughter, son’s daughter, father, true grandfather, mother, true grandmother, full sister, consanguine sister, uterine sister, uterine brother; (2) **Residuaries (asaba):** those who take the residue (son, father, brother etc., and females made residuaries by males: daughter with son); (3) **Distant kindred (dhawul arham):** everyone else related by blood, in four classes.

| Heir                      | Share                                                                                                          |
|---------------------------|----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------|
| Husband                   | 1/2 if no child or child of son; 1/4 if there is a child                                                       |
| Wife (or wives together)  | 1/4 if no child; 1/8 if there is a child                                                                       |
| Daughter                  | 1/2 if the only child; 2/3 for two or more; with son she becomes a residuary and takes half of the son’s share |
| Mother                    | 1/6 if there is a child or two or more brothers or sisters; otherwise 1/3                                      |
| Father                    | 1/6 if there is a child; otherwise a residuary                                                                 |
| Uterine brother or sister | 1/6 for one; 1/3 for two or more, shared equally                                                               |

- **Doctrine of Aul (increase):** if the total of the sharers’ fractions exceeds unity, each share is proportionately reduced. **Doctrine of Radd (return):** if there is a surplus and no residuary, the surplus returns to the sharers (except husband and wife in Hanafi law). Shia law rejects Aul and the concept of taasib; it classifies heirs in three classes (parents and children; grandparents, brothers and sisters; paternal and maternal uncles and aunts), allows representation and gives a childless widow no share in land (restricted share in immovable property).

- **Disqualifications:** homicide, difference of religion (but Caste Disabilities Removal Act 1850 and Section 28 HSA principles limit this in India: convert not excluded), illegitimacy limitations, slavery (obsolete).

- **Posthumous child:** counted as an heir if born within the maximum period of gestation (Hanafi 2 years; Shia 10 months).

- **Muslim Personal Law (Shariat) Application Act 1937:** applies Muslim law to intestate succession notwithstanding custom (agricultural land included since 1943).

### 8.3 Christians and Parsis: Indian Succession Act 1925

- **Christians (Part V, Chapter II, Sections 31–49):** where the intestate leaves a widow and lineal descendants, the widow takes **one-third** and the descendants **two-thirds** (Section 33); where there are no descendants but kindred, the widow takes **one-half** and the kindred the rest; if there are no kindred, the widow takes the whole (Section 33 and 34 pattern). A widower takes his wife’s intestate estate on a footing similar to a widow’s (Section 35: verify the exact share). Order of kindred: father, then mother and siblings, etc. (Sections 36–48). Sons and daughters take equally.

- **Parsis (Part V, Chapter III, Sections 50–56):** a widow and each child take equal shares and each parent of the intestate takes half the share of a child (Section 51), with parallel rules for a female Parsi intestate (Section 52).

- **John Vallamattom v Union of India (2003):** Section 118 ISA (restriction on a Christian’s bequest for religious or charitable purposes) struck down as violating Article 14 (the Court also urged a UCC).

- **Special Marriage Act:** Section 21 (ISA applies) and Section 21A (HSA applies when the spouses are Hindu, Buddhist, Sikh or Jain).

### 8.4 Historical and other points that MCQs love

- Hindu Law of Inheritance (Amendment) Act 1929: son’s daughter, daughter’s daughter, sister and sister’s son added as heirs.

- Hindu Women’s Rights to Property Act 1937: widow got a limited estate; replaced by HSA 1956.

- Hindu Gains of Learning Act 1930: gains of learning are separate property.

- Marumakkattayam, Aliyasantana and Nambudri law: matrilineal systems in Kerala and Karnataka; Section 17 HSA contains special rules.

- State amendments to Section 6: Andhra Pradesh (1986), Tamil Nadu (1989), Karnataka (1994), Maharashtra (1994).

### Memory Capsule: Chapter 8

- Daughter is a coparcener **by birth** (2005), not merely a Class I heir. Father need not be alive on 9 September 2005 (Vineeta Sharma).

- Class I heirs = **16** (12 originally, 4 added in 2005). Father is a **Class II** heir.

- A Hindu female’s property: Section 15(1) order begins with sons, daughters and husband.

- Muslim will limit: one-third; heirs’ consent for more.

- Aul = increase (shares reduced); Radd = return (surplus returned).

- Section 6 HSA proviso date: **20 December 2004** (partition) and **9 September 2005** (commencement).

## Chapter 9: Will, Gift and Wakf

**Core idea:** Will (testamentary succession), gift (inter vivos transfer without consideration) and wakf (permanent dedication of property for pious, religious or charitable purposes) are the three main tools of voluntary disposition. Hindu wills and gifts are governed by the Indian Succession Act 1925 (ISA) and the Transfer of Property Act 1882 (TPA); Muslim wills (wasiyat) and gifts (hiba) by uncodified Muslim law; wakf by Muslim law as modified by the Waqf Act 1995 (as amended in 2013 and 2025) and the Mussalman Wakf Validating Act 1913.

### 9.1 Will under the Indian Succession Act 1925 (applies to Hindus, Buddhists, Sikhs, Jains, Christians, Parsis)

- **Definition (Section 2(h)):** legal declaration of the intention of a testator with respect to his property which he desires to be carried into effect after his death. A will is **ambulatory** (revocable), operates only on death, and requires no consideration.

- **Capacity (Section 59):** every person of sound mind and not a minor may make a will. A married woman can bequeath property she can alienate. A deaf, dumb or blind person can make a will if he knows what he does. Will by a person who is intoxicated, ill or otherwise unable to know what he is doing is void. Will obtained by fraud, coercion or importunity is void (Section 61).

- **Execution (Section 63):** (a) testator signs or affixes his mark, or another signs in his presence and by his direction; (b) signature or mark so placed as to appear to give effect to the writing as a will; (c) attested by two or more witnesses, each of whom has seen the testator sign or has received from him a personal acknowledgement of his signature; each witness signs in the presence of the testator (not necessarily in each other’s presence). Registration is **not** mandatory.

- **Privileged wills (Sections 65–66):** soldiers employed in an expedition or actually engaged in warfare, airmen and mariners at sea may make an unwritten (nuncupative) will before two witnesses; no formal attestation.

- **Revocation:** Section 70: an unprivileged will is revoked only by another will or codicil, by a writing declaring an intention to revoke it executed like a will, or by burning, tearing or otherwise destroying it with that intention. Section 62 states that a will is liable to be revoked or altered by the maker at any time when he is competent. Codicil (Section 2(b)) is an instrument supplementing the will.

- **Probate:** Probate of a will is the copy certified under the seal of a court of competent jurisdiction; compulsory (Section 213) for wills made by Hindus, Buddhists, Sikhs and Jains in the territories of the former Presidency towns (Bombay, Calcutta and Madras) (Section 57(c)) and for Parsi and Christian wills in India. Letters of administration are for intestacy or for wills without executors (Section 218). Succession certificate for debts (Section 370 ISA, now Section 372).

- **Proof of will:** *H. Venkatachala Iyengar v B.N. Thimmajamma* (1959) set out the principles: the propounder must prove due execution and the testator’s sound disposing mind; suspicious circumstances must be explained; *Jaswant Kaur v Amrit Kaur* (1977), *Rabindra Nath Mukherjee v Panchanan Banerjee* (1995) and *Meenakshiammal v Chandrasekaran* (2005) apply it.

- **Hindu coparcener’s will:** Section 30 HSA allows a Hindu to dispose by will of his coparcenary interest (Explanation to Section 30).

- **Muslim will (wasiyat) rules:**

  - The testator must be an adult of sound mind and a Muslim of free consent; a will of a suicidal act (a will made after taking poison or injury) is void in Hanafi law.

  - **One-third rule:** a bequest cannot exceed one-third of the net estate (after funeral expenses and debts) unless the heirs consent after the testator’s death (Sunni) or at any time (Shia).

  - **No bequest to an heir** without the consent of the other heirs (Sunni; Shia allows bequest to an heir within one-third).

  - The legatee must be in existence at the testator’s death (a child in the womb who is born within six months qualifies); a legatee who causes the testator’s death is disqualified.

  - A will may be oral; no writing, no registration, no witness required; may be revoked expressly or by implication.

  - Part VI of the Indian Succession Act does not apply to Muslim wills (Section 58), and the Shariat Act 1937 originally left wills outside its scope unless a person declared under Section 3, so Muslim will law is largely uncodified (Hedaya, Fatwa-i-Alamgiri).

  - Death-bed gifts (marz-ul-maut) are treated like wills (limited to one-third and requiring consent for more).

### 9.2 Gift

**Hindu gift: TPA Sections 122–129**

| 122     | Gift is the transfer of certain existing movable or immovable property made voluntarily and without consideration by one person (the donor) to another (the donee) and accepted by or on behalf of the donee. Acceptance must be made during the lifetime of the donor and while he is still capable of giving |
| 123     | Mode: for immovable property, a **registered instrument** signed by or on behalf of the donor and **attested by at least two witnesses**; for movables, either by a registered instrument or by **delivery**                                                                                                   |
| 124     | Gift comprising existing and future property is void as to the future                                                                                                                                                                                                                                          |
| 125     | Gift to several donees where one does not accept: void as to that donee’s share                                                                                                                                                                                                                                |

| 127     | Onerous gifts: donee takes the benefit and the burden of a gift of several things                                                                                                                                                                                                                              |

| 129     | Exceptions: gifts of movables in contemplation of death (donatio mortis causa) and Muslim law rules                                                                                                                                                                                                            |

- **Acceptance:** need not be express; can be inferred from conduct (*Asokan v Lakshmikutty*, 2007). A gift is complete when accepted; possession is not essential for a registered immovable gift (*K. Balakrishnan v K. Kamalam*, 2004; *Renikuntla Rajamma v K. Sarwanamma*, 2014: a gift deed cannot be treated as valid without proof of acceptance and attestation).

- **Karta’s gifts:** a Hindu karta cannot gift ancestral immovable property except small gifts for pious purposes.

**Muslim gift (hiba)**

- **Definition:** an immediate and unconditional transfer of the ownership of the substance of a thing, without any return.

- **Essentials:** (1) **declaration (ijab)** by the donor; (2) **acceptance (qabul)** by the donee, express or implied; (3) **delivery of possession (qabza)**: actual or constructive, appropriate to the property. Registration is not required for validity (Section 129 TPA keeps Muslim gifts out of Section 123; *Mohammad Abdul Ghani v Fakhr Jahan Begam*, PC 1922; *Hafeeza Bibi v Shaikh Farid*, SC 2011: oral gift valid if all three requirements are satisfied; writing alone is not sufficient).

- **Capacity:** donor must be a Muslim, major, of sound mind, and the owner; donee may be any person, including a minor (accepted by guardian), an unborn child (invalid except if born within six months).

- **Mushaa (undivided share):** a gift of an undivided share in property capable of division is invalid under Hanafi law unless exceptions apply; the Indian courts require division except in the case of shares in a freehold property in large buildings, shares in a company, etc.. Shia law has no objection.

- **Contingent and future gifts:** a gift to take effect in future is void; a condition repugnant to absolute ownership is void though the gift itself may stand, while use of property without ownership is the separate transaction of ariyat.

- **Kinds:** *Hiba-bil-iwaz* (gift for an exchange or consideration; it is a sale in effect); *Hiba-ba-shart-ul-iwaz* (with a stipulation for return); *Sadaqah* (gift for religious merit; cannot be revoked after delivery); *Ariyat* (loan of the use of property); *Hiba of mushaa* (as above).

- **Revocation:** before delivery, the donor can withdraw; after delivery, only by a decree of court or consent of the donee; a gift becomes irrevocable on the donee’s death, the donor’s death, the donee being a relative within prohibited degrees, a spouse, on the donee’s alienation, when consideration has been given (hiba-bil-iwaz), when the gift is sadaqah, and when the subject is lost or increased.

- **Death-bed gift (marz-ul-maut):** treated as a will; valid only up to one-third of the estate unless the heirs consent.

- **Mahr in lieu:** hiba-i-mahr; wife’s remission of dower.

### 9.3 Wakf

**Definition (Waqf Act 1995, Section 3(r), as amended by the Waqf (Amendment) Act 2025):** the permanent dedication of any movable or immovable property for any purpose recognised by Muslim law as pious, religious or charitable. The 2025 amendment replaced the earlier open definition (any person could create a wakf) with a requirement that the person showing or demonstrating that he is practising Islam for at least five years and has ownership of the property may create a waqf, removed the category of ‘waqf by user’ prospectively, and provided that a waqf-alal-aulad (family waqf) shall not deny inheritance rights of female heirs and other heirs; the Supreme Court stayed the five-year practice condition until rules are framed (see below). The principal Act was also renamed (the Unified Waqf Management, Empowerment, Efficiency and Development Act 1995: verify).

**Essentials:** (1) a person competent to dedicate (a Muslim of sound mind, major and owner); (2) permanent dedication (a wakf cannot be for a limited time); (3) property capable of dedication, movable or immovable; (4) a pious, religious or charitable purpose; (5) the dedication must be unconditional and irrevocable (Hanafi law: according to Abu Yusuf’s view followed in India a wakf is complete by declaration without delivery to the mutawalli; Imam Muhammad requires delivery; Abu Hanifa treats it as revocable until a judicial decree). **Wakf vs trust:** in a wakf the ownership vests in the Almighty; the mutawalli is a manager (not owner); a wakf is irrevocable and perpetual; a trust vests ownership in the trustee.

**Types:** wakf-ul-ahli or wakf-alal-aulad (family wakf; valid under the Mussalman Wakf Validating Act 1913 if the ultimate benefit is for the poor); wakf-khairi (public wakf); wakf by user (long public religious use; abolished prospectively by the 2025 amendment, SC said the abolition is not arbitrary); wakf by declaration, instrument or by dedication.

**Management:** **Mutawalli** (Section 3(i) of the 1995 Act); **State Waqf Boards** (supervision, registration of wakfs, powers under Sections 32–33); **Central Waqf Council** (advisory body to the Central Government; Section 9); **Waqf Tribunal (Section 83)** decides disputes relating to waqf property; Section 85 bars civil court jurisdiction; Section 51 (alienation of wakf property without Board’s sanction is void); registration (Section 36); survey by a Survey Commissioner (Sections 4–6).

## Chapter 10: Uniform Civil Code (UCC)

**Core idea:** Article 44 (Directive Principle, Part IV) says: ‘The State shall endeavour to secure for the citizens a uniform civil code throughout the territory of India.’ It is non-justiciable (Article 37). The debate is between equality and gender justice (Articles 14, 15) and religious freedom and cultural pluralism (Articles 25–29). As of 2026 there is still no national UCC; Goa has a historic common code, and Uttarakhand (2024) and Gujarat (2026) have enacted State UCCs.

### 10.1 Constitutional background

- **Constituent Assembly (23 November 1948):** Draft Article 35 (now 44) was debated; Muslim members sought a proviso protecting personal laws; Dr. Ambedkar, K.M. Munshi and others supported the provision; it was adopted as a Directive Principle on the understanding that it would be enacted when society was ready.

- **Related provisions:** Article 25(2)(b) (State may regulate social welfare and reform), Article 13, Entry 5 of List III, Article 254 (a State UCC that conflicts with a Central law needs Presidential assent), Article 371A and Sixth Schedule (tribal customs).

- **Goa:** the Goa, Daman and Diu Administration Act 1962 continued the Portuguese Civil Code 1867 as the Goa Civil Code; the Supreme Court in *Jose Paulo Coutinho v Maria Luiza Valentina Pereira* (2019) described Goa as a shining example of a State with a uniform civil code, while noting that the Goa code recognises some religion-based exceptions (verify).

### 10.2 Landmark cases

| Case                                                 | Point                                                                                                                                                                                      |
|------------------------------------------------------|--------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------|
| Mohd. Ahmed Khan v Shah Bano Begum (1985)            | Chandrachud CJ: a common civil code will help national integration by removing disparate loyalties to laws having conflicting ideologies; regretted that Article 44 remained a dead letter |

### 10.3 Law Commission and policy

- **21st Law Commission (2018):** Consultation Paper on Reform of Family Law: a UCC is ‘neither necessary nor desirable at this stage’; reform personal laws to remove discrimination instead.

- **22nd Law Commission (14 June 2023):** invited public views on a UCC (renewed consultation); no final report on UCC was published at the last report (verify).

- **Prime Minister (Independence Day 2024):** called for a ‘secular civil code’ (reported).

- **Assam (2024):** the Assam Muslim Marriages and Divorces Registration Act 1935 was repealed and a compulsory registration law was enacted (verify).

### 10.4 Uttarakhand Uniform Civil Code Act 2024 (first State UCC)

- **Process:** committee headed by Justice (retd.) Ranjana Prakash Desai; Bill passed by the State Assembly in **February 2024**; Presidential assent in **March 2024**; brought into force in **January 2025** after rules were notified (reported; verify exact dates).

- **Scope:** applies to residents of Uttarakhand (also residents living outside the State) and excludes Scheduled Tribes and persons whose customary rights are protected by the Constitution; covers marriage and divorce, succession (wills and intestate), live-in relationships, and miscellaneous provisions.

- **Key features:** equal marriage age (21 for men, 18 for women); monogamy (bigamy prohibited); **mandatory registration** of marriages and divorces; equal grounds of divorce for men and women; prohibition of practices such as triple talaq, halala and iddat; marriages and divorces by religious customs allowed as to ceremonies but not to substance; equal inheritance rights for sons and daughters; children of void or voidable marriages and live-in relationships treated as legitimate; adopted and biological children treated alike; **live-in relationships to be registered** and terminated with notice.

- **Challenge:** petitions have been filed in the High Court against the live-in registration and other provisions (verify status).

### 10.5 Gujarat Uniform Civil Code 2026 (second State UCC)

- **Process:** committee chaired by Justice (retd.) Ranjana Prakash Desai; Bill introduced on **24 March 2026**, passed the same day by the Gujarat Assembly after about seven and a half hours’ debate; Presidential assent reported in early **October 2026**; the State must notify rules before it comes into force (reported; verify).

- **Scope:** Marriage and divorce, succession and live-in relationships; applies to residents of Gujarat and to Gujarat residents living outside the State; excludes Scheduled Tribes and groups whose customary rights are protected by the Constitution.

- **Reported features:** registration of live-in relationships and of their termination; prohibition of bigamy (a marriage is valid only if neither party has a living spouse); all separations must be through civil courts (reportedly ending extra-judicial or customary divorce); compulsory registration of marriages; common succession framework.

- **Opposition:** concerns that it is politically driven and may infringe religious freedom and fundamental rights.

### 10.6 Arguments

| For                                                                                                                                                      | Against                                                                                                                                                                                                                                      |
|----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------|----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------|
| Gender justice and equality; national integration; removes discriminatory customs (triple talaq, unequal inheritance); modernisation; Article 44 mandate | Threatens religious freedom (Article 25) and minority cultural rights (Article 29); diversity of customs (tribal, Christian, Sikh); the 2018 Law Commission view; reform within each community preferable; risk of majoritarian codification |
| States are laboratories: Entry 5, List III permits State action                                                                                          | A ‘uniform’ code ‘throughout the territory of India’ implies a national law; State UCCs create patchwork and Article 254 issues                                                                                                              |

### 10.7 Related constitutional doctrines

- **Essential religious practices test (Shirur Mutt, 1954)** and its use in triple talaq (Shayara Bano), Sabarimala (2018, Indian Young Lawyers Association v State of Kerala) and Hijab cases.

- **Narasu Appa Mali (1952)** and the question whether personal laws are subject to fundamental rights (unsettled).

- **Reform by consent vs imposition:** the UCC can be enacted by Parliament under Entry 5, List III or Article 44 as a directive; no deadline.

### Memory Capsule: Chapter 10

- Article 44 is in **Part IV** (DPSP), not Part III; it is not enforceable.

- Uttarakhand was the first **State** to enact a UCC post-Independence; Goa’s code is a continuation of a pre-Independence Portuguese law.

- The Law Commission (2018) said UCC was **neither necessary nor desirable at this stage**; the 22nd Commission started fresh consultation in 2023.

- State UCCs exclude **Scheduled Tribes**.

- Shah Bano, Sarla Mudgal, John Vallamattom each urged the government to enact a UCC; none ordered it.

### Analysis Angle: Chapter 10

**Recent trend:** Gujarat’s 2026 Act and assent, Uttarakhand’s 2025 implementation, rule-making and High Court challenges, and the Law Commission’s silence make this a highly probable MCQ area: ‘Which State first enacted a UCC after Independence?’ (Uttarakhand), ‘Which was the second?’ (Gujarat), ‘Which State has a Portuguese-origin code?’ (Goa), ‘Who chaired the committees?’ (Justice Ranjana Prakash Desai), ‘Which Article?’ (44).

### 11.5 Frequent confusions (MCQ traps in one place)

- **Void vs voidable:** HMA Sections 11 (void) and 12 (voidable); child is legitimate in both (Section 16).

- **Judicial separation vs divorce:** marriage subsists after judicial separation.

- **Mutual consent vs irretrievable breakdown:** 13B is statutory; irretrievable breakdown is Article 142 only.

- **Dower vs dowry; mahr vs mutah (gift on divorce) vs muta (temporary marriage).**

- **Hizanat (custody) vs wilayat (guardianship):** mother has hizanat; father has wilayat.

- **Guardian and Wards Act (secular) vs HMGA (Hindu).**

- **Hiba (gift) vs wasiyat (will):** hiba effective immediately with delivery; wasiyat after death.

- **Aul vs Radd:** Aul reduces shares; Radd returns the surplus.

- **Wakf vs trust:** ownership in God; mutawalli is not owner.

- **Section 125 CrPC vs Section 144 BNSS:** same subject; do not confuse with Section 144 CrPC (prohibitory orders).

- **Natural guardian of a married Hindu girl:** her husband.
