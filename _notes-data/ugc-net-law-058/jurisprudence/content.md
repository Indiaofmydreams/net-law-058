# Jurisprudence: Detailed Notes for UGC NET Law (058)

## Chapter 0: How to Use These Notes and What the Exam Looks Like

UGC NET Law (subject code 58) is conducted by the NTA in computer-based mode. Paper 1 (teaching and research aptitude) has 50 questions and Paper 2 (Law) has 100 questions, every question carries 2 marks, and there is no negative marking. Both papers are taken in one continuous three-hour sitting. The Paper 2 syllabus has ten units and the questions are spread fairly evenly across them, so Jurisprudence (Unit I) usually contributes roughly ten questions, which is about 20 marks out of 200. Because there is no negative marking, you should never leave a question blank.

### 0.1 Question formats

The question formats that appear repeatedly in Law Paper 2 are:

- **Direct memory questions:** who wrote which book, who propounded which theory, which case laid down which principle, which Article says what.

- **Statement-based questions:** two or more statements are given and you must choose the correct combination (Statement I true and II false, and so on). These test whether you know the nuance, not just the name.

- **Match the list (List I with List II):** jurist with theory, jurist with book, case with principle, concept with definition. This is the single most common format in Jurisprudence.

- **Assertion and Reason:** you must decide whether the reason correctly explains the assertion.

- **Chronological or sequence questions:** for example, arranging schools or thinkers in order of appearance.

- **'Which is NOT correct' questions:** these reward careful reading of exceptions and common mix-ups, such as confusing Austin with Bentham or Hart with Kelsen.

### 0.2 Observed trends

- Questions are increasingly *applied*: the examiner gives a situation or a proposition and asks which jurist or school it matches, instead of asking only for names.

- Indian Supreme Court and High Court judgments on **legal personality** (rivers, animals, idols, religious books), **privacy**, **constitutional morality** and **liability** appear far more often than they used to.

- Modern and contemporary thinkers (Rawls, Dworkin, Amartya Sen, Martha Nussbaum, Thomas Pogge, Foucault, Derrida, Lyotard) now get regular representation, alongside the classical names (Austin, Salmond, Savigny, Pound).

- Topics 8, 9 and 10 (Law, poverty and development; Global justice; Modernism and post-modernism) are comparatively newer in the syllabus and are very predictable: a handful of thinkers and books decide almost every question.

- The Hart–Devlin, Hart–Fuller and Hart–Dworkin debates, the distinction between 'is' and 'ought', and the contrast between 'rights' (Hohfeld, Salmond) and 'duties' keep recurring.

### 0.3 How to study this unit

build a two-column memory sheet for every school (thinker, book, core idea, one-line criticism), memorise the 15 to 20 cases flagged as high-yield in each topic, and revise the 'common confusions' boxes at the end of each topic the day before the exam.

## Chapter 1: Nature and Sources of Law

### 1.1 Meaning of Jurisprudence

The word *jurisprudence* comes from the Latin *juris prudentia*, meaning 'knowledge of law' or 'skill in law'. Jurisprudence is the study of the fundamental principles on which law rests, as distinct from the study of a particular statute or branch of law. It asks questions such as what law is, why it binds, how it differs from morality, and what ends it should serve.

**Definitions of jurisprudence (frequently asked):**

- **Ulpian (Roman jurist):** knowledge of things divine and human, the science of the just and the unjust.

- **John Austin:** the philosophy of positive law.

- **Salmond:** the science of the first principles of the civil law.

- **Holland:** the formal science of positive law.

- **Roscoe Pound:** the science of law, using the word 'law' in the juridical sense of the body of principles recognised or enforced by public and regular tribunals.

- **Julius Stone:** the lawyer's extroversion, that is, the lawyer's examination of the precepts, ideals and techniques of the law in the light of present-day knowledge from other disciplines.

- **Gray:** the science of law, that is, the statement and systematic arrangement of the rules followed by courts and the principles involved in those rules.

- **Dias:** the study of legal theory in the widest sense (he preferred a wide, multi-purpose approach).

**Divisions of jurisprudence:**

- **Bentham** divided it into **expositorial** (what the law is) and **censorial** (what the law ought to be). Bentham also coined the term 'international law'.

- **Austin** divided it into **general** (principles common to all legal systems) and **particular** (a given system of law). He treated only the general, analytical part as true jurisprudence and excluded 'legislation' (what ought to be) from its province.

- **Salmond** speaks of **legal theory** in the widest sense and recognises three main approaches: **analytical**, **historical** and **ethical**.

- **Salmond** also distinguishes jurisprudence in the *wider* sense (the whole of legal theory) from jurisprudence in the *narrow* sense (the science of the first principles of civil law).

**Method of jurisprudence (modern view):** analytical (breaking concepts into parts), historical (tracing origin and growth), sociological (law in society), philosophical or ethical (justice and values), and comparative (across legal systems).

### 1.2 Nature of Law: Definitions and Characteristics

There is no single universally accepted definition of law. Each definition reflects the school of the thinker. Learn them as pairs: *jurist and one key phrase*.

| Jurist            | Definition or key idea                                                                                                     |
|-------------------|----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------|
| Austin            | Law is the command of the sovereign backed by sanction                                                                     |
| Blackstone        | A rule of civil conduct prescribed by the supreme power in a state, commanding what is right and prohibiting what is wrong |
| Salmond           | The body of principles recognised and applied by the State in the administration of justice                                |
| Holmes            | The prophecies of what the courts will do in fact, and nothing more pretentious                                            |
| John Chipman Gray | What the judges declare, not the source of law but law itself                                                              |
| Savigny           | An expression of the common consciousness of the people (*Volksgeist*)                                                     |
| Ihering           | The form of the guarantee of the conditions of life of society, assured by the State's power of constraint                 |
| Kelsen            | A primary norm which stipulates a sanction; a coercive order, a hierarchy of norms                                         |
| Hart              | The union of primary rules of obligation and secondary rules (recognition, change, adjudication)                           |
| Dworkin           | An interpretive concept: law as integrity, comprising rules and principles                                                 |
| Fuller            | The enterprise of subjecting human conduct to the governance of rules (inner morality of law)                              |
| Roscoe Pound      | A social institution to satisfy social wants, a tool of social engineering                                                 |
| Cicero            | Highest reason implanted in nature (the natural law idea)                                                                  |
| Aquinas           | An ordinance of reason for the common good, made by one who has care of the community, and promulgated                     |
| Henry Maine       | Law develops from status to contract (a historical generalisation)                                                         |

**Essential characteristics of law (as most schools accept them):**

- **Normative:** it prescribes conduct, it does not merely describe it.

- **General:** it applies to classes of persons and acts, not to a single named individual.

- **Prospective:** it generally operates for the future (retrospective penal laws are restricted by Article 20(1) of the Indian Constitution).

- **Public and certain:** it must be promulgated and knowable.

- **Coercive or backed by sanction:** this is emphasised by positivists and denied as the sole feature by Hart, who stresses the *internal point of view* of those who accept the rules.

- **Institutional:** it is made, applied and enforced through institutions such as legislatures, courts and administrative bodies.

**Fuller's eight desiderata (inner morality of law)** from *The Morality of Law* (1964): generality, promulgation, non-retroactivity, clarity, non-contradiction, possibility of compliance, constancy through time, and congruence between declared rule and official action. Fuller's story of 'King Rex' illustrates the eight ways of failing to make law. Remember: Fuller says a system that fails *entirely* on these cannot be called law.

**Functions of law:** social control; dispute settlement; protection of rights; social change and engineering (Pound, and in India the Constitution's transformative aims); allocation of power and authority; maintenance of order and security; and providing a framework for economic and social life.

**Classification of law (common textbook divisions):**

- **Substantive and procedural law:** substantive law defines rights and duties (for example the Indian Penal Code or its successor), procedural law prescribes how they are enforced (for example the Code of Criminal Procedure). Salmond calls the law of procedure the law of the *remedy*.

- **Public and private law:** public law governs the relationship between the State and individuals (constitutional, administrative, criminal), private law governs relations among individuals (contract, tort, property, family).

- **Municipal and international law:** Austin said international law is merely 'positive international morality' because it lacks a sovereign command, while Salmond, Oppenheim and the modern view regard it as true law.

- **Civil and criminal law, and common law and equity.**

- **Salmond's classification of law:** imperative law, physical or scientific law, natural or moral law, conventional law, customary law, practical or technical law, and international law.

### 1.3 Austin's Command Theory (The Core of Analytical Positivism)

John Austin (1790 to 1859), the first Professor of Jurisprudence at the University of London, delivered the lectures later published as **The Province of Jurisprudence Determined (1832)**. His programme was to separate *law as it is* (positive law) from *law as it ought to be* (morality, ethics).

**Austin's classification of laws:**

- **Laws properly so called:** (a) divine laws (set by God to men), and (b) human laws (set by men to men).

- **Human laws** divide into *positive law* (set by political superiors to political inferiors, and by persons acting in pursuance of legal rights) and *positive morality* (set by men who are not political superiors, such as rules of clubs, and also the 'law of fashion' and international law).

- **Laws improperly so called:** laws by analogy (rules of fashion, honour, international law) and laws by metaphor (laws of nature, like the law of gravity).

**Elements of law in Austin's theory:**

- **Command:** the expression of a wish by a determinate person that something be done, with the power and purpose to inflict evil (sanction) if the wish is not complied with.

- **Duty:** the obligation to obey the command, existing because of the likelihood of the sanction.

- **Sanction:** the evil that will be inflicted if the command is disobeyed.

- **Sovereign:** a determinate human superior who receives habitual obedience from the bulk of the society and who does not himself habitually obey any other like superior. Sovereignty is, in Austin's view, **indivisible, illimitable** (legally unlimited) and **determinate**.

**Examples used by Austin and his critics:** Maine pointed out that Ranjit Singh of the Punjab was a good example of an Austinian sovereign (an absolute ruler issuing commands), but even he did not make general laws, he mostly made specific orders and left custom largely undisturbed.

**Criticisms (high yield):**

- **Hart** says Austin's model is the 'gunman situation writ large' and cannot explain **power-conferring rules** (for example how to make a will or contract) which do not command anything, they only confer powers.

- Austin cannot explain **customary law** (law that existed before and independently of any sovereign command) or **international law** or **constitutional law** (the sovereign is bound by the constitution).

- His idea of an **indivisible sovereign** does not fit **federal systems** where sovereignty is shared (as in India or the USA).

- Law's binding force rests on **acceptance** (Hart's internal point of view), not just fear of sanction.

- He ignored the **law-making role of judges** and the difference between **obligation and being obliged**.

- **Natural lawyers** criticise the total separation of law and morality.

**Austin's defenders:** Austin's contribution was the clear *analytical method* and the insistence on distinguishing law as it is from law as it ought to be. Even Hart's own project is built on revising Austin.

**Bentham versus Austin (a common confusion):** Bentham's **Of Laws in General** (published only in the twentieth century by H.L.A. Hart as *Of Laws in General*, 1970) is richer than Austin's version: Bentham recognised that a law can be a *complex of commands* and accepted *permissions* and *powers*, and he allowed the sovereign to be limited by public opinion and popular acceptance. Bentham's *An Introduction to the Principles of Morals and Legislation* (1789) is the source of **utilitarianism** (the greatest happiness of the greatest number, with the hedonic or felicific calculus).

### 1.4 Sources of Law: Concept and Classification

The expression 'sources of law' is ambiguous, so examiners often test the classification.

**Salmond's classification (the most commonly asked):**

- **Formal source:** the will of the State, which gives the rule its validity and force as law. Law gets its legal validity from this.

- **Material sources:** the places from which the *matter* (content) of law is drawn, not the validity. These are of two kinds:

  - **Legal sources:** those which the law itself recognises as *authoritative* (gateways through which new principles enter the law). They are **legislation, precedent, custom, agreement (conventional law)** and, to a lesser extent, professional or juristic opinion and equity.

  - **Historical sources:** those which influence the law only indirectly, such as legal literature, foreign law, and writings. They are not authoritative until a legal source adopts them.

**Other classifications:**

- **Austin** treated *legislation* (sovereign command) as the real source and the others as 'sources' only in a loose sense.

- **Allen** in *Law in the Making* concentrates on **legislation, precedent and custom** as the main 'sources', and treats the study of how the law is made as the study of its sources.

- **Gray** lists statutes, judicial precedents, opinions of experts, customs and principles of morality.

- **Keeton** distinguishes **binding sources** (legislation, precedent, custom) from **persuasive sources** (books of authority, foreign decisions, principles of morality).

- **Hart** uses the **rule of recognition**, a social rule used by officials to identify valid law, as the ultimate criterion of validity within a legal system, so the 'source thesis' says legal validity depends on social facts (pedigree) and not on moral merit.

- **Kelsen** traces validity upward to the **Grundnorm** (basic norm), a presupposed norm.

**Sources of Indian law (for applied questions):** the Constitution of India; statutes enacted by Parliament and State legislatures; ordinances (Articles 123 and 213); delegated legislation (rules, regulations, bye-laws); judicial precedent (Article 141); custom and usage (Article 13(3)(a) treats custom or usage having the force of law as 'law'); personal laws (partially, by religion); and international treaties as implemented through Article 253 and, increasingly, as guiding principles (for example *Vishaka v State of Rajasthan*, 1997, which read international conventions into fundamental rights where domestic law is silent).

### 1.5 Legislation

**Meaning:** legislation is the **declaration of legal rules by a competent authority**, and it is the most important source in modern states. Salmond says that legislation is that source of law which consists in the declaration of legal rules by a competent authority.

**Kinds (Salmond):**

- **Supreme legislation:** that which emanates from the sovereign power and cannot be repealed, annulled or controlled by any other legislative authority. In Salmond's day, the British Parliament. In India, Parliament is *not* supreme in this sense because of the written Constitution and judicial review.

- **Subordinate legislation:** made by an authority subordinate to the supreme legislature. Salmond lists five forms: **colonial** (legislatures of colonies), **executive** (the executive's power to make rules and regulations by delegation), **judicial** (rules of court made by judges), **municipal** (bye-laws by local authorities) and **autonomous** (laws by private bodies like universities or railway companies, with the State's permission).

**Delegated legislation in India (concept and cases):**

- *In re Delhi Laws Act* (1951): the legislature cannot delegate its **essential legislative function**, but may delegate non-essential functions. Conditional legislation is valid.

- *Hamdard Dawakhana v Union of India* (1960): the legislature must lay down the policy and legal principle, leaving only details to the delegate.

- *Gwalior Rayon Silk Manufacturing Co v Asst. Commissioner of Sales Tax* (1973): the legislature must declare the policy; excessive delegation is unconstitutional.

- *Indian Express Newspapers v Union of India* (1985): delegated legislation can be challenged for being **manifestly arbitrary** and unreasonable under Article 14.

- *Chintaman Rao*? Not required; focus on the three above.

**Codification and consolidation:** codification arranges existing law systematically in a code (Bentham's wish; the Indian Contract Act 1872 and the older Penal Code are examples), consolidation merges many statutes on one subject without changing substance. Bentham was a strong advocate of codification and rejected 'judge-made law' as 'dog law'.

**Merits of legislation over precedent:** it is deliberate, prospective, systematic, can create new law quickly, is made by representatives, and is accessible. **Demerits:** it can be rigid, may lag behind facts, can be poorly drafted, and may be rendered uncertain by interpretation.

**Interpretation of statutes (rules the examiners like):**

- **Literal rule:** words are given their plain ordinary meaning.

- **Golden rule:** depart from the literal meaning only to avoid absurdity.

- **Mischief rule (Heydon's case, 1584):** ask what the common law was, what the mischief was, what remedy Parliament appointed, and the true reason of the remedy.

- **Purposive approach:** the court looks to the legislative purpose, which is now the preferred approach in India and in most modern systems.

- **Ejusdem generis, noscitur a sociis, expressio unius est exclusio alterius** are the commonest maxims.

### 1.6 Precedent

**Meaning:** a judicial decision to which authority is attributed in the later decisions of the courts. The doctrine of **stare decisis** ('stand by what has been decided') provides that like cases should be decided alike. Salmond defines precedent as the decision of a court of justice treated as an authority in later cases.

**Two parts of a judgment:**

- **Ratio decidendi:** the principle of law on which the decision rests, the binding part.

- **Obiter dicta:** remarks made by the way, not necessary for the decision. They are persuasive only (although the Indian Supreme Court has said considered obiter of the Supreme Court deserves great weight).

**Tests for finding the ratio:**

- **Wambaugh's inversion test:** reverse the proposition; if the decision would still stand, the proposition is not the ratio.

- **Goodhart's test:** the ratio is found by taking into account the **material facts** treated as material by the judge, and the decision based on them. Goodhart rejected the idea that the ratio is simply the judge's stated reasoning.

- **Halsbury's view (Quinn v Leathem, 1901):** a case is only an authority for what it actually decides, not what logically follows from it.

**Kinds of precedent (Salmond):**

- **Authoritative and persuasive precedents.** Authoritative precedents must be followed even if the judge disagrees with them. Persuasive precedents (foreign decisions, decisions of lower courts, obiter) are only considered.

- **Declaratory and original precedents.** A declaratory precedent merely applies existing law; an original precedent creates and applies new law. Salmond notes that original precedents are the real law-making ones.

- **Absolutely authoritative and conditionally authoritative:** absolutely authoritative precedents bind the court *and cannot be disregarded in any circumstances*; conditionally authoritative precedents may be disregarded if they are wrong in a certain way (for example if decided *per incuriam*).

**Departing from precedent:**

- **Distinguishing:** the later court finds a material difference in facts.

- **Overruling:** a higher court (or in some cases the same court, or a larger bench) declares the earlier decision wrong.

- **Reversing:** a higher court on appeal in the same case sets aside the decision of the lower court.

- **Per incuriam:** a decision given in ignorance of a binding statute or binding authority. See *Young v Bristol Aeroplane Co* (1944).

- **Sub silentio:** a point passed without argument or consideration is not binding.

- **Prospective overruling:** a decision is declared wrong but applies only to the future, not to past transactions. The doctrine was adopted in India in **Golak Nath v State of Punjab (1967)** (borrowed from American practice, *Linkletter v Walker*).

**Indian position on precedent:**

- **Article 141:** the law declared by the Supreme Court is binding on all courts within the territory of India. It does not bind the Supreme Court itself.

- **Article 129 and Article 215:** the Supreme Court and High Courts are courts of record.

- **Bengal Immunity Co v State of Bihar (1955):** the Supreme Court is not bound by its own earlier decisions and may depart from them in appropriate cases.

- **Keshav Mills Co v CIT (1965):** the Court can reconsider an earlier decision only if there are compelling reasons, the settled law should not be lightly disturbed.

- **Union of India v Raghubir Singh (1989):** a classic statement by Chief Justice Pathak on the need for certainty and the proper approach to precedent.

- **Central Board of Dawoodi Bohra Community v State of Maharashtra (2005):** a bench of the Supreme Court is bound by a decision of a coordinate or larger bench; if it disagrees, it must refer the matter to a **larger bench**. A smaller bench cannot overrule a larger bench.

- **Article 145(3):** the minimum bench of five judges for constitutional questions (Constitution Bench).

- High Court decisions bind subordinate courts in that State, and are persuasive in other States.

**English position (for comparison):** The House of Lords in *London Tramways v LCC* (1898) held itself absolutely bound by its earlier decisions, which was relaxed by the **Practice Statement of 1966** (the Lords may depart from previous decisions 'when it appears right to do so'). The Supreme Court of the United Kingdom has inherited this approach.

**Merits of precedent:** certainty, predictability, equality, efficiency, and gradual growth of law through experience. **Demerits:** rigidity, danger of bad decisions being perpetuated, complexity because of a vast mass of cases, and artificial distinctions.

### 1.7 Custom

**Meaning:** custom is a rule of conduct which has been observed over a long time by a community, and is accepted by that community as binding. Salmond calls custom the embodiment of those principles which have commended themselves to the national conscience as principles of justice and public utility. The **historical school** (Savigny, Puchta) treated custom as the *primary* form of law, the direct expression of the Volksgeist.

**Requisites of a valid custom (the classical tests, often framed in MCQs):**

- **Antiquity:** it must be ancient, in English law traditionally from 'time immemorial' (1189). In India, courts accept customs of long usage, but there is no fixed period; it must be of such age as to be taken as having been always observed.

- **Continuity:** it must have been observed continuously without interruption.

- **Peaceable enjoyment:** it must have been exercised without force or secrecy.

- **Certainty:** it must be definite in its nature and scope.

- **Reasonableness:** it must be reasonable (it can be contrary to the common law but not contrary to the fundamental principles of justice).

- **Obligatory force:** it must be regarded by those who follow it as binding, not merely optional.

- **Consistency:** it must not conflict with another custom.

- **Conformity with statute law and public policy:** a custom cannot override an express statute or public morality.

- **Generality or locality:** a general custom applies to the whole community, a local one only to a particular place or group.

**Kinds of custom (Salmond):**

- **Legal custom:** one that has the force of law in itself (further divided into **local** and **general**). The two kinds are **local custom** (a particular locality) and **general custom** (the whole kingdom, which is part of the common law).

- **Conventional custom (usage):** binds only because the parties have agreed to it, expressly or by implication, as part of their contract (for example, trade usages).

**Austin and Holland on custom:** Austin treated custom as a mere 'positive morality' until the courts adopt it, at which point it becomes law by judicial recognition (*custom is transmuted into law by judicial decision*). Holland and Salmond say that custom is law *before* judicial recognition because it is accepted as such in the community.

**Indian law on custom:**

- **Article 13(3)(a):** 'law' includes custom or usage having in the territory of India the force of law, so a custom can be tested for violation of fundamental rights (although the courts have been cautious in applying this to personal law).

- **Collector of Madura v Mootoo Ramalinga Sethupathy (1868):** the Privy Council famously held that clear proof of usage will outweigh the written text of the law. This is the leading authority on the priority of custom over the Smritis in Hindu law.

- Section 13 of the Indian Evidence Act (now in the Bharatiya Sakshya Adhiniyam, 2023) allows custom to be proved by showing specific instances.

- **Hindu Marriage Act 1955 section 3(a)** defines 'custom and usage' as a rule which has been continuously and uniformly observed for a long time, is certain and not unreasonable or opposed to public policy, and in the case of a rule applicable only to a family, has not been discontinued by the family. (The same wording is used in the Hindu Adoptions and Maintenance Act 1956.)

**Custom and statute today:** legislation has largely displaced custom in modern states, but custom remains alive in personal laws, tribal laws (for instance the Fifth and Sixth Schedule areas), constitutional conventions and international law (custom is a source under Article 38(1)(b) of the ICJ Statute, which requires *state practice* and *opinio juris*).

### 1.8 Other Sources: Agreement, Juristic Writings, Equity, Constitution

- **Agreement (conventional law):** private agreements create law inter partes, and treaties create law between states.

- **Juristic writings:** in the common law system, books of authority (Coke, Blackstone, Glanville) can be authority; in Roman, Hindu and Muslim law, the writings of jurists were primary sources (for example the Dharmashastras and Manusmriti, Yajnavalkya Smriti and the commentaries *Mitakshara* and *Dayabhaga*).

- **Equity:** developed in the English Court of Chancery as a corrective to the rigidity of common law (maxims such as 'equity follows the law' and 'he who seeks equity must do equity'). In India, equity operates under 'justice, equity and good conscience'.

- **Constitution:** in written-constitution systems, it is the *supreme source* from which all other legal authority derives, and under **Article 13** laws inconsistent with fundamental rights are void.

### 2.2 Natural Law School

**Basic position:** there exists a higher law, derived from nature, reason, God or human nature, which is *superior to man-made law* and provides the standard by which the justice of positive law is measured. The slogan associated with it is *lex iniusta non est lex* (an unjust law is no law), which comes from St Augustine and is developed by Aquinas.

**Stages of development (very important for chronological questions):**

**(a) Greek natural law**

- **Heraclitus** and the **Sophists** debated *physis* (nature) against *nomos* (convention).

- **Socrates, Plato and Aristotle:** Plato's *Republic* describes justice as each class performing its proper function. **Aristotle** (in *Nicomachean Ethics* and *Politics*) distinguished **natural justice** (valid everywhere with the same force) from **legal or conventional justice**, and divided justice into **distributive** and **corrective (rectificatory)** justice. He said man is a political animal.

- **The Stoics** (Zeno, Epictetus, Marcus Aurelius) taught that the universe is governed by reason (*logos*), and that all men, being rational, are subject to a universal law of nature.

**(b) Roman natural law**

- **Cicero** in *De Re Publica* described true law as right reason in agreement with nature, of universal application, unchanging and everlasting.

- Roman jurists developed the *jus naturale*, the *jus gentium* (law of nations) and *jus civile* (civil law). Gaius and Ulpian explained these categories.

**(c) Medieval natural law (the theological phase)**

- **St Augustine** (*City of God*): an unjust law does not seem to be law.

- **St Thomas Aquinas** (*Summa Theologica*) gives the most famous classification of law, a *four-fold* scheme:

  - **Lex aeterna** (eternal law): the divine reason governing the whole universe.

  - **Lex divina** (divine law): the revealed law of Scripture.

  - **Lex naturalis** (natural law): man's participation in the eternal law through reason.

  - **Lex humana** (human law): the rules made by human authorities, derived from natural law, and *valid only if consistent with it*.

- Aquinas defines law as *an ordinance of reason for the common good, made by one who has care of the community, and promulgated*.

**(d) The Renaissance and the age of reason (the secular, rationalist phase)**

- **Hugo Grotius** (1583 to 1645), the 'father of international law', in *De Jure Belli ac Pacis* (1625) argued that natural law would exist **even if God did not exist**, because it rests on human reason and man's social nature (*appetitus societatis*). He separated natural law from theology.

- **Thomas Hobbes** (*Leviathan*, 1651): in the state of nature life is 'solitary, poor, nasty, brutish and short'; men surrender their rights to an absolute sovereign through a social contract for security.

- **John Locke** (*Two Treatises of Government*, 1689): in the state of nature men have natural rights to **life, liberty and property**; government exists by consent to protect these, and may be resisted if it breaches the trust. Locke influenced the American Declaration of Independence and constitutionalism.

- **Jean-Jacques Rousseau** (*The Social Contract*, 1762): 'Man is born free and everywhere he is in chains'; sovereignty rests in the **general will** of the people.

- **Immanuel Kant** (*Metaphysics of Morals*, *Rechtslehre*): the **categorical imperative**; law is the sum of conditions under which the freedom of one can co-exist with the freedom of all according to a universal law of freedom.

**(e) Decline in the nineteenth century:** the natural law approach was attacked by the **historical school** (Savigny), **utilitarians** (Bentham called natural rights 'nonsense upon stilts') and **positivists** (Austin). It was seen as vague and subjective.

**(f) Revival in the twentieth century:** two World Wars, the Nazi regime and the Nuremberg trials revived the idea that there are **limits to the validity of positive law**.

- **Rudolf Stammler** (*Theory of Just Law*): *natural law with a variable content*. The form of just law is constant, the content changes with time and place. He proposed the *social ideal* of a community of free-willing men, and two principles of **respect** and **participation**.

- **Josef Kohler:** law as an evolving civilisation; 'jurisprudence of culture' (neo-Hegelian).

- **Giorgio del Vecchio:** natural law as the inherent consciousness of justice in man.

- **Gustav Radbruch:** after WWII, advanced the **Radbruch formula**: a positive law, even if duly enacted, must yield to justice where the contradiction is intolerable (extreme injustice is not law).

- **Lon Fuller** (*The Morality of Law*, 1964): the *procedural natural law* of eight desiderata (see Topic 1) and the claim that law has an **inner morality**. Fuller's argument in the **Hart–Fuller debate** (*Harvard Law Review*, 1958) is the classic 'natural law reply' to positivism.

- **John Finnis** (*Natural Law and Natural Rights*, 1980): a **new natural law** approach drawn from Aquinas, setting out the **basic goods**: **life, knowledge, play, aesthetic experience, sociability (friendship), practical reasonableness and religion**. Finnis argues that law's moral force is a *central case* matter: unjust laws are law in a defective sense.

- **Ronald Dworkin:** *principles* in law (see Dworkin below) and his 'moral reading' of the Constitution, a neo-natural law element although he does not use the label.

- **John Rawls** (*A Theory of Justice*, 1971): a modern social-contract account of justice (see Topic 9).

**The Hart–Fuller debate (1958) in detail:**

- **Context:** the *post-war German informer case*: a woman denounced her husband under a Nazi statute for insulting remarks about Hitler; after the war, she was prosecuted for illegally depriving him of liberty. The question was whether the Nazi statute was 'law'.

- **Hart (positivist):** the Nazi statute was law, but it was *so evil that it should not be obeyed*. The right solution is to pass a retrospective statute and say openly that it is a departure from the principle against retroactivity, and to separate the question of 'what law is' from 'what it ought to be'.

- **Fuller (natural law):** a system so flawed that it fails to meet the *inner morality* of law is not law at all; such a regime is incompatible with the idea of legality. The court should have refused to apply the statute.

- **Hart's reference to the core and penumbra:** a rule has a *core of settled meaning* and a *penumbra of uncertainty*, the standard example being a rule that bans vehicles in a park (does it ban roller skates?). Fuller answered that interpretation involves purpose, not merely words.

**Features of natural law thinking (for 'which of the following' questions):**

- Law and morality are **necessarily connected**.

- Law has a **moral validity test**, unjust rules are defective or void.

- Law is **discovered** by reason, not merely made.

- It supplies a **critical standard** for judging and reforming positive law.

- It has been used both to **support revolution** (Locke, American and French Revolutions) and to **justify the status quo** (feudal order under medieval theology).

**Indian connection:** the Indian concept of **Dharma** has features of natural law (a universal order). In constitutional adjudication, the Supreme Court's use of **basic structure** (*Kesavananda Bharati v State of Kerala*, 1973), the reading of natural justice and 'due process' into Article 21 (*Maneka Gandhi v Union of India*, 1978), and the expansion of fundamental rights are often analysed as natural-law-flavoured reasoning. In *Golak Nath* (1967), fundamental rights were treated as 'natural rights' beyond amendment, although that decision was later overruled in *Kesavananda*.

**Criticisms:** vagueness and subjectivity (whose reason? whose nature?); confusion between *is* and *ought*; has been used to justify contradictory positions; Hume's guillotine says one cannot derive an 'ought' from an 'is'; and the failure to explain why an immoral rule can still be valid (because validity and justice are different).

### 2.3 Analytical Positivism

**Basic position:** law is a matter of **social fact**, not moral merit. The **separation thesis** is that there is no *necessary* connection between law and morality, and the **sources thesis** is that the existence of law depends on social sources (who made it, how), not its content.

**Bentham (1748 to 1832):**

- Founder of **utilitarianism**: the principle of utility requires that the right act or law is the one which produces the greatest happiness of the greatest number. He measured pleasure and pain through the **hedonic (felicific) calculus** with seven factors: intensity, duration, certainty, propinquity (nearness), fecundity, purity and extent.

- Works: *A Fragment on Government* (1776), *An Introduction to the Principles of Morals and Legislation* (1789), *Of Laws in General*, *Limits of Jurisprudence Defined*.

- Rejected natural rights and the social contract; supported **codification**; classified law into expositorial and censorial; and coined the terms **international law, codification** and **utilitarian**.

**John Stuart Mill:** *Utilitarianism* (1863), *On Liberty* (1859): introduced a **qualitative** distinction among pleasures ('better to be Socrates dissatisfied than a fool satisfied') and the **harm principle** (power may be rightfully exercised over an individual against his will only to prevent harm to others). The harm principle is central in the law and morality debate (Topic 3).

**John Austin:** see Topic 1.3.

**H.L.A. Hart (1907 to 1992)** *The Concept of Law* (1961): the leading modern positivist, Professor of Jurisprudence at Oxford.

- **Criticism of Austin:** the gunman analogy shows the 'command and threat' model cannot account for *obligation*; the model cannot explain **power-conferring rules**, continuity of legislative authority, customary law, or the self-binding nature of the legislature.

- **Distinction between 'being obliged' and 'having an obligation':** a gunman *obliges* you to hand over money, but you are not under an obligation to do so.

- **Law as the union of primary and secondary rules:**

  - **Primary rules** impose duties (what people must or must not do). They solve the problem of *static* rules.

  - **Secondary rules** are rules about rules. They are of three types: the **rule of recognition** (identifies valid rules; solves the problem of *uncertainty*), **rules of change** (provide for making and altering rules; solve the *static* quality), and **rules of adjudication** (empower courts to decide breaches; solve the *inefficiency* of social pressure).

- **The rule of recognition** is the ultimate rule of the system. It is not itself 'valid' or 'invalid', it simply exists as a matter of fact (accepted by officials). It is the key to the **concept of legal validity**.

- **Internal and external point of view:** the *internal* point of view is that of someone who accepts the rule as a standard of conduct (the *participant*), and the *external* point of view is that of an observer who only records regularities. Law cannot be understood from the external viewpoint alone.

- **Open texture of law:** because language is general, every rule has a **core** of settled meaning and a **penumbra** of doubt where judges exercise **discretion**.

- **Minimum content of natural law:** Hart accepted that there are certain *truisms* about human nature which give law a minimum moral content: **human vulnerability, approximate equality, limited altruism, limited resources, and limited understanding and strength of will.** Hence rules protecting persons, property and promises are present in all societies. This is often tested as 'Hart's concession to natural law'.

- **Hart's theory of justice and morality:** he distinguishes *legal obligation* from *moral obligation*, and recognises that morals can influence law, but does not require it for validity.

- **Hart versus Devlin (1959 onwards):** see Topic 3.

- **Hart on punishment:** *Punishment and Responsibility* (1968): the justifying aim of punishment is *general deterrence*, but it is limited by retributive principles of *who* may be punished and how much (a mixed theory).

**Joseph Raz (1939 to 2022):** developed **exclusive (hard) legal positivism**: legal validity depends only on **sources** (social facts), never on moral criteria. His works include *The Authority of Law* (1979) and *The Morality of Freedom* (1986). He developed the 'service conception of authority'. In contrast, **inclusive (soft) positivism** (Hart's *Postscript*, Jules Coleman, W.J. Waluchow) allows that a legal system *may* incorporate moral criteria in its rule of recognition (for example, a constitutional bill of rights).

**Key differences within positivism:**

| Point         | Bentham/Austin             | Hart                                 | Raz                                                |
|---------------|----------------------------|--------------------------------------|----------------------------------------------------|
| Nature of law | Command backed by sanction | Union of primary and secondary rules | Authoritative directives; exclusive sources thesis |
| Sovereign     | Needed                     | Replaced by rule of recognition      | Law claims authority                               |
| Validity      | Source in sovereign        | Rule of recognition                  | Social sources only                                |
| Morality      | Separate                   | Minimum content of natural law       | Strictly separate                                  |

### 2.4 Ronald Dworkin and the Hart–Dworkin Debate

**Ronald Dworkin (1931 to 2013):** *Taking Rights Seriously* (1977), *Law's Empire* (1986), *A Matter of Principle* (1985), *Justice for Hedgehogs* (2011), *Justice in Robes* (2006), *Freedom's Law* (1996).

- **Attack on positivism:** a legal system contains not only **rules** but also **principles** (standards observed because they are a requirement of justice, fairness or morality) and **policies** (standards setting a goal for community welfare). Rules apply in an *all or nothing fashion*, principles have **weight** and can be balanced against each other. Hart's rule of recognition cannot identify principles by pedigree alone.

- **Hard cases and the rights thesis:** in hard cases the judge does **not have strong discretion**; the judge must find the *right answer* by constructing the theory that best fits and justifies the existing legal materials. Judges decide on **principle (rights)**, not on policy, which belongs to the legislature.

- **Hercules:** the ideal, superhumanly learned and patient judge who can construct such a theory.

- **Law as integrity:** in *Law's Empire*, law is an *interpretive concept*; the judge is a writer of a **chain novel**, adding a chapter that fits the earlier chapters, and showing the practice in its best moral light. Integrity combines **fit** and **justification**. Law's Empire opposes **conventionalism** and **pragmatism**.

- **Cases cited by Dworkin:** *Riggs v Palmer* (1889, New York: a murderer cannot inherit from his victim under the will, the principle that no man may profit from his own wrong) and *Henningsen v Bloomfield Motors* (1960, on freedom of contract and unequal bargaining power).

- **Rights as trumps:** individual rights *trump* collective goals.

- **Hart's reply (Postscript to The Concept of Law, 1994):** principles can be identified by the rule of recognition if the system uses *inclusive* criteria; Dworkin's theory misunderstands the descriptive nature of Hart's project.

### 2.5 Kelsen's Pure Theory of Law

**Hans Kelsen (1881 to 1973)**, Austrian jurist and the principal drafter of the 1920 Austrian Constitution. Works: *Pure Theory of Law* (1934, revised 1960), *General Theory of Law and State* (1945).

**Aims and the idea of purity:**

- **Methodological purity:** law should be studied as it is, not as it ought to be morally. Kelsen wanted to purge jurisprudence of **psychology, sociology, ethics and political theory**. This is a *science of law*, not a theory of justice.

- **Is and ought:** a norm is an 'ought' proposition. Natural science deals with causality (**is**), legal science with **imputation** (if X then *ought* to follow Y).

- **A legal norm is valid** if it belongs to a legal order which is, on the whole, efficacious. **Validity** comes from a higher norm, not from the facts.

**The hierarchy of norms (Stufenbau):** every lower norm derives validity from a higher norm: individual decision then statute then constitution then **Grundnorm** (basic norm). The Grundnorm is **not enacted**, it is a **presupposition** of juristic thinking: 'one ought to obey the historically first constitution'. It is a transcendental-logical presupposition (borrowed from Kant), not a moral or natural-law norm.

**Other features:**

- Law is a **coercive order**: it attaches sanctions to conduct.

- **State and law are identical:** the State is merely the personification of the legal order; Kelsen rejected the **dualism** of state and law.

- **Static and dynamic systems of norms:** in a *static* system norms are valid because of their content (as in morality), in a *dynamic* system norms are valid because they were created in a particular way, which is the character of law.

- **Public and private law distinction:** Kelsen rejected it as ideological. **Rights and duties:** a right is merely a reflex of a duty.

- **International law:** Kelsen advocated the **primacy of international law** (monism).

- **Grundnorm and revolutions:** when a revolution succeeds and the new regime becomes effective, the **Grundnorm changes**. This explains the following decisions, often asked:

  - **State v Dosso (1958, Pakistan):** Munir CJ applied Kelsen to hold that the successful coup of 1958 created a valid legal order. This was *overruled* in **Asma Jilani v Government of Punjab (1972)**.

  - **Uganda v Commissioner of Prisons, ex parte Matovu (1966):** the Ugandan High Court held that the 1966 revolution had created a new legal order with a new Grundnorm.

  - **Madzimbamuto v Lardner-Burke (1968)** (Rhodesia): the court considered the **Unilateral Declaration of Independence** and the Grundnorm. The Privy Council held the illegal regime had no legal authority, whereas the General Division of the High Court in Rhodesia had recognised the new regime on the basis of Kelsen's reasoning. (*Rhodesia: also R v Ndhlovu*, 1968.)

**Criticisms:** the Grundnorm is a fiction and is not needed; the theory is too formal and ignores the content of law and justice; the dependence on 'efficacy' compromises purity; Hart said it is wrong to see the rule of recognition as presupposed rather than a social fact; Julius Stone said the theory is 'empty'; and Kelsen's thesis that every legal norm is a conditional sanction-rule is oversimplified.

**Kelsen versus Austin versus Hart (a standard comparative table):**

| Issue               | Austin               | Kelsen                     | Hart                                          |
|---------------------|----------------------|----------------------------|-----------------------------------------------|
| Law is              | Command of sovereign | Hierarchy of norms         | Union of primary and secondary rules          |
| Source of validity  | Sovereign            | Grundnorm                  | Rule of recognition (a social fact)           |
| Sanction            | Essential            | Essential (coercive order) | Not essential for each rule                   |
| Law versus morality | Separate             | Separate                   | Separate, with minimum content of natural law |
| Approach            | Analytical           | Normative and formal       | Analytical and descriptive sociology          |

### 2.6 Historical School

**Basic position:** law is not a product of deliberate legislation or abstract reason. It is a **gradual, organic growth**, rooted in the history, customs and genius (**Volksgeist**) of a people, in the same way as language. The school arose in Germany as a **reaction against the French Revolution, natural law rationalism and the movement for codification**.

**Precursors:**

- **Montesquieu** (*The Spirit of the Laws*, 1748): laws are shaped by climate, soil, religion, customs, commerce and the form of government. He is often called a precursor of both the historical and sociological schools and of the doctrine of **separation of powers**.

- **Edmund Burke:** a conservative who stressed continuity, tradition and slow reform in opposition to the French Revolution.

- **Gustav Hugo:** a German jurist who taught that law is the product of the nation's growth and that positive law need not be rationalised.

**Friedrich Karl von Savigny (1779 to 1861):** the chief exponent.

- **Controversy with Thibaut (1814):** Thibaut, a Heidelberg professor, urged a **uniform civil code** for the German states. Savigny replied in his pamphlet **Of the Vocation of Our Age for Legislation and Jurisprudence (1814)** that Germany was not ready for a code: the science of law was undeveloped, and a code would freeze and distort what ought to evolve. The result was a delay of codification until the German Civil Code (BGB) of 1900.

- **Volksgeist (the spirit of the people):** law, like language, originates in the common consciousness of the people. It is *found*, not *made*. Law grows with the people, strengthens with them, and finally dies when the nation loses its individuality.

- **Custom as the first source:** law first appears as custom, then as juristic law (developed by lawyers as representatives of the people), and finally legislation, which should merely give effect to the popular will.

- **Principal works:** *System of Modern Roman Law* (*System des heutigen romischen Rechts*, 1840 to 1849), and *The Law of Possession* (*Das Recht des Besitzes*, 1803), a work that made his early reputation.

- **Georg Friedrich Puchta**, a pupil of Savigny, extended the idea in his work on **customary law** (*Das Gewohnheitsrecht*) and claimed that custom stands *above* statutes and that the jurist is a mediator between the Volksgeist and the law.

**Sir Henry Maine (1822 to 1888):** the English historical jurist and a member of the Viceroy's Council in India (the Law Member in 1862 to 1869), who applied the historical method to comparative legal history, including India.

- **Works:** *Ancient Law* (1861), *Village Communities in the East and West* (1871), *Early History of Institutions* (1875), *Early Law and Custom* (1883), *Dissertations on Early Law and Custom*.

- **From status to contract:** 'the movement of the progressive societies has hitherto been a movement **from Status to Contract**.' In early societies a person's rights and duties derived from family status; in progressive societies they derive from freely made contracts.

- **Static and progressive societies:** only the progressive societies develop their law beyond early customary stages.

- **Stages in the development of law:** (1) law by **Themistes** (judgments dictated by the king under divine inspiration, as in Homer); (2) **customary law**, held by an aristocratic minority; (3) **codes** (the Twelve Tables of Rome, the Laws of Manu in India, the Code of Draco in Athens); (4) **instruments of legal change**: **legal fictions, equity and legislation**, in that order.

- **Patriarchal theory:** primitive society was composed of patriarchal families, not individuals.

- **Indian dimension:** Maine's discussion of the Indian village community and of the Hindu law of caste and joint family has had a lasting influence on colonial legal studies.

**Merits of the historical school:** it introduced the idea of *evolution*, gave a scientific, comparative understanding of law, showed the role of custom, and warned against hasty legislation. **Criticisms:** the Volksgeist is vague and unprovable; the school overstates the role of custom and undervalues legislation; it ignores the role of deliberate reform and conscious legal planning (as in modern welfare states); the idea of one 'national spirit' ignores the diversity of groups; it was **misused by Nazi ideology** to glorify race and nation; and ironically, Savigny's own work is based on **Roman law**, a foreign import.

### 2.7 Sociological School

**Basic position:** law is a **social phenomenon** and an instrument of social control. It must be studied in its **actual working** and its **effects on society** (not just as an abstract rule), and it should be a tool to satisfy social interests. The approach is functional.

**Forerunners:**

- **Auguste Comte** (1798 to 1857): founder of **positivism** and of *sociology*; the 'law of three stages' (theological, metaphysical and positive).

- **Herbert Spencer:** an organic analogy of society and 'survival of the fittest'; wrote *Social Statics*.

- **Montesquieu** (see above).

**Emile Durkheim (1858 to 1917):** *The Division of Labour in Society* (1893).

- Law is the visible index of **social solidarity**.

- **Mechanical solidarity** (found in simple societies, based on likeness) is sustained by **repressive law** (penal), while **organic solidarity** (in complex societies with division of labour, based on interdependence) is sustained by **restitutive (cooperative) law** (civil, commercial, administrative).

- Law is a social fact; crime offends the collective conscience.

**Rudolf von Ihering (1818 to 1892):** a German jurist, one of the main founders of the sociological and teleological approach, who moved from conceptual jurisprudence to a purposive theory.

- **Works:** *Law as a Means to an End* (*Der Zweck im Recht*, 1877 to 1883), *The Struggle for Law* (*Der Kampf ums Recht*, 1872), *Spirit of Roman Law*.

- Law is the **sum of the conditions of social life** assured by the State through external compulsion. Law is created for a **purpose** (the interest of society), and 'purpose is the creator of the entire law'.

- *Social utilitarianism:* he combined the idea of social interest with the individual's interest; he held that **rights are legally protected interests**.

- **The struggle for law:** it is an individual's *moral duty* to assert and defend his rights, and the sum of such struggles creates law.

**Eugen Ehrlich (1862 to 1922):** Austrian jurist, the founder of the **sociology of law** (a *free law* thinker).

- **Work:** *Fundamental Principles of the Sociology of Law* (*Grundlegung der Soziologie des Rechts*, 1913).

- **Living law:** the true centre of gravity of legal development 'lies not in legislation, nor in juristic science, nor in judicial decision, but in society itself'. The **living law** is the law which dominates life even though not posited in legal propositions, as against the **state law** (norms for decision), which is made by the State.

- Studied the actual customs of the Bukovina region (then in Austria) for evidence.

**Leon Duguit (1859 to 1928):** French jurist, a leader of the **solidarism** approach.

- **Works:** *Law in the Modern State*, *Les Transformations du droit public*, *Traite de droit constitutionnel*.

- Law is based on **social solidarity** (interdependence of men, an idea he took from Durkheim). **Social function**, not rights, is the basic concept: property is a social function; there are **no natural or subjective rights**, only duties of each person to contribute to social interdependence.

- The State is **not a sovereign** commanding, but a **cooperative of public services**. The *public service* is the key concept of public law.

**Roscoe Pound (1870 to 1964):** Dean of Harvard Law School, the main American sociological jurist.

- **Works:** *The Scope and Purpose of Sociological Jurisprudence* (1911 to 1912), *An Introduction to the Philosophy of Law* (1922), *Interpretations of Legal History* (1923), *Social Control through Law* (1942), *Jurisprudence* (five volumes, 1959).

- **Law as social engineering:** law is a social institution to satisfy social wants, to the maximum extent with the minimum friction and waste. Jurisprudence is *a social engineering* of balancing competing interests, as an engineer builds a structure.

- **Interests protected by law (three categories):**

  - **Individual interests:** personality, domestic relations, interests of substance (property, succession, freedom of industry and contract).

  - **Public interests:** interests of the State as a juristic person, and as guardian of social interests.

  - **Social interests:** general security, security of social institutions, **general morals**, **conservation of social resources**, **general progress** and **individual life** (the social interest in the individual's human life).

- **Jural postulates:** the statements about what should be in a given civilisation, for example that people must be able to assume that others will not commit intentional aggression, and that men must act with due care.

- **Law in books and law in action:** the *gap* between the formal rule and how it works in practice. He urged the study of the **working** of law and the **effects** of legal doctrines on society.

- **Theory of social control:** law is a developed form of social control, among religion and morality, as the society gets more complex.

- **Criticisms:** the theory of 'interests' is vague and has no principle for ranking them; the 'engineering' metaphor suggests a technocratic, uncritical approach; and the theory gives the judge too much room.

**Max Weber (1864 to 1920):** *Economy and Society*. **Three types of legitimate authority:** **traditional, charismatic and legal-rational**. Weber analysed the **rationalisation** of law, identifying *formal rationality* as characteristic of modern capitalist legal systems (as against substantive or irrational types).

**Benjamin Cardozo (1870 to 1938):** *The Nature of the Judicial Process* (1921). Judges legislate in the gaps (*interstitially*) and use four methods: **logic (philosophy), history (evolution), custom (tradition) and sociology (social welfare)**. Cardozo's method of sociology means deciding in view of public welfare.

**Julius Stone:** *The Province and Function of Law* (1946), who distinguished the functions of law and wrote on the *lawyers' extroversion*.

**Indian application:** the sociological approach is at the heart of the **Directive Principles of State Policy** (Part IV), and the *Preamble*, which makes law an instrument of social and economic justice. The Supreme Court's **Public Interest Litigation (PIL)** and the idea of **social-action litigation** are examples (*Hussainara Khatoon v State of Bihar*, 1979; *S.P. Gupta v Union of India*, 1981 (on *locus standi*)). The *Maneka Gandhi* and *Olga Tellis* decisions illustrate purposive reading. Also associated is **Justice V.R. Krishna Iyer**, who often used the sociological approach to justice.

### 2.8 Realist School

**Basic position:** law is **what courts and officials actually do**, not what is written in rules. The focus is on **judicial behaviour**, the psychological, social and personal influences on decision, and on law in action. There are two strands.

**American Realism:**

- **Oliver Wendell Holmes Jr.** (1841 to 1935): a forerunner; *The Common Law* (1881) ('The life of the law has not been logic: it has been experience') and **'The Path of the Law'** (1897), which proposed the **'bad man' theory**: 'If you want to know the law and nothing else, you must look at it as a bad man, who cares only for the material consequences which such knowledge enables him to predict.' Law is a **prediction of the incidence of public force through the courts**.

- **John Chipman Gray:** *The Nature and Sources of the Law* (1909): law is what the judges declare; statutes are only sources of law, because they become law only when interpreted by judges.

- **Karl Llewellyn** (1893 to 1962): *The Bramble Bush* (1930, lectures to law students), *The Common Law Tradition: Deciding Appeals* (1960); the principal drafter of the **Uniform Commercial Code**. He stressed **'law in action'**, the study of what officials do, and distinguished 'paper rules' from 'real rules'. He was a **rule-sceptic**. He listed the **law-jobs** (such as conflict resolution, channelling conduct and allocating authority).

- **Jerome Frank** (1889 to 1957): *Law and the Modern Mind* (1930), *Courts on Trial* (1949). A **fact-sceptic** (the uncertainty of law lies mainly in the uncertainty of facts and in the personality of the judge and the jury), who held that legal certainty is a **myth (the 'basic legal myth')**. He criticised the idea that law can be fully predictable (what he called the 'quest for certainty' or 'father-substitute').

- **Rule scepticism** (Llewellyn) versus **fact scepticism** (Frank) is a common test point.

**Scandinavian Realism:**

- **Axel Hagerstrom** (Uppsala): rejected metaphysical concepts like 'duty' and 'right' as **meaningless**, 'non-natural' facts.

- **Karl Olivecrona:** *Law as Fact* (1939): law is a set of 'independent imperatives'; the binding force of law is a psychological illusion.

- **Vilhelm Lundstedt:** *Legal Thinking Revised* (1956): *social welfare* method, rejecting the idea of justice.

- **Alf Ross:** *On Law and Justice* (1953): law is the body of rules *actually effective* in the minds of judges, which they feel socially bound to follow; **'Tu-tu'** (an invented word to show that a legal concept like 'ownership' is an empty word used merely for convenience of speech).

**Criticisms:** exaggerates the unpredictability of law; ignores normative element; judges do follow rules in most cases; cannot explain how to distinguish a rule from a habit; and is a theory *about adjudication*, not about the whole of law.

### 2.9 Marxist Jurisprudence

- **Karl Marx and Friedrich Engels:** law belongs to the **superstructure** that rests on the **economic base** (the relations of production). In a class society, law is an instrument of class domination, reflecting the interests of the ruling class. Bourgeois law is the will of the bourgeois class 'made into a law for all'.

- **Withering away of the state and law:** with a classless communist society, there will be no need for law and coercive state.

- **Lenin:** law is the will of the ruling class; during the transition, the dictatorship of the proletariat needs 'proletarian' law.

- **E.B. Pashukanis** (*General Theory of Law and Marxism*, 1924): the **commodity-exchange theory**, according to which the legal form arises from the relations of commodity exchange; thus legal form will disappear with capitalism. He was executed in the Stalinist purges.

- **A.Y. Vyshinsky:** the Stalinist view that law is the *aggregate of rules of conduct expressing the will of the ruling class*, established by legislation, and backed by the coercive force of the State. He rejected the Pashukanis approach.

- **Antonio Gramsci:** **hegemony**; law helps to secure the consent of the governed.

- **Criticisms:** economic determinism; neglect of the autonomy of law; the rule of law as a real constraint; its predictions of the withering away of law were falsified by the experience of Soviet states.

- **Relevance in India:** Marxist ideas on law and development, class, and labour influence the thinking of writers like **Upendra Baxi** and the idea of social justice in the Constitution.

### 2.10 Economic Analysis of Law (Law and Economics)

- **Ronald Coase:** *The Problem of Social Cost* (1960), the **Coase theorem**: if transaction costs are zero, parties will bargain to an efficient outcome regardless of the initial allocation of legal entitlements; if transaction costs are positive, the law should allocate rights to minimise costs.

- **Richard Posner:** *Economic Analysis of Law* (1973): the common law is efficient; the goal of law is **wealth maximisation**.

- **Guido Calabresi:** *The Costs of Accidents* (1970); **property rules and liability rules** (with Melamed, 1972, 'One View of the Cathedral').

- The approach is criticised for treating efficiency as the only value and for the 'ability to pay' problem (as in Dworkin's critique of wealth maximisation).

### 2.11 Indian Jurisprudence: Ancient and Modern

- **Dharma** is the central concept: the cosmic and social order, righteous conduct, duty. It is a **wider concept than law**. The sources of Dharma according to **Manu** (*Manusmriti*) are the **Vedas (Sruti), Smriti, the conduct of the virtuous (Sadachara) and one's own conscience (Atmatushti)**.

- **Kautilya's Arthashastra** names the four sources (or legs) of law: **Dharma (sacred law), Vyavahara (evidence or transactions), Charitra (history or custom) and Rajasasana (edict of the king)**. In case of conflict, each later one overrides the earlier, with Rajasasana having the final authority.

- **Danda (punishment)** is essential for the maintenance of order; the king is *under the law* (Dharma).

- **Mimamsa** (Jaimini) contributed principles of interpretation, which are still used in Hindu law.

- **Commentaries:** *Mitakshara* (Vijnaneshwara; birth-right in the joint family property) and *Dayabhaga* (Jimutavahana; Bengal school, no birth right).

- **Modern Indian thinkers:** **Mahatma Gandhi** (truth, non-violence, *Satyagraha* and trusteeship), **B.R. Ambedkar** (constitutional morality, social democracy), **Jawaharlal Nehru**, **Upendra Baxi** (*The Crisis of the Indian Legal System*, human rights, Bhopal).

## Chapter 3: Law and Morality

### 3.1 The Core Question and the Vocabulary

The central question is whether there is a **necessary connection** between law and morality and, a separate question, whether the **law should enforce morality**. Examiners test (a) which school takes which view, (b) the Hart–Devlin and Hart–Fuller debates, and (c) Indian cases in which 'constitutional morality' was set against 'public' or 'social' morality.

**Key terms:**

- **Positive (or conventional) morality:** the moral beliefs actually held by a society.

- **Critical (or ideal) morality:** the moral principles by which social practices, including law, can be criticised (Hart's terms).

- **Legal moralism:** the view that conduct may be legally prohibited simply because it is immoral (Devlin).

- **Legal paternalism:** the law may restrict a person's liberty for the person's own good (for example seat-belt laws).

- **Harm principle:** the law may restrict liberty only to prevent harm to others (Mill).

- **Offence principle:** the law may prohibit conduct that is seriously offensive to others even if not harmful (Joel Feinberg, *The Moral Limits of the Criminal Law*, four volumes, 1984 to 1988: *Harm to Others, Offense to Others, Harm to Self, Harmless Wrongdoing*).

- **Constitutional morality:** adherence to the core values of the Constitution (liberty, equality, dignity, fraternity, rule of law) as against popular or majoritarian morality.

### 3.2 Differences Between Law and Morality (Concept Table)

| Basis        | Law                                                                | Morality                                        |
|--------------|--------------------------------------------------------------------|-------------------------------------------------|
| Source       | Made by the State or recognised by it (statute, precedent, custom) | Conscience, religion, society, reason           |
| Sanction     | External, organised, coercive (fine, imprisonment)                 | Internal (guilt) and social disapproval         |
| Concern      | Mainly external conduct; mental state only as element (mens rea)   | Includes inner motives, intentions and thoughts |
| Certainty    | Relatively definite and written                                    | Often vague and varying                         |
| Universality | Differs between states; territorial                                | Claimed to be universal (in natural law view)   |
| Change       | Changes by formal process                                          | Changes slowly by social evolution              |
| Scope        | Does not cover all moral duties (such as ingratitude)              | Wider; includes duties not enforced by law      |
| Enforcement  | Courts and police                                                  | Conscience and public opinion                   |

**Where they overlap:** crimes such as murder, theft and fraud; duty of care in tort; good faith in contract; maintenance and child-protection laws. **Where law is morally neutral:** traffic rules, limitation periods, company formalities.

### 3.3 Views of the Schools on Law and Morality

- **Natural law:** a **necessary connection**. An unjust law lacks full legal status (Augustine, Aquinas, Radbruch, Fuller, Finnis).

- **Analytical positivism:** **separation thesis**. Austin: 'The existence of law is one thing; its merit or demerit is another.' Hart accepts a *minimum content of natural law* but insists on conceptual separation. Raz: strict source thesis.

- **Kelsen:** the validity of law is independent of morality; justice is an irrational ideal outside legal science.

- **Historical school:** law and morality both come from the Volksgeist.

- **Sociological school:** law serves social interests which include morals (Pound listed 'general morals' as a social interest).

- **Realists:** morality matters only in so far as it influences judges.

- **Marxists:** both are ideological reflections of the economic order.

- **Dworkin:** law *includes* moral principles; the best interpretation of legal practice involves moral judgment (law as integrity).

- **Fuller:** the **internal morality of law** (the eight desiderata) is the necessary morality of legality, and law has a moral dimension essential to its existence.

- **Hart's description:** the existence of law is a matter of fact; but it is the minimum content of natural law, the union of rules and the **internal point of view** that links law to morality in practice.

### 3.4 The Hart–Fuller Debate (1958)

See Topic 2.2 for the details of the informer case. In outline:

- Hart, 'Positivism and the Separation of Law and Morals' (*Harvard Law Review*, 1958): even an evil rule may be law; the right response is to say 'this is law, but too evil to be obeyed', so as to keep moral criticism honest.

- Fuller, 'Positivism and Fidelity to Law: A Reply to Professor Hart' (1958): the **internal morality** of law; the idea of law cannot be separated from the idea of fidelity to law, and a system of extreme injustice cannot be law.

- **Outcome:** both views remain influential; many cite the **Radbruch formula** as a middle position.

### 3.5 The Hart–Devlin Debate: Should Law Enforce Morality?

**Background: the Wolfenden Report (1957).** The Committee on Homosexual Offences and Prostitution, chaired by **Sir John Wolfenden**, recommended that **homosexual behaviour between consenting adults in private should no longer be a criminal offence**. It said the function of criminal law is to preserve public order and protect citizens from what is offensive or injurious, and 'there must remain a realm of private morality and immorality which is, in brief and crude terms, not the law's business.' It was implemented by the **Sexual Offences Act 1967** (England and Wales).

**Lord Patrick Devlin** (*The Enforcement of Morals*, the Maccabaean Lecture, 1959):

- Society is held together by a **shared morality**; a society has a right to protect itself from threats, including **moral** threats, just as against treason. The suppression of vice is as much the law's business as the suppression of subversive activities.

- **Disintegration thesis:** if society's shared morality is flouted, society may disintegrate; hence the law may punish immorality as such.

- **The test of the reasonable man** (the 'man in the jury box' or 'man on the Clapham omnibus'): the limits of tolerance are those of the *right-minded person*. The marks of a public morality are '**intolerance, indignation and disgust**'. Where there is a real feeling of 'reprobation', the law may act, but it must always have regard for the privacy of the individual, and tolerate as much as is consistent with the integrity of society.

**H.L.A. Hart** (*Law, Liberty and Morality*, 1963; *The Morality of the Criminal Law*, 1965):

- **Rejects the disintegration thesis** as lacking empirical evidence: society does not disintegrate because of changes in sexual morality; the idea that a change in morality is like treason is incorrect.

- Distinguishes **positive morality** from **critical morality**. Devlin's argument, Hart says, confuses *social acceptance* with *moral rightness*. Mere moral outrage, disgust and intolerance cannot justify the use of criminal law.

- Supports **Mill's harm principle**, while recognising certain justifiable cases of **paternalism** (for example, to protect people from exploitation or when consent is unreal).

- Called Devlin's view 'a moral populism', and said that to enforce morality merely because it is shared is an unjustified form of moral tyranny of the majority.

- Also noted that the *Wolfenden* position was consistent with **utilitarian liberalism**.

**Other contributions:**

- **J.S. Mill, *On Liberty* (1859):** the only purpose for which power can be rightfully exercised over any member of a civilised community, against his will, is to **prevent harm to others**. His own good, either physical or moral, is not a sufficient warrant.

- **James Fitzjames Stephen** (*Liberty, Equality, Fraternity*, 1873): an early critic of Mill who argued that law may enforce morality; Devlin's views are close to his.

- **Ronald Dworkin** ('Lord Devlin and the Enforcement of Morals', *Yale Law Journal*, 1966) criticised Devlin for treating *popular reaction* as the same as *moral conviction*.

**Case law (English and American) often used as illustrations:**

- *Shaw v DPP* (1962): the House of Lords recognised a common-law offence of **conspiracy to corrupt public morals** (the 'Ladies' Directory' case), accepted by commentators as Devlin's view in action.

- *Knuller v DPP* (1973): the Lords reaffirmed the offence of outraging public decency.

- *R v Brown* (1993): the Lords upheld convictions for consensual sadomasochistic acts (a paternalism and public-morality case); the **Mill and Hart** view would have favoured the defendants.

- *Bowers v Hardwick* (1986, US Supreme Court) upheld a Georgia sodomy law, but this was **overruled by *Lawrence v Texas* (2003)**, which emphasised liberty and privacy.

- *Obergefell v Hodges* (2015, US) recognised same-sex marriage.

### 3.6 Indian Cases on Law, Morality and Constitutional Morality (Highest Recent Yield)

The Indian Supreme Court's recent judgments have been drawn on repeatedly in questions. Learn the **name, year, and the one-line holding**.

**(a) Homosexuality and Section 377 IPC**

- *Naz Foundation v Government of NCT of Delhi* (2009): the Delhi High Court (A.P. Shah CJ) read down Section 377 IPC to exclude consensual adult sexual acts in private; it relied on **constitutional morality** and said popular morality or public disapproval cannot justify limits on fundamental rights.

- *Suresh Kumar Koushal v Naz Foundation* (2013): the Supreme Court reversed that decision, holding that the question was for Parliament and that LGBT persons were a 'miniscule fraction'.

- *National Legal Services Authority (NALSA) v Union of India* (2014): recognised the **right of transgender persons to self-identify** their gender and held that they are entitled to fundamental rights, and are a 'third gender'.

- *K.S. Puttaswamy v Union of India* (2017): a nine-judge bench unanimously held that **privacy is a fundamental right** under Article 21 (and Part III as a whole), overruling *M.P. Sharma* (1954) and *Kharak Singh* (1962) in part.

- *Navtej Singh Johar v Union of India* (2018): a five-judge Constitution Bench **read down Section 377** and decriminalised consensual same-sex acts between adults, overruling *Koushal*. It emphasised **constitutional morality over social morality**, dignity, and the transformative character of the Constitution. Bestiality and non-consensual acts remain punishable.

- **Note:** the **Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita, 2023** (in force from 1 July 2024) has replaced the IPC and has no counterpart of Section 377; check the statutory mapping if the exam gives section-numbered questions.

**(b) Adultery:** *Joseph Shine v Union of India* (2018): Section 497 IPC (adultery) was struck down as violating Articles 14, 15 and 21; it treated a wife as the property of the husband and was based on outdated morality.

**(c) Religion, gender and constitutional morality**

- *Indian Young Lawyers Association v State of Kerala (Sabarimala)* (2018): by a 4:1 majority, the Court held the exclusion of women of menstruating age from the Sabarimala temple violated Articles 14, 15, 17 and 25; **constitutional morality** must prevail over religious practices that violate dignity. Justice Indu Malhotra dissented, saying courts should not interfere with essential religious practices.

- *Kantaru Rajeevaru v Indian Young Lawyers Association* (2020): review petitions were referred to a **larger bench** to consider the broader questions of the scope of religious freedom and essential religious practices. (Check the latest status of the nine-judge bench.)

- *Shayara Bano v Union of India* (2017): by 3:2 the Court struck down instant **triple talaq** (talaq-e-biddat) as arbitrary and unconstitutional.

**(d) Obscenity and public morality (Section 292 IPC, now Section 294 BNS)**

- *Ranjit D. Udeshi v State of Maharashtra* (1965): adopted the English **Hicklin test** (*R v Hicklin*, 1868), asking whether the material tends to deprave and corrupt those whose minds are open to such immoral influences.

- *K.A. Abbas v Union of India* (1970): on censorship of films, upheld pre-censorship as a reasonable restriction.

- *Bobby Art International v Om Pal Singh Hoon* (1996): *Bandit Queen*; the context and the message of the film matter.

- *Aveek Sarkar v State of West Bengal* (2014): rejected the Hicklin test and adopted the **'community standards' test** (from the US *Roth* approach), holding that a picture of Boris Becker with his fiancee, published for an anti-racism message, was not obscene.

**(e) Dignity, death and autonomy**

- *Aruna Shanbaug v Union of India* (2011): allowed **passive euthanasia** under judicial supervision.

- *Common Cause v Union of India* (2018): recognised the **right to die with dignity** under Article 21 and allowed 'living wills' (advance directives) with safeguards.

**(f) Constitutional morality in other contexts:** *Manoj Narula v Union of India* (2014) on appointment of tainted persons as ministers, where the Court explained constitutional morality as conformity with the norms of the Constitution; the concept was invoked by **Dr B.R. Ambedkar** in the Constituent Assembly (borrowing from the historian **George Grote**) as the 'effective co-ordination between conflicting interests'; it is not a natural sentiment and must be cultivated.

### 3.7 Law, Morality and Other Normative Orders

- **Law and religion:** historically fused (Dharma, Sharia, canon law); modern states separate them, though personal laws persist (Article 25 and the debate on the **Uniform Civil Code** under Article 44).

- **Law and ethics of profession:** Bar Council rules on professional misconduct.

- **Law and justice:** justice is a moral value that law aims to achieve; for Aristotle, justice includes *distributive* and *corrective* aspects; for Kelsen, justice is outside the 'pure' science; for Rawls, justice is 'the first virtue of social institutions'.

- **Civil disobedience** (Thoreau; Gandhi's *Satyagraha*; Rawls, Dworkin): the claim that an unjust law may justify non-violent, public, conscientious breach.

## Chapter 4: Concept of Rights and Duties

### 4.1 Meaning and Definitions of a Legal Right

A **legal right** is an interest or claim that the law recognises and protects, and the violation of which is a legal wrong, while the respect for which is a legal duty. Unlike a mere moral claim, a legal right can be enforced by the courts.

| Jurist               | Definition                                                                                                                                                                         |
|----------------------|------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------|
| Salmond              | An interest recognised and protected by a rule of right; any interest respect for which is a duty and the disregard of which is a wrong                                            |
| Austin               | A faculty which resides in a determinate party or parties by virtue of a given law, and which avails against a party or parties other than the party or parties in whom it resides |
| Holland              | A capacity residing in one man of controlling, with the assistance of the State, the actions of others                                                                             |
| Gray                 | A power which a man has to make a person or persons do or refrain from doing something, so far as the power is derived from the law                                                |
| Ihering              | A legally protected interest                                                                                                                                                       |
| Bentham              | Rights are the creatures of law and arise from it; there is no right without a corresponding duty                                                                                  |
| Kant, Hegel, Savigny | A right is the power or sphere of free will                                                                                                                                        |
| Pound                | An interest recognised by law, whether an individual, public or social interest                                                                                                    |
| Hohfeld              | A right strictly is a *claim*; correlatives are duties                                                                                                                             |

### 4.2 Theories of Rights

- **Will (choice) theory:** a right is a protected sphere of **individual will or choice**, such that the right-holder has *control* over the corresponding duty (for example, to waive or enforce it). Associated with **Kant, Hegel, Savigny, Puchta, Windscheid**; in modern times **H.L.A. Hart** (choice theory). *Criticism:* it cannot explain the rights of infants, mentally ill persons, and unborn children, or inalienable rights.

- **Interest (benefit) theory:** a right is a **legally protected interest**. Associated with **Ihering, Bentham, Salmond, Austin (partly) and Joseph Raz** ('X has a right if X's well-being is a sufficient reason for holding another to be under a duty'; Raz in *The Morality of Freedom*). *Criticism:* third-party beneficiaries have interests but not rights; trust beneficiaries.

- **Hybrid theories:** MacCormick and others combine will and interest.

- **Rights as trumps (Dworkin):** individual rights override collective goals.

- **Social function theory (Duguit):** rights are replaced by social functions and duties.

- **Kelsen:** a right is a **reflex of a duty**; the 'right' is the legal duty seen from the standpoint of the person entitled.

### 4.3 Elements of a Legal Right (Salmond)

1.  **The subject** or owner of the right (the person who has the benefit).

2.  **The person of incidence**, or the person against whom the right is available (the person bound by the duty).

3.  **The content of the right**, or the act or forbearance which the person bound must do or abstain from doing.

4.  **The object of the right**, the thing or subject matter over which the right is exercised.

5.  **The title of the right**, the facts or events by which the right is vested in the owner (for example, a contract or purchase).

### 4.4 Hohfeld's Analysis of Fundamental Legal Conceptions

**Wesley Newcomb Hohfeld** (American jurist, 1879 to 1918) published *Fundamental Legal Conceptions as Applied in Judicial Reasoning* (articles of 1913 and 1917, published as a book in 1919). He argued that the term 'right' is used loosely and in at least four different senses. He identified **eight jural conceptions** in two sets of four: jural **opposites** and jural **correlatives**.

| Jural opposites            |     | Jural correlatives    |
|----------------------------|-----|-----------------------|
| Right (claim) ↔ No-right   |     | Right ↔ Duty          |
| Privilege (liberty) ↔ Duty |     | Privilege ↔ No-right  |
| Power ↔ Disability         |     | Power ↔ Liability     |
| Immunity ↔ Liability       |     | Immunity ↔ Disability |

**Definitions:**

- **Right (claim, right in the strict sense):** a claim that another **must** do or not do something. Correlative: **duty**. (If A has a right that B not trespass on his land, B has a duty not to trespass.)

- **Privilege (liberty):** the freedom of the holder to do something *without being under a duty not to*. Correlative: **no-right** (others cannot claim that he should not do it). Opposite: **duty**.

- **Power:** the ability of a person, by his own act, to **change legal relations** (his own or another's). Examples: the power to make a will or contract, or to sell property. Correlative: **liability**. Opposite: **disability**.

- **Immunity:** freedom from the power of another to change one's legal position. Correlative: **disability** (in the other). Opposite: **liability**. (Example: the immunity of diplomats from jurisdiction; judges' immunity.)

- **Liability:** the position of being subject to another's power to change one's legal relations (for example, a debtor's liability to be sued; a mortgagor's liability to a power of sale).

- **Disability:** the absence of a power (for example, a minor's incapacity to make a binding contract).

- **Duty:** being legally bound to act or forbear. **No-right:** the absence of a claim.

**Key exam trap:** **correlatives** are two sides of the *same* relation and exist *in different persons* (right in A is duty in B); **opposites** are the *negation* of a conception *in the same person* (a person who has a right does not have a no-right). Hohfeld called the four conceptions of right, privilege, power and immunity the **'advantages'**, and the other four the **'disadvantages'**.

### 4.5 Kinds of Rights (Salmond and Others)

- **Perfect and imperfect rights:** a perfect right is one that is both recognised and enforceable (for example, the right to recover a debt); an imperfect right is recognised but not enforceable (for example, a time-barred debt).

- **Positive and negative rights:** a positive right corresponds to a duty to *do* something (to pay a debt); a negative right corresponds to a duty to *forbear* (not to trespass).

- **Real rights (*in rem*) and personal rights (*in personam*):** a right *in rem* is available against the world at large (the right of ownership, the right to reputation); a right *in personam* is available against a determinate person or persons (contractual rights). Salmond notes that **rights in rem are generally negative** and **rights in personam generally positive**, but there are exceptions.

- **Proprietary and personal rights:** proprietary rights relate to a person's estate or property and are valuable (ownership, debts, shares), whereas personal rights relate to a person's status or condition (right to liberty, reputation, family rights).

- **Rights *in re propria* and rights *in re aliena*:** the right of ownership over one's own property, or **jura in re propria**, and encumbrances on the property of another, **jura in re aliena**. The four categories of **encumbrances** are **leases, servitudes (easements), securities (mortgages, pledges and liens) and trusts**.

- **Principal and accessory rights:** an accessory right is subordinate to a principal one (for example, a mortgage securing a debt).

- **Vested and contingent rights:** a vested right is fully acquired, while a contingent right depends on a future event.

- **Primary (antecedent) and secondary (sanctioning or remedial) rights:** a primary right exists independently of any wrong (the right to bodily integrity); a secondary right arises from a violation (the right to damages).

- **Legal and equitable rights; public and private rights; municipal and international rights; rights against the State.**

- **Human rights and fundamental rights:** a human right is a claim recognised by international law and moral theory, and a fundamental right is a human right incorporated into the Constitution and enforceable under **Articles 32 and 226**.

**Salmond's and Austin's views on 'rights without correlative duties':** Austin said that there are **absolute duties** (those not correlative to any right), such as duties owed to oneself, to God, to the lower animals and to the public. Salmond argues that *every right has a duty*, but not every duty has a right (absolute duties exist but are rights of the State). Hohfeld says the correlative of right is duty, but privileges have **no-right** as correlatives.

**Key maxims:** *ubi jus ibi remedium* (where there is a right there is a remedy); *damnum sine injuria* (loss without legal injury is not actionable, *Gloucester Grammar School case*, 1410; *Bradford Corporation v Pickles*, 1895); *injuria sine damno* (violation of right without loss is actionable, *Ashby v White*, 1703; the right to vote).

### 4.6 Duties

**Meaning:** a duty is an act of forbearance that the law obliges a person to perform in the interest of another, and the neglect of which is a wrong. **Salmond:** duty is an obligatory act, the opposite of which is a wrong. **Austin:** the idea of duty is *prior* to the idea of right, and the duty is the basic concept (the right is only the other side). **Kelsen:** the right is a reflex of the duty. **Duguit:** only duties exist, as everyone has the duty to perform a social function.

**Kinds of duties:**

- **Positive and negative duties.**

- **Primary (or antecedent) and secondary (or sanctioning) duties:** the duty to pay compensation after a wrong is secondary.

- **Absolute and relative duties:** relative duties correspond to rights in a determinate person; absolute duties have no corresponding right in an individual (for example, duties towards the public or the State).

- **Duties in rem and in personam.**

- **Legal and moral duties.**

- **Penal and remedial (civil) duties.**

**Fundamental duties in India (Part IV-A, Article 51A):**

- Introduced by the **Constitution (Forty-second Amendment) Act, 1976**, on the recommendation of the **Swaran Singh Committee**, and inspired by the **USSR Constitution**.

- Originally **ten** duties; an eleventh (duty of a parent or guardian to provide opportunities for education to a child between the ages of six and fourteen) was added by the **Eighty-sixth Amendment Act, 2002**.

- They include respect for the Constitution, the national flag and anthem; cherishing the ideals of the freedom struggle; upholding sovereignty and integrity; defending the country; promoting harmony and renouncing practices derogatory to the dignity of women; preserving the composite culture; protecting the natural environment; developing the scientific temper and humanism; safeguarding public property and abjuring violence; and striving for excellence.

- **Not directly enforceable** by writ, but useful in interpreting laws and in determining the reasonableness of restrictions on rights. See *Rural Litigation and Entitlement Kendra v State of UP* (1985); *AIIMS Students' Union v AIIMS* (2002) (fundamental duties are not enforceable by writ but the court can use them to interpret); and *Bijoe Emmanuel v State of Kerala* (1986) (national anthem and the right not to sing).

**Duties of the State (in the Constitution):** the **Directive Principles** (Part IV) impose duties on the State, but are non-justiciable (Article 37). The Court has expanded fundamental rights to read in many directive principles (for example, *Unni Krishnan v State of AP*, 1993, on the right to education, which led to **Article 21A** by the 86th Amendment).

### 4.7 Right–Duty Correlation: Summary

- Most theorists (Salmond, Holland, Hohfeld, Bentham) treat right and duty as **correlatives**: no right without a duty in someone else.

- Austin and Kelsen see duty as the primary notion.

- Duguit and the sociological school see duty (social function) as the basic concept.

- Hohfeld's contribution is to show that the **word 'right'** hides *four* different relations: claim, liberty, power and immunity.

### 4.8 Indian Rights Jurisprudence: Landmark Cases for Recent Trends

- *Kharak Singh v State of UP* (1962) and *Gobind v State of MP* (1975): early recognition of privacy.

- *Maneka Gandhi v Union of India* (1978): the 'procedure established by law' in Article 21 must be **just, fair and reasonable**; the 'golden triangle' of Articles 14, 19 and 21.

- *Olga Tellis v Bombay Municipal Corporation* (1985): the **right to livelihood** is part of the right to life.

- *Francis Coralie Mullin v Administrator, Delhi* (1981): the right to live with human dignity.

- *Vishaka v State of Rajasthan* (1997): guidelines on sexual harassment at the workplace, drawing on international conventions (CEDAW).

- *K.S. Puttaswamy v Union of India* (2017): privacy; *Puttaswamy (Aadhaar)* (2018): limited validity of Aadhaar.

- *Mohini Jain v State of Karnataka* (1992) and *Unni Krishnan* (1993): the right to education; **Right of Children to Free and Compulsory Education Act, 2009**.

- **Property:** the right to property was removed from fundamental rights by the **44th Amendment (1978)** and made a constitutional right under **Article 300A**.

- *PUCL v Union of India* (2001): the right to food (Article 21).

- *Subhash Kumar v State of Bihar* (1991) and *M.C. Mehta* cases: the right to a wholesome environment.

## Chapter 5: Legal Personality

### 5.1 Meaning of 'Person' and Legal Personality

The word *person* comes from the Latin **persona**, the mask worn by actors in the Roman theatre, hence a role or character played in the legal drama. In law, a **person** is any being, real or imaginary, to which the law attributes **legal rights and duties**. **Legal personality** is the capacity to be the holder of rights and duties.

- **Salmond:** a person is any being whom the law regards as capable of rights or duties. Any being that is so capable is a person, whether a human being or not, and no being that is not so capable is a person, even though he be a man.

- **John Chipman Gray:** a person is an entity to which rights and duties may be attributed.

- **Kelsen:** the legal person is not a real entity; it is the *personification of a unity of legal norms* (a complex of rights and duties), an auxiliary concept of legal thinking.

- **Paton:** legal personality is a technique to attribute rights and duties to a subject.

**Two classes of persons:**

- **Natural persons:** human beings. Not every human being has been a legal person in history (slaves in Roman law were things, not persons, and the 'civil death' of convicts existed in old English law). Today all human beings are persons, but **capacity** may vary (minors, persons of unsound mind).

- **Legal (juristic, artificial) persons:** entities other than human beings which the law treats as persons: corporations, institutions, funds, idols, and, in some recent decisions, rivers and animals.

### 5.2 Status of Natural Persons in Special Situations

**The unborn child (nasciturus):**

- Salmond says an unborn child is not a person in the full sense, but the law protects the child's **contingent rights**, for example, in property and inheritance. A child *en ventre sa mere* is regarded as born for the purposes of **beneficial** rights, if it is subsequently born alive.

- Indian law: **Section 13 of the Transfer of Property Act, 1882** (a transfer for the benefit of an unborn person, with a prior life interest in a living person, is valid, subject to conditions); **Section 20 of the Hindu Succession Act, 1956** (a child in the womb who is later born alive has the same right of inheritance as if born before the death of the intestate).

- Tort: *Montreal Tramways v Leveille* (1933, Supreme Court of Canada) and the **Congenital Disabilities (Civil Liability) Act 1976** (UK) allowed claims for pre-natal injury once the child was born alive.

- Salmond's classic approach is that the unborn child's rights are **suspended** until birth; an unborn child cannot sue in its own right but its interests can be protected by trustees or guardians.

**The dead person:**

- Salmond and Austin say that a dead man has **no legal personality**, though the law respects wishes expressed in a will (the 'posthumous' will); the dead have an interest in **reputation** (Explanation 1 to the defamation provision allows prosecution if the imputation would harm the reputation of the deceased if he were living and hurts the feelings of his family) and in **decent burial**.

- *Pt. Parmanand Katara v Union of India* (1989): the right to dignity and fair treatment under Article 21 extends not only to a living man but also to his body **after death**.

- *Ashray Adhikar Abhiyan v Union of India* (2002): the dignity of the dead extends to the homeless dead, who are entitled to a decent funeral.

**Minors, persons of unsound mind, convicts, alien enemies:** have personality but **limited capacity** (see the Indian Contract Act, 1872, Section 11, and the Indian Majority Act, 1875: majority at 18). A **minor's agreement is void ab initio** (*Mohori Bibee v Dharmodas Ghose*, 1903, Privy Council).

### 5.3 Kinds of Legal Persons

**(a) Corporations**

- **Corporation aggregate:** a body of persons united to form a single legal person (a company, a registered society, a university or a municipal body). The members change but the corporation continues, with **perpetual succession**.

- **Corporation sole:** a series of successive individuals holding an *office*, treated as a single person (the Crown, a bishop, the President of India, the Administrator-General, the Official Trustee, the Public Trustee). The office, not the individual, is the person.

- **Salmond's conception:** a corporation is a *group or series of persons* which is treated by law as a person.

**(b) Institutions:** a charitable fund, a hospital, a church or a temple endowed with property may be treated as a legal person, for example the **idol** in Hindu law.

**(c) Funds and estates:** a **trust fund**, the estate of a **deceased person** or a **bankrupt's estate** may be treated as juristic person for purposes of administration (in India, the **trust is not a person**; the trustee holds the property).

**(d) The State:** the Union and the States are juristic persons capable of suing and being sued (**Article 300**). Local authorities and statutory corporations are also persons (for example, LIC, SBI, Municipal Corporations).

**(e) Entities that are *not* legal persons in India:** a **partnership firm** (it is not a separate legal entity under the Partnership Act, 1932, although it is recognised as a unit under some statutes, such as Income Tax), a **Hindu undivided family** (HUF), a **trust**, and an **unregistered association**. By contrast, a **company** (Companies Act, 2013; **Section 9** deals with the effect of registration: the company is a body corporate with perpetual succession and a common seal) and a **limited liability partnership** (LLP Act, 2008, Section 3: an LLP is a body corporate and a legal entity separate from its partners) are legal persons.

### 5.4 Theories of Corporate Personality (Essential for MCQs)

| Theory                                      | Main proponents                                            | Core idea                                                                                                                                      |
|---------------------------------------------|------------------------------------------------------------|------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------|
| Fiction theory                              | Savigny, Salmond, Pope Innocent IV (*persona ficta*), Coke | Only human beings are real persons; the corporation is an artificial person created by the law through a legal fiction                         |
| Concession (grant) theory                   | Savigny (with fiction), Dicey, Austin                      | A corporation exists only because the State grants it recognition; the State may withdraw it                                                   |
| Realist (organic, group-personality) theory | Gierke, Maitland, Zitelmann                                | The group is a real entity with a real will of its own, existing before and independently of State recognition; the State only *recognises* it |
| Bracket (symbolist) theory                  | Ihering, Hohfeld, Holland                                  | The corporation is a convenient bracket for the real persons (members) behind it; only human beings have rights                                |
| Purpose (Zweckvermogen) theory              | Brinz, Bekker                                              | There is no person, only property devoted to a purpose; the rights belong to nobody but the assets are bound to a specific purpose             |
| Kelsen's normative theory                   | Kelsen                                                     | The legal person is merely the personification of a set of norms; there is no difference between natural and juristic persons                  |
| Hohfeldian/Procedural device                | Hohfeld, Frederick Hallis (later writers)                  | Corporate personality is only a *technical device* for convenience of legal handling                                                           |

*(Textbooks differ somewhat on exact labels, but for exam purposes: **Fiction: Savigny/Salmond; Concession: Savigny/Dicey; Realist: Gierke/Maitland; Bracket: Ihering; Purpose: Brinz; Normative: Kelsen**.)*

**Key points:**

- **Gierke** (*Das deutsche Genossenschaftsrecht*) argued that a group has a *Gesamtperson* with its own will; **F.W. Maitland** translated part of his work and popularised it in England. Realist theory supports the claim that **associations (such as trade unions) have rights independent of the State's grant**, and was used in discussing the *Taff Vale* case (1901).

- **Salmond's view:** for the sake of convenience law treats the corporate body as a person, even though it is only a fiction; the *rights and liabilities belong to the corporation*, which are quite distinct from those of its members.

- **Duguit** denies the reality of personality of corporations, but he did not espouse the fiction approach either: he thought the idea of 'person' was *metaphysical*, and replaced it by 'social function'.

### 5.5 Characteristics of a Corporate Person

- **Separate legal entity** from its members: *Salomon v A. Salomon & Co Ltd* (1897, House of Lords): a company is a separate person, even if one man holds virtually all its shares, and the shareholder-creditor Salomon was not liable to the company's creditors.

- **Perpetual succession:** the death, insolvency or retirement of members does not affect the existence of the company.

- **Common seal and capacity to sue and be sued in its own name.**

- **Limited liability** (for companies limited by shares).

- **Separate property:** *Macaura v Northern Assurance Co* (1925): a shareholder has no insurable interest in company property.

- **Capacity limited by its objects** (the *ultra vires* doctrine, *Ashbury Railway Carriage Co v Riche*, 1875), though the Companies Act, 2013 has diluted its effect.

- **Rights of a corporation:** a company is a person under Article 14 but is **not a citizen** and so cannot claim Article 19 rights (*Tata Engineering and Locomotive Co v State of Bihar*, 1964). In *R.C. Cooper v Union of India* (1970) (the Bank Nationalisation case) and *Bennett Coleman v Union of India* (1973) the Court allowed shareholders to challenge the infringement of their own rights when the company's action also affected them.

### 5.6 Lifting (Piercing) the Corporate Veil

Courts sometimes disregard the separate personality of the company and look at the real persons behind it (**lifting the veil**).

**Grounds (judicial and statutory):**

- **Fraud or improper conduct:** *Gilford Motor Co v Horne* (1933) (a company formed to evade a restrictive covenant was a mere cloak); *Jones v Lipman* (1962) (a company was a 'device' or 'sham' to avoid specific performance).

- **Determination of character (enemy character):** *Daimler Co v Continental Tyre & Rubber Co* (1916): a company registered in England but controlled by enemy aliens was regarded as an enemy.

- **Tax evasion:** *Juggilal Kamlapat v CIT* (1969); *Dinshaw Maneckjee Petit* (1927) (a person who made himself a company to evade super-tax).

- **Agency or trust and Group enterprises:** *DHN Food Distributors v Tower Hamlets* (1976), later qualified, *Adams v Cape Industries* (1990), which narrowed veil lifting.

- **Protection of revenue and public interest:** *State of UP v Renusagar Power Co* (1988); *Life Insurance Corporation of India v Escorts Ltd* (1986) (the court may look behind the corporate form where necessary to see the real transaction); *Delhi Development Authority v Skipper Construction Co* (1996) (fraud; the court lifted the veil to reach the property of directors and their families).

- **Corporate groups and Foreign investment:** *Vodafone International Holdings BV v Union of India* (2012) (the Supreme Court respected separate corporate personality and looked at the 'substance' of the transaction as a whole).

- **Prest v Petrodel Resources** (2013, UK Supreme Court): distinguishes **concealment** from **evasion** principles; veil piercing is available only when a person is under an existing legal obligation or liability which he deliberately evades by interposing a company.

- **Statutory instances under the Companies Act, 2013:** Section 7(7) (incorporation by false information), Section 34 and 35 (misstatement in the prospectus), Section 339 (fraudulent conduct of business) and Section 447 (punishment for fraud), and Section 2(60) (officer in default). Also 'trading with fewer than the statutory minimum members' (earlier legislation).

**Alter ego:** *Bacha F. Guzdar v CIT* (1955): a shareholder is not the owner of the corporation's assets; the shareholder has only a right to a share of profit in the form of dividend.

### 5.7 Criminal Liability of Corporations

- A corporation has no body to imprison or a mind to be guilty, yet it may be liable for crimes under the **doctrine of identification** (the 'directing mind and will' of the company is the company). See *Lennard's Carrying Co v Asiatic Petroleum Co* (1915); *Tesco Supermarkets v Nattrass* (1972); *H.L. Bolton (Engineering) Co v T.J. Graham & Sons* (1957) (Lord Denning's 'brain and nerve centre' analogy).

- Indian position: *Standard Chartered Bank v Directorate of Enforcement* (2005): a company can be prosecuted even for offences that require mandatory imprisonment, and **fine** can be imposed in such cases. *Iridium India Telecom Ltd v Motorola Inc* (2011): a company can be liable for offences requiring *mens rea* (cheating), the knowledge of the directing officers being attributed to it. *Sunil Bharti Mittal v CBI* (2015): the **principle of attribution**; directors are not automatically liable for the company's acts and a company can be liable only under the 'alter ego' principle.

### 5.8 Legal Personality of Idols, Religious Books, Rivers, Animals (High-Yield Indian Material)

**Idols and religious texts**

- *Pramatha Nath Mullick v Pradyumna Kumar Mullick* (1925, Privy Council): a Hindu idol is a **juristic person** capable of holding property; the *shebait* acts as its manager and representative. The idol's ownership is *ideal*, the shebait's powers are real.

- *Yogendra Nath Naskar v Commissioner of Income Tax* (1969): the idol, a juristic person, is **assessable to income tax** as an 'individual'.

- *Shiromani Gurdwara Parbandhak Committee, Amritsar v Som Nath Dass* (2000): the **Guru Granth Sahib** is a **juristic person**; the Court recognised it as a legal entity capable of owning property.

- The *Ram Janmabhoomi* (Ayodhya) judgment (*M. Siddiq v Mahant Suresh Das*, 2019) also discussed the deity as a juristic person that can sue through a next friend; read its treatment of the deity and the 'asthan' carefully before relying on it in an exam.

- **A mosque** is not a juristic person in Indian law.

**Rivers and natural entities**

- *Mohd. Salim v State of Uttarakhand* (2017): the **Uttarakhand High Court** declared the rivers **Ganga and Yamuna** and their tributaries as **legal and living entities** with the status of a legal person, and appointed the Director NAMAMI Gange, the Chief Secretary and the Advocate General as 'persons in loco parentis' to protect them. The **Supreme Court stayed** the decision in *State of Uttarakhand v Mohd. Salim* (2017) on grounds of practical difficulties (for example, liability for floods).

- *Lalit Miglani v State of Uttarakhand* (2017): the same High Court extended this status to the **Himalayan glaciers (Gangotri and Yamunotri)**, streams, rivulets, lakes, air, meadows, dales, jungles, forests and springs.

- *Sukhna Lake case* (*Court on its Own Motion v Union Territory of Chandigarh*, 2020): the Punjab and Haryana High Court declared **Sukhna Lake** a 'living entity' with legal personality.

- **Foreign comparisons:** the **Whanganui River** in New Zealand (*Te Awa Tupua (Whanganui River Claims Settlement) Act, 2017*) and **Te Urewera Act 2014**; the **Constitution of Ecuador (2008)** recognises the **rights of nature** (*Pachamama*); **Bolivia**'s *Law of Mother Earth* (2010); and the Atrato River case of Colombia (2016).

**Animals**

- *Animal Welfare Board of India v A. Nagaraja* (2014): the **Jallikattu** case: the Supreme Court held that animals have a right to live with **dignity**, extended the idea of Article 21 'life' to animals, and listed the **'five freedoms'** of animals: freedom from hunger, thirst and malnutrition; from fear and distress; from physical and thermal discomfort; from pain, injury and disease; and freedom to express normal patterns of behaviour. It also invoked **Article 51A(g)** (compassion for living creatures) and the **Prevention of Cruelty to Animals Act, 1960**.

- *Narayan Dutt Bhatt v Union of India* (2018): the Uttarakhand High Court declared the **entire animal kingdom**, including avian and aquatic species, as **legal entities** having the status of legal persons, and citizens as their guardians.

- *Karnail Singh v State of Haryana* (2019): the Punjab and Haryana High Court declared the animal kingdom a legal entity (the Supreme Court stayed this in 2019).

- **Comparative note:** in the US, the New York Court of Appeals (2022, *Nonhuman Rights Project v Breheny*, the elephant 'Happy') refused to treat an elephant as a legal person for habeas corpus.

**Artificial intelligence and other emerging entities:** in 2017 the **European Parliament** debated a proposal on **'electronic personhood'** for sophisticated autonomous robots, which many criticised; Saudi Arabia symbolically granted 'citizenship' to the robot **Sophia** in 2017. Indian law has no concept of AI personhood. The debate centres on *liability gaps* and whether legal personality is a pragmatic device.

## Chapter 6: Concepts of Property, Ownership and Possession

### 6.1 Property: Meaning, Kinds and Theories

**Meaning in different senses (Salmond):**

1.  **All legal rights of a person**, proprietary and personal (this is the widest sense, used by Locke and in the older writers).

2.  **Proprietary rights only** (the right of ownership, and rights in the estate, as distinguished from personal rights, rights of status).

3.  **Material things** that are the subject-matter of proprietary rights (land and chattels).

4.  **Corporeal property** in the sense of material things (land or goods), and **incorporeal property** (rights such as copyright, patents, debts, shares, goodwill).

**Kinds of property:**

- **Movable and immovable property;** real and personal property (English law distinction based on the type of action available).

- **Corporeal (tangible) and incorporeal (intangible) property:** intangible includes **intellectual property** (patent, copyright, trademark, design), goodwill and choses in action.

- **Private, public, and common property.** Public trust doctrine: *M.C. Mehta v Kamal Nath* (1997): natural resources like the rivers, forests and the seashore are held by the State as a **trustee** for the public.

**Theories of property (important for conceptual and match-the-following questions):**

| Theory                  | Thinker                            | Idea                                                                                                                                         |
|-------------------------|------------------------------------|----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------|
| Labour (natural rights) | John Locke (*Two Treatises*)       | Property arises when a man mixes his labour with what is held in common, subject to the proviso that 'enough and as good' be left for others |
| Occupation              | Grotius, Kant (in a limited sense) | First occupation gives title                                                                                                                 |
| Utilitarian             | Bentham, Hume                      | Property is a creation of law; 'property and law are born together and die together'; it creates security of expectation                     |
| Personality             | Hegel, Kant                        | Property is the embodiment of personality; needed for self-development                                                                       |
| Marxist                 | Marx, Engels; Proudhon             | Private property is the basis of class exploitation; Proudhon: 'property is theft' (*What is Property?*, 1840)                               |
| Social function         | Duguit                             | Property is not a right but a social function                                                                                                |
| Economic efficiency     | Demsetz, Posner                    | Property rights internalise externalities and promote efficient use                                                                          |
| Historical              | Maine, Savigny                     | Property evolves from collective to individual ownership                                                                                     |
| Natural law             | Aquinas, Blackstone                | Property is derived from natural law; Blackstone called it 'that sole and despotic dominion' over external things                            |

**Indian constitutional position on property:**

- The **right to property** (Articles 19(1)(f) and 31) was a **fundamental right** until the **Constitution (Forty-fourth Amendment) Act, 1978** deleted these provisions and inserted **Article 300A** in Part XII: *no person shall be deprived of his property save by authority of law*. It is now a **constitutional right** (not a fundamental right) and a **human right** (*Vidya Devi v State of Himachal Pradesh*, 2020).

- **Article 31A, 31B and 31C** protect agrarian reform and the Directive Principles (Articles 39(b) and (c)); the **Ninth Schedule** (inserted by the First Amendment, 1951) immunises listed laws, but *I.R. Coelho v State of Tamil Nadu* (2007) held that laws placed in the Ninth Schedule after **24 April 1973** (the date of *Kesavananda Bharati*) are open to challenge on the ground of **violation of the basic structure** or fundamental rights.

- Cases: *Kameshwar Singh v State of Bihar* (1952); *State of West Bengal v Bela Banerjee* (1954) (compensation must be a just equivalent); *R.C. Cooper* (1970) (rejected the argument that the compensation was non-justiciable); *Kesavananda Bharati* (1973); *K.T. Plantation v State of Karnataka* (2011) (Article 300A requires public purpose and authority of law, though not necessarily compensation); *Jilubhai Nanbhai Khachar v State of Gujarat* (1995). The **Right to Fair Compensation and Transparency in Land Acquisition, Rehabilitation and Resettlement Act, 2013** replaced the Land Acquisition Act, 1894.

### 6.2 Ownership

**Definitions:**

- **Austin:** a right *indefinite in point of user*, *unrestricted in point of disposition* and *unlimited in point of duration* over a determinate thing.

- **Salmond:** ownership, in its most comprehensive signification, denotes the relation between a person and any right that is vested in him. A person's *owner* is the person in whom the right is vested; ownership of a material thing is **the greatest possible interest** in it, the entirety of the powers of use and enjoyment, a right in rem.

- **Holland:** the plenary control over an object.

- **Hibbert:** ownership is a *bundle of rights*; a plenary right of dominion over a thing.

- **Hohfeld:** ownership is a **bundle of Hohfeldian relations** (claims, privileges, powers, immunities).

- **Blackstone:** 'that sole and despotic dominion which one man claims and exercises over the external things of the world, in total exclusion of the right of any other individual in the universe.'

**A.M. Honore's 'Ownership' (1961)** gives the modern *standard incidents of ownership*: (1) **the right to possess**, (2) **the right to use**, (3) **the right to manage**, (4) **the right to the income**, (5) **the right to the capital** (the power to alienate, consume or destroy), (6) **the right to security** (immunity from expropriation), (7) **transmissibility**, (8) **absence of term** (indefinite duration), (9) **the prohibition of harmful use**, (10) **liability to execution** (for debt), and (11) **residuary character** (the right to what remains when lesser interests lapse).

**Essential characteristics of ownership (Salmond/Austin):** it is **indeterminate in extent** (the owner has all the rights over the thing not given to others), it is **residuary** (rights left after lesser interests are carved out), it is **permanent** (it outlasts the owner by inheritance), and it is a **right in rem** (good against everybody).

**Kinds of ownership (Salmond):**

- **Corporeal and incorporeal ownership:** over a material thing and over an intangible right (a patent, a copyright, a debt).

- **Sole ownership and co-ownership:** two or more persons may own a thing together (**joint tenancy** with the right of survivorship; **tenancy in common** with undivided shares; in India, co-ownership in joint Hindu family property, and the Transfer of Property Act).

- **Trust and beneficial ownership:** the **trustee** holds legal title while the **beneficiary** has the equitable (beneficial) interest (**Section 3 of the Indian Trusts Act, 1882**).

- **Legal and equitable ownership.**

- **Vested and contingent ownership:** vested where the title is complete; contingent where it depends on a future event.

- **Absolute and limited ownership;** **ownership limited by encumbrances** (leases, easements, mortgages).

**Modes of acquisition of ownership (Salmond):**

- **Original acquisition:** by *absorption* (taking things not owned: occupation, finding, accession), by *prescription* (the long enjoyment of a right).

- **Derivative acquisition:** by *transfer* (alienation by agreement, voluntary), by *inheritance* (succession on death, involuntary), by *execution or bankruptcy* (**forced transfer**).

- **Prescription (positive and negative):** under Indian law, **Section 27 of the Limitation Act, 1963**, extinguishes the *right to property* when the limitation period for suing for possession has expired. **Adverse possession:** **Article 65** of the Schedule to the Limitation Act, 1963 (12 years for private property and **30 years** for property of the Government, Article 112). Landmark cases: *Karnataka Board of Wakf v Government of India* (2004); *Hemaji Waghaji Jat v Bhikhabhai Khengarbhai Harijan* (2009) (the Court expressed unease with adverse possession); *Ravinder Kaur Grewal v Manjit Kaur* (2019) (a person claiming adverse possession may use it as a **sword** as well as a shield, with a plaintiff able to seek a declaration of title).

**Modern limitations on ownership:** planning and zoning laws, environmental regulations, land ceiling laws, rent control, **eminent domain** (compulsory acquisition), taxation, and the **public trust doctrine**.

### 6.3 Possession

**Meaning:** possession is the *de facto* relationship between a person and a thing, the **effective control over a thing** with the intention to hold it to the exclusion of others. It is a notoriously elusive concept in law.

**Definitions and theories:**

- **Salmond:** possession is the *continuing exercise of a claim to the exclusive use of a thing*. He distinguishes **possession in fact** and **possession in law**.

- **Savigny (subjective theory):** possession has two elements: **corpus possessionis** (the physical control, or the power to deal with the thing) and **animus domini (animus possidendi)** (the intention to hold the thing as an owner, to the exclusion of all others). If there is *corpus* without *animus*, the person only has **detention** (*detentio*), as in a tenant, servant or bailee who recognises another's ownership.

- **Ihering (objective theory):** *animus* is not an independent element; it is merely the **intention to deal with the thing as an ordinary man would deal with his own** (corpus alone is enough, subject to the law's exceptions). Possession is protected by law as the **'outpost' (outer defence) of ownership**, and its protection serves *ownership*.

- **Pollock and Holmes:** possession is **power to exclude others** with the intention to do so. **Holmes** (*The Common Law*, 1881): the protection of possession follows from the idea of preventing breaches of the peace and of the person's will; **Pollock** emphasises *effective control*. Pollock and Wright's *Possession in the Common Law* (1888) is the standard work.

- **Kant and Hegel:** the 'intelligible possession' arises from will and is connected to right and not just to physical control.

**Elements in Salmond's view:** an **intention** (animus) and a **corpus** (a degree of physical power or control).

**Kinds of possession:**

- **Corporeal (possession of things) and incorporeal (possession of rights**, such as an easement or a way, sometimes called *quasi-possession*).

- **Mediate and immediate possession:** an owner who lets another hold the thing for him (a bailee, a tenant) retains **mediate** possession, while the bailee has **immediate** possession.

- **Possession in fact and in law (constructive possession).**

- **Concurrent possession:** different persons have possession of the same thing in different ways (a pledgor and pledgee).

- **Adverse possession:** possession that is hostile to the true owner's title, with the intention to claim ownership.

- **Joint and sole possession.**

**Acquisition of possession:** by **taking** (with or without the consent of the previous possessor), by **delivery** (actual or constructive), and by **operation of law**.

**Leading cases on possession and finders (frequent MCQs):**

- *Armory v Delamirie* (1722): a chimney-sweep's boy who found a jewel acquired a **title good against everybody except the true owner**; the jeweller was liable for refusing to return it.

- *Bridges v Hawkesworth* (1851): a customer who found banknotes on the floor of a shop was entitled to them against the shopkeeper, since the notes were never in the shopkeeper's possession.

- *South Staffordshire Water Co v Sharman* (1896): the owner of land has a better claim than the finder to **things found embedded in the land** (the rings found in a pool); the occupier, having a greater degree of control, has possession.

- *Hannah v Peel* (1945): the finder of a brooch in a requisitioned house was entitled to it against the occupier who had never lived there.

- *Parker v British Airways Board* (1982): the occupier's superior claim exists only if he manifested an intention to control the premises and things on them; the finder's rights were set out.

- *Merry v Green* (1841): the buyer of a bureau with a secret drawer who found money in it was guilty of larceny, as the property had not passed to him.

- **Indian statutes:** **Sections 71 and 168 of the Indian Contract Act, 1872** (the finder of goods is a bailee and has a right to retain and to sue for reward only in some conditions); **Section 6 of the Specific Relief Act, 1963** (a person dispossessed of immovable property otherwise than in due course of law can recover possession by a summary suit within **six months**, regardless of title, and no appeal lies from the order); Section 110 of the Indian Evidence Act, 1872 (possession as *prima facie* proof of ownership), now carried into the **Bharatiya Sakshya Adhiniyam, 2023**.

**Importance of possession:** possession is *prima facie* evidence of ownership (the maxim *possession is nine points of law*), possession is protected even against the true owner who takes the law into his own hands, a person in possession can sue trespassers, and possession is a root of title for **prescription** and **adverse possession**.

### 6.4 Ownership versus Possession

| Basis            | Ownership                                                                                                      | Possession                                                    |
|------------------|----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------|---------------------------------------------------------------|
| Nature           | A right *de jure* (legal title)                                                                                | A fact *de facto* (effective control)                         |
| Duration         | Permanent, indefinite                                                                                          | May be temporary                                              |
| Extent           | The totality of rights over the thing                                                                          | A limited relationship with the thing (control and enjoyment) |
| Protection       | Remedies for recovery and declaration of title                                                                 | Protected by summary remedies, and against wrongdoers         |
| Transmissibility | Inheritable and alienable                                                                                      | Not always transferable as a right                            |
| Relation         | A thief has possession without ownership; an owner who loses a thing retains ownership but has lost possession |                                                               |

**Ihering's remark:** possession is the **outer fortress of ownership**. **Savigny:** possession is a mere fact, but with legal consequences. The two are **separate** concepts that usually coincide, hence the usual case of an owner in possession; Salmond treats ownership as an *ideal* and possession as *the visible* relationship.

- **Locke:** labour theory; **Bentham:** property and law born together; **Hegel:** personality; **Proudhon:** property is theft; **Duguit:** social function.

- **Possession:** *corpus* and *animus*; **Savigny** (subjective, animus domini) versus **Ihering** (objective, protection of ownership); **Salmond:** continuing exercise of a claim to exclusive use.

- **Cases:** *Armory v Delamirie* (1722), *Bridges v Hawkesworth* (1851), *South Staffordshire v Sharman* (1896), *Hannah v Peel* (1945), *Parker v British Airways* (1982).

## Chapter 7: Concept of Liability

### 7.1 Meaning and Nature of Legal Liability

**Liability** is the condition of being legally answerable for an act or omission. If a right is violated, the person who violated it incurs liability, and the law supplies a remedy (compensation, punishment or restoration).

- **Salmond:** liability or responsibility is *the bond of necessity that exists between the wrong-doer and the remedy of the wrong*. It is the vinculum juris (bond of law) that links the wrongdoer to the remedy.

- **Austin:** liability is the condition of being exposed to a sanction as a consequence of a wrongful act or omission.

- **Kelsen:** liability is the sanction attaching to the delict; a person is liable when a sanction can be directed against him (a person is *responsible* if he is the one against whom the sanction is directed, whether or not he is the wrongdoer).

- **Hart (*Punishment and Responsibility*, 1968):** distinguishes four senses of responsibility: *role-responsibility*, *causal-responsibility*, *liability-responsibility* and *capacity-responsibility*.

- **Hohfeld's 'liability'** is a different, technical idea: the correlative of a *power* (a person who may have his legal position changed by another's act). Do not confuse it with Salmond's liability.

**The foundation of liability: the maxim *actus non facit reum nisi mens sit rea*** (an act does not make one guilty unless the mind be guilty). Under the traditional approach, liability requires (a) a **wrongful act** (or omission), and (b) a **blameworthy state of mind**, except where the law imposes strict liability.

### 7.2 The Wrongful Act and Its Elements (Salmond's Analysis)

- **A wrong** is an act contrary to the rule of right and justice. Wrongs are **civil** (redressed by compensation or restitution, as in tort or breach of contract) or **criminal** (redressed by punishment).

- **Elements of a wrongful act:** (1) an **act or omission** (the *actus reus*), (2) a **mental element** (*mens rea*: intention, recklessness, negligence), (3) **damage or injury** in the legal sense (not always required), and (4) a **causal link** between act and harm.

- **Maxims:** *damnum sine injuria* (damage without violation of a right is not actionable) and *injuria sine damno* (violation of a legal right is actionable even without loss) (see Topic 4).

- **Mental states:**

  - **Intention:** the foresight of an act or consequence together with the desire for it (Salmond: intention is *the purpose or design with which an act is done*). **Oblique intention** (virtual certainty of consequence) is relevant in murder: *R v Woollin* (1998).

  - **Motive:** the ultimate end or reason behind the intention. **Motive is generally irrelevant to liability** (*Bradford Corporation v Pickles*, 1895; *Allen v Flood*, 1898), though it can be relevant to sentencing and in some torts (malicious prosecution, nuisance, conspiracy).

  - **Malice:** *malice in law* (wrongful act done intentionally without just cause or excuse) versus *malice in fact* (actual ill will).

  - **Negligence (Salmond's view):** a state of mind, being inadvertence (**subjective** theory). **Winfield** and modern law treat it as a **breach of duty** (an **objective** standard of the reasonable man). **Recklessness:** conscious taking of an unjustified risk.

### 7.3 Kinds of Liability

**(a) Civil and criminal liability:**

| Basis             | Civil liability                                            | Criminal liability                                 |
|-------------------|------------------------------------------------------------|----------------------------------------------------|
| Purpose           | Redress for the wronged person (compensation, restitution) | Punishment of the wrongdoer in the public interest |
| Who initiates     | The injured party                                          | The State                                          |
| Standard of proof | Balance of probabilities                                   | Beyond reasonable doubt                            |
| Parties           | Plaintiff and defendant                                    | Prosecutor and accused                             |
| Outcome           | Damages, injunction, specific performance                  | Fine, imprisonment, death                          |
| Compromise        | Generally possible                                         | Only for compoundable offences                     |

**(b) Remedial and penal liability (Salmond):**

- **Penal liability:** the wrongdoer is punished (imprisonment, fine); the law aims at *deterrence or retribution*.

- **Remedial liability:** the law merely enforces the right which has been violated, as when a debtor is compelled to pay, specific performance is ordered, or a trespasser is required to restore possession. The purpose is **restitution**.

- Salmond notes that criminal liability is penal, and civil liability may be penal (exemplary damages) or remedial.

**(c) Vicarious liability:** liability of one person for the wrong of another, arising from a relationship such as master and servant, principal and agent, partners, or employer and employee. The maxims are **respondeat superior** and **qui facit per alium facit per se** (he who does through another does through himself).

- **Rationale:** the employer controls the enterprise and benefits from it; the employer is in a better position to compensate and insure.

- **Requirements:** relationship, and a wrong committed **in the course of employment** (the *Salmond test*: an act authorised by the master, or an unauthorised mode of doing an authorised act). **Lloyd v Grace, Smith & Co** (1912): the employer liable for the solicitor's clerk's fraud; **Limpus v London General Omnibus Co** (1862) (the driver's unauthorised racing was an unauthorised mode of an authorised act).

- **Employer and independent contractor:** generally not liable for an independent contractor, with exceptions (non-delegable duties, hazardous activities).

- **Vicarious liability of the State:** under **Article 300** of the Constitution, the Union and the State may sue and be sued in relation to their affairs 'in the like cases as the Dominion of India and the Provinces'. The old distinction between **sovereign and non-sovereign functions** (*P. & O. Steam Navigation Co v Secretary of State for India*, 1861) was applied in *Kasturi Lal Ralia Ram Jain v State of UP* (1965) (the State was not liable for the loss of gold seized by police, as the police acted in sovereign functions). *State of Rajasthan v Vidyawati* (1962) held the State liable for the negligence of a government driver, treating the State as an employer in the same way as a private person. **The modern trend** has moved to compensation for violation of fundamental rights as a **public law remedy**, independent of sovereign immunity (*Rudul Sah v State of Bihar*, 1983; *Bhim Singh v State of J&K*, 1985; *Nilabati Behera v State of Orissa*, 1993; *D.K. Basu v State of West Bengal*, 1997).

- **Vicarious criminal liability:** generally not recognised in the Penal Code except where a statute provides (for example, **Section 141 of the Negotiable Instruments Act, 1881**, for companies and persons in charge; *Sunil Bharti Mittal v CBI*, 2015).

**(d) Strict and absolute liability:** liability without proof of *mens rea* or negligence.

- **Strict liability in tort:** *Rylands v Fletcher* (1868): a person who for his own purposes brings on his land and collects and keeps there anything likely to do mischief if it escapes must keep it at his peril, and is *prima facie* answerable for all damage which is the natural consequence of its escape. **Exceptions:** act of God, consent of plaintiff, plaintiff's default, act of a stranger, statutory authority, and common benefit.

- **Absolute liability (India):** *M.C. Mehta v Union of India* (1987) (the **Oleum gas leak** case, Delhi): the Supreme Court said the *Rylands v Fletcher* rule is too narrow for modern hazardous industries and laid down the rule of **absolute liability**: an enterprise engaged in hazardous or inherently dangerous activity is **strictly and absolutely liable** to compensate those harmed, and **none of the exceptions applies**. The enterprise's capacity to pay determines the **quantum of damages** (the 'deep pocket' principle: the larger and more prosperous the enterprise, the greater the amount of compensation).

- *Union Carbide Corporation v Union of India* (1989): the **Bhopal Gas Tragedy** settlement. The **Bhopal Gas Leak Disaster (Processing of Claims) Act, 1985** made the Union the sole representative of the victims. Related legislation: **Public Liability Insurance Act, 1991**, and **National Green Tribunal Act, 2010** (Section 17: **no-fault liability**).

- *Indian Council for Enviro-Legal Action v Union of India* (1996) (Bichhri, Rajasthan): the **polluter pays principle**; *Vellore Citizens' Welfare Forum v Union of India* (1996): adopted the **precautionary principle** and **polluter pays principle** as part of Indian law and as part of sustainable development.

- **Statutory strict liability:** Workmen's Compensation (now Employees' Compensation Act, 1923), the Motor Vehicles Act (no-fault liability, Section 140, and Section 164), and the Consumer Protection Act (product liability under the 2019 Act).

- **Strict liability in criminal law:** *Sherras v De Rutzen* (1895) (presumption of *mens rea* may be displaced); *R v Prince* (1875); *State of Maharashtra v Mayer Hans George* (1965) (offences under FERA/customs: *mens rea* not required where the statute so requires); *Nathulal v State of MP* (1966) (the Court reaffirms the presumption of *mens rea*); *Sweet v Parsley* (1970) (presumption of mens rea is strong in 'truly criminal' offences).

**(e) Joint and several liability, contributory liability, and liability of the corporation (see Topic 5).**

### 7.4 Negligence and Causation

- *Donoghue v Stevenson* (1932): the **neighbour principle**; a manufacturer owes a duty of care to the ultimate consumer.

- **Elements of the tort of negligence:** duty of care, breach, and damage caused by the breach, which is not too remote.

- *Bolton v Stone* (1951): a risk which is very small does not require precautions; *Blyth v Birmingham Waterworks Co* (1856): negligence is the omission to do something which a reasonable man would do.

- **Remoteness:** *Re Polemis* (1921) (directness test) replaced by *The Wagon Mound (No. 1)* (1961) (reasonable foreseeability).

- **Res ipsa loquitur:** the thing speaks for itself (*Byrne v Boulton*, 1863; *Scott v London & St Katherine Docks*, 1865).

- **Criminal negligence in India (medical negligence):** *Jacob Mathew v State of Punjab* (2005): for a criminal prosecution of a doctor, **gross negligence or recklessness** must be shown, not mere lack of care; civil negligence is the standard of the *Bolam test* (*Bolam v Friern Hospital*, 1957), accepted in India.

- **Indian Consumer Protection Act and medical negligence:** *Indian Medical Association v V.P. Shantha* (1995): medical services come within the Consumer Protection Act, 1986.

### 7.5 Defences, Exceptions and Capacity

- **Insanity:** *M'Naghten's case* (1843): the accused must show that at the time of the act he was labouring under such a defect of reason from disease of the mind as not to know the nature and quality of the act or that it was wrong. India: **Section 84 IPC** (now Section 22 BNS).

- **Infancy:** a child under seven is *doli incapax* (Section 82 IPC; Section 20 BNS); between seven and twelve it depends on maturity (Section 83 IPC; Section 21 BNS). Juvenile Justice (Care and Protection of Children) Act, 2015.

- **Necessity:** *R v Dudley and Stephens* (1884): necessity is no defence to murder.

- **Self-defence, consent, accident, mistake of fact** (and not of law), duress, and **act of God** (in tort).

- **Statutory exceptions in the Penal Code (General Exceptions, Chapter IV IPC; Chapter III BNS).**

- **Note:** the **Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita, Bharatiya Nagarik Suraksha Sanhita and Bharatiya Sakshya Adhiniyam (2023)** came into force on **1 July 2024**, replacing the IPC, CrPC and Evidence Act; the exam may use either set of section numbers during the transition.

### 7.6 Theories of Punishment (Closely Linked to Penal Liability)

| Theory                       | Thinker                            | Core idea                                                                                                             | Criticism                                                                  |
|------------------------------|------------------------------------|-----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------|----------------------------------------------------------------------------|
| Retributive                  | Kant, Hegel, Bradley               | Punishment is deserved; the offender must suffer in proportion to the wrong ('an eye for an eye'); looking *backward* | Does not reduce crime; can be vengeful                                     |
| Deterrent                    | Bentham, Beccaria, Paley, Salmond  | Punishment prevents others (general deterrence) and the offender (specific deterrence) by fear; looking *forward*     | Ineffective if offenders do not calculate; can justify excessive penalties |
| Preventive (incapacitation)  | Plato, modern penologists          | Remove the offender from society (imprisonment, death)                                                                | Does not reform; costly                                                    |
| Reformative (rehabilitative) | Modern penology; Indian reformists | Punishment aims to reform the offender; prison as a correctional institution                                          | May not deter; difficult to assess reform                                  |
| Expiatory                    | Religious thought                  | Punishment atones for guilt                                                                                           | Hard to defend in a secular state                                          |
| Utilitarian                  | Bentham, Mill                      | Punishment is justified only if it produces more good (happiness) than evil                                           | Might punish the innocent for deterrence                                   |
| Mixed (Hart)                 | H.L.A. Hart                        | General justifying aim is deterrence, subject to distribution principles based on responsibility and proportionality  | --                                                                         |
| Restorative                  | Victimology, Howard Zehr           | Repair the harm; reconcile offender, victim and community                                                             | Limited for serious crimes                                                 |

**Salmond's view:** *punishment is an evil inflicted on the wrongdoer for his wrong*; deterrence is the **primary** purpose, with retribution, prevention and reform as secondary.

**Indian cases on punishment:**

- **Capital punishment:** *Jagmohan Singh v State of UP* (1973) (the death penalty is constitutional); *Rajendra Prasad v State of UP* (1979); **Bachan Singh v State of Punjab (1980)** (the Constitution Bench upheld constitutionality of the death penalty and laid down the **'rarest of rare'** doctrine, requiring a balance of aggravating and mitigating circumstances); *Machhi Singh v State of Punjab* (1983) (guidelines); *Mithu v State of Punjab* (1983) (the **mandatory death penalty** under Section 303 IPC was struck down); *Shatrughan Chauhan v Union of India* (2014) (undue delay in disposal of mercy petitions is a ground for commutation). The **262nd Law Commission Report (2015)** recommended abolition of the death penalty for all crimes other than terrorism-related offences.

- **Reformative approach:** *Mohd. Giasuddin v State of Andhra Pradesh* (1977) (Justice Krishna Iyer, the need to humanise punishment and use reform); the **Probation of Offenders Act, 1958**; *Santosh Kumar Satishbhushan Bariyar v State of Maharashtra* (2009) (sentencing in death cases must consider the possibility of reform).

- **Beccaria's *On Crimes and Punishments* (1764)** opposed torture and the death penalty and favoured certain, swift punishment.

- **Strict liability:** *Rylands v Fletcher* (1868); **absolute liability:** *M.C. Mehta* (1987) (Oleum gas); polluter pays: *Indian Council for Enviro-Legal Action* (1996); precautionary principle: *Vellore Citizens* (1996).

## Chapter 8: Law, Poverty and Development

### 8.1 Why This Topic Matters for the Exam

This is one of the newer additions to the Jurisprudence syllabus, so questions are drawn from a **small and predictable pool**: **Amartya Sen** (entitlements, capabilities, freedoms), **Nussbaum's central capabilities**, **the Right to Development (1986)**, the **MDGs and SDGs**, the **law and development movement** (and its critics), **Hernando de Soto's legal empowerment** idea, and **Indian Supreme Court decisions on the rights of the poor**. Memorise names, books, dates and numbers.

### 8.2 Concepts of Poverty

- **Absolute poverty:** inability to afford the minimum basket of goods and services needed for survival (the poverty line). **Relative poverty:** deprivation measured against the average standard of living in a society.

- **Multidimensional poverty:** poverty measured by deprivations in health, education and living standards, not only by income. The **Global Multidimensional Poverty Index (MPI)** was introduced in 2010 by the **UNDP and the Oxford Poverty and Human Development Initiative (OPHI)** (Sabina Alkire and James Foster developed the method). It has three dimensions (health, education, living standards) and ten indicators.

- **India's poverty measurement:** the **Lakdawala** method; the **Tendulkar Committee** (2009); the **Rangarajan Committee** (2014). **NITI Aayog's National MPI** (first report 2021; the 2023 report) showed a sharp fall in multidimensional poverty (roughly from about 25 per cent in 2015-16 to about 15 per cent in 2019-21). Check the latest figures, as they are updated.

- **World Bank's international poverty line:** updated in 2022 to US\$ 2.15 a day (2017 prices) and, in 2025, to US\$ 3.00 a day (2021 prices), so verify the current figure if it appears in your exam.

- **Human Development Index (HDI):** introduced in 1990 in the first *Human Development Report* by **Mahbub ul Haq** with Amartya Sen's contribution; it measures health (life expectancy), education (years of schooling) and standard of living (GNI per capita).

### 8.3 Amartya Sen and Martha Nussbaum

**Amartya Sen** (Nobel Prize in Economics, 1998; Bharat Ratna, 1999):

- ***Poverty and Famines: An Essay on Entitlement and Deprivation* (1981):** famines are caused not only by a shortage of food but by a failure of **entitlements** (the means by which people command food: production, trade, labour, inheritance). The **Bengal famine of 1943** is his example, where food supply was not much lower than in previous years.

- ***Commodities and Capabilities* (1985)**, ***Development as Freedom* (1999)**: development is the **expansion of the real freedoms that people enjoy**; poverty is **capability deprivation** (not just low income). He identified **five instrumental freedoms**: **political freedoms, economic facilities, social opportunities, transparency guarantees, and protective security**.

- **The capability approach:** the focus is on what people are actually *able to do and to be* (their **functionings** and **capabilities**), instead of resources or utility. He criticised both utilitarianism and Rawls's focus on primary goods.

- ***The Idea of Justice* (2009):** a critique of **'transcendental institutionalism'** (the Rawlsian search for perfectly just institutions) in favour of a **realisation-focused comparative approach** to reduce manifest injustice. He draws a distinction in Sanskrit between **niti** (rules, institutions, organisation) and **nyaya** (realised justice in the lives of people), and the images of **the three children and the flute** (Anne, Bob, Carla). He values **public reasoning** and the **impartial spectator** (from Adam Smith).

- Also: *Collective Choice and Social Welfare* (1970), *Inequality Reexamined* (1992), *The Argumentative Indian* (2005), *Development and Participation*.

**Martha Nussbaum:** a philosopher who developed the capabilities approach, with a more specific, universal list.

- **Works:** *Women and Human Development* (2000), *Frontiers of Justice* (2006), *Creating Capabilities* (2011).

- **Ten central human capabilities:** (1) life; (2) bodily health; (3) bodily integrity; (4) senses, imagination and thought; (5) emotions; (6) practical reason; (7) affiliation; (8) other species; (9) play; (10) control over one's environment (political and material).

- Nussbaum says each citizen must have these to a minimum threshold; she ties them to constitutional guarantees.

- **Difference from Sen:** Sen does not give a fixed list (he leaves it to public reasoning); Nussbaum proposes a definite list.

**Mahbub ul Haq** (the HDI) and **Muhammad Yunus** (Grameen Bank, microfinance; Nobel Peace Prize 2006) are associated with the practical side.

### 8.4 The Law and Development Movement

- **Meaning:** the study and practice of using law as a tool for economic and social development, especially in developing countries.

- **First movement (1960s to 1970s):** led mainly by American foundations (Ford Foundation) and law schools; it assumed that exporting Western legal institutions, legal education and 'modern' law to the Third World would speed up modernisation. Its failure was explained in **David Trubek and Marc Galanter, 'Scholars in Self-Estrangement: Some Reflections on the Crisis in Law and Development Studies in the United States' (1974)**.

- **Second movement (1980s to 2000s):** concentrated on the **rule of law, property rights, judicial reform** and **market-friendly legal frameworks** in support of the **Washington Consensus** (a term coined by **John Williamson**, 1989) and **structural adjustment** policies of the **IMF and World Bank**; the World Bank's *World Development Report 2002* and the *Doing Business* report (discontinued in 2021 after data irregularities) belong to this phase.

- **Contemporary phase:** the stress on **institutions and governance**, human rights-based approaches, and **sustainable development**. In 2024, the **Nobel Prize in Economic Sciences** was awarded to **Daron Acemoglu, Simon Johnson and James Robinson** for studies of how institutions are formed and affect prosperity (see Acemoglu and Robinson, *Why Nations Fail*, 2012: *inclusive* versus *extractive* institutions). The 2019 Nobel went to **Abhijit Banerjee, Esther Duflo and Michael Kremer** for an **experimental approach to alleviating global poverty**.

- **Douglass North** (Nobel 1993): institutions, including law, are the 'rules of the game' that shape economic performance.

- **Hernando de Soto** (*The Mystery of Capital*, 2000): the poor hold vast assets as **'dead capital'** because they lack formal, enforceable property titles; formalising property rights unlocks credit and capital. The **Commission on Legal Empowerment of the Poor** (co-chaired by **Madeleine Albright and Hernando de Soto**, report *Making the Law Work for Everyone*, 2008) identified four pillars: **access to justice and the rule of law, property rights, labour rights, and business rights**.

- **Critics:** **Third World Approaches to International Law (TWAIL)** scholars (**B.S. Chimni, Upendra Baxi, Antony Anghie**) see development law as a continuation of colonial hierarchies. **Dependency theorists** (Prebisch, Frank) see underdevelopment as a product of the global capitalist system. Others stress that legal transplants fail without local fit (the *transplant effect*).

- **New International Economic Order (NIEO, 1974)** (UN General Assembly Resolution 3201 (S-VI)), the **Charter of Economic Rights and Duties of States (1974)**, and **Permanent Sovereignty over Natural Resources** (General Assembly Resolution 1803 (XVII), 1962) reflect the developing countries' demands.

### 8.5 The Right to Development and Sustainable Development

- **Declaration on the Right to Development (1986)** (UNGA Resolution 41/128, 4 December 1986): Article 1 describes development as an **inalienable human right** by virtue of which every human person and all peoples are entitled to participate in, contribute to and enjoy economic, social, cultural and political development. The **human person is the central subject** of development. It was reaffirmed in the **Vienna Declaration (1993)** as a universal and inalienable right and an integral part of fundamental human rights. **Arjun Sengupta** (an Indian economist) was Independent Expert on the right to development.

- **Stockholm Conference (1972)**, **Brundtland Commission's report *Our Common Future* (1987)**: **sustainable development** is development that meets the needs of the present without compromising the ability of future generations to meet their own needs; **Rio Declaration (1992)** (Principle 3: right to development to be fulfilled so as to equitably meet developmental and environmental needs of present and future generations); **Johannesburg (2002)**; **Rio+20 (2012)**.

- **Millennium Development Goals (MDGs)** (2000 to 2015): eight goals (including halving extreme poverty and hunger by 2015).

- **Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs)** (Agenda 2030, adopted in 2015): **17 goals and 169 targets**. **SDG 1: No Poverty**; SDG 2: Zero Hunger; SDG 3: Good Health; SDG 4: Quality Education; SDG 5: Gender Equality; SDG 10: Reduced Inequalities; SDG 16: Peace, Justice and Strong Institutions; SDG 17: Partnerships.

- **Indian recognition:** *Vellore Citizens' Welfare Forum* (1996) treated sustainable development as part of Indian law; *Narmada Bachao Andolan v Union of India* (2000) (the Sardar Sarovar Dam case; the Court balanced displacement and development and upheld the project with conditions for rehabilitation); *Samatha v State of Andhra Pradesh* (1997) (tribal land cannot be transferred to non-tribals; mining leases on scheduled-area land).

### 8.6 Law as an Instrument of Social Change in India: Poverty and the Constitution

- **Preamble:** justice, social, economic and political; equality of status and opportunity.

- **Directive Principles:** **Article 38** (social order for the promotion of welfare, minimising inequalities), **Article 39(a) to (f)** (adequate means of livelihood, distribution of resources, equal pay), **Article 39A** (equal justice and free legal aid, inserted by the 42nd Amendment, 1976), **Article 41** (right to work, education and public assistance), **Article 43** (living wage), **Article 46** (promotion of the educational and economic interests of Scheduled Castes, Scheduled Tribes and weaker sections), **Article 47** (raising the level of nutrition and public health).

- **Granville Austin** (*The Indian Constitution: Cornerstone of a Nation*, 1966) described the Constitution as a **social document** and Fundamental Rights and Directive Principles as a **'seamless web'** and the conscience of the Constitution.

- **Fundamental rights and the poor:**

  - *Hussainara Khatoon v State of Bihar* (1979): **speedy trial** and **free legal aid** are part of Article 21 (undertrial prisoners' case).

  - *M.H. Hoskot v State of Maharashtra* (1978); *Khatri v State of Bihar* (1981).

  - *People's Union for Democratic Rights v Union of India* (**Asiad Workers' case**, 1982): the **denial of minimum wages** is forced labour under **Article 23**; payment below the minimum wage is *begar*.

  - *Bandhua Mukti Morcha v Union of India* (1984): **bonded labour** is violative of Articles 21 and 23.

  - *Olga Tellis v Bombay Municipal Corporation* (1985): the **right to livelihood** is part of the right to life; pavement dwellers cannot be evicted without a fair procedure.

  - *Francis Coralie Mullin* (1981): the right to live with dignity includes the minimum necessities of life.

  - *Shantistar Builders v Narayan Khimalal Totame* (1990) and *Chameli Singh v State of UP* (1996): the **right to shelter** ('roti, kapda aur makaan') is part of Article 21.

  - *Paschim Banga Khet Mazdoor Samity v State of West Bengal* (1996): **right to emergency medical aid**; *Consumer Education and Research Centre v Union of India* (1995): the **right to health**.

  - *People's Union for Civil Liberties v Union of India* (2001, the **right to food** case): the Court monitored the mid-day meal scheme and public distribution to prevent starvation; later legislated through the **National Food Security Act, 2013**.

  - *Unni Krishnan* (1993) and **Article 21A**; **Right to Education Act, 2009**.

- **Public Interest Litigation (PIL):** *S.P. Gupta v Union of India* (1981) (the **Judges' Transfer case**) broadened *locus standi*, allowing any public-spirited person to move court on behalf of the poor; the 'epistolary jurisdiction' (letters treated as writ petitions) and **social action litigation** were developed by **Justices P.N. Bhagwati and V.R. Krishna Iyer**. **Upendra Baxi**'s *Taking Suffering Seriously: Social Action Litigation in the Supreme Court of India* (1985) is the classic scholarly treatment. **Criticism:** judicial overreach, lack of expertise in policy, and the 'PIL that serves the middle class'.

- **Legislation for the poor:** **Legal Services Authorities Act, 1987** (free legal aid; Lok Adalats); **Mahatma Gandhi National Rural Employment Guarantee Act (MGNREGA), 2005** (a legal guarantee of 100 days of wage employment per rural household); **Right to Information Act, 2005**; **Forest Rights Act, 2006**; **National Food Security Act, 2013**; **Right to Fair Compensation... Act, 2013**; Bonded Labour System (Abolition) Act, 1976; Minimum Wages Act (now the Code on Wages, 2019).

- **Economic reservations:** *Indra Sawhney v Union of India* (1992) held that reservation cannot be based on economic criteria alone, while the **103rd Constitutional Amendment (2019)** inserted Articles 15(6) and 16(6) giving up to **10 per cent reservation for economically weaker sections (EWS)**, upheld by a 3:2 majority in *Janhit Abhiyan v Union of India* (2022).

### 8.7 Law, Intellectual Property and Access to Essential Goods

- **TRIPS Agreement (1994)** and the **Doha Declaration on TRIPS and Public Health (2001)** affirmed the right of countries to protect public health and promote access to medicines for all (flexibilities such as compulsory licensing).

- *Novartis AG v Union of India* (2013): the Supreme Court refused a patent for the beta crystalline form of imatinib mesylate (Glivec) under **Section 3(d)** of the Patents Act, 1970, as the new form did not show increased therapeutic efficacy; seen as pro-access.

## Chapter 9: Global Justice

### 9.1 The Central Question

**Global justice** asks whether principles of justice (fairness, equality, rights and distribution of burdens and benefits) apply only *within* states or also *across* borders to all persons. It deals with global poverty and inequality, human rights, war and humanitarian intervention, climate change, trade, migration, and international institutions. The debate is between **cosmopolitans**, who hold that the same moral principles apply to all human beings wherever they live, and **statists, nationalists or communitarians**, who hold that principles of distributive justice arise only among members of a political community.

| Position                             | Core view                                                                                                            | Key thinkers                                                                    |
|--------------------------------------|----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------|---------------------------------------------------------------------------------|
| Cosmopolitanism                      | Persons are the ultimate units of moral concern; borders are morally arbitrary; duties of justice are global         | Kant (cosmopolitan right), Beitz, Pogge, Singer, Barry, Nussbaum, Sen (in part) |
| Statism or nationalism               | Distributive justice depends on shared institutions or co-nationality; only humanitarian duties exist across borders | Nagel, Miller, Blake, Rawls (in *Law of Peoples*, in a qualified way)           |
| Communitarianism                     | Justice is relative to the shared understandings of particular communities                                           | Walzer, MacIntyre, Sandel                                                       |
| Realism (in international relations) | States pursue interests; moral universals have little place                                                          | Morgenthau, Waltz                                                               |

### 9.2 John Rawls: The Foundation

**A Theory of Justice (1971)** is the starting point of modern theories of justice.

- **The original position and the veil of ignorance:** free and equal persons choose principles of justice behind a *veil of ignorance* (they do not know their class, race, talents, conception of the good). This device ensures **impartiality** ('justice as fairness').

- **The two principles of justice (in lexical order):**

  1.  **Equal basic liberties:** each person has an equal right to the most extensive scheme of equal basic liberties compatible with a similar scheme for others.

  2.  **Social and economic inequalities** must (a) be attached to offices and positions open to all under conditions of **fair equality of opportunity**, and (b) be to the **greatest benefit of the least advantaged** (the **difference principle**, a 'maximin' idea).

- **Primary goods:** rights, liberties, opportunities, income, wealth and the social bases of self-respect.

- **Reflective equilibrium:** a method of adjusting principles and considered judgments until they cohere.

- **Rawls versus utilitarianism:** he rejects the sacrifice of the few for the greater good of the many; 'each person possesses an inviolability founded on justice'.

- **Political Liberalism (1993):** the idea of an **overlapping consensus** among reasonable comprehensive doctrines and **public reason**.

- **The Law of Peoples (1999):** Rawls's account of international justice. He extends the social contract idea to a **'second original position'** among **peoples** (not individuals). He sets out **eight principles**: (1) peoples are free and independent and their freedom is to be respected; (2) peoples observe treaties; (3) peoples are equal and parties to the agreements that bind them; (4) peoples observe a **duty of non-intervention**; (5) peoples have the **right of self-defence** but no right to instigate war; (6) peoples are to **honour human rights**; (7) peoples are to observe certain specified **restrictions in the conduct of war**; (8) peoples have a **duty to assist other peoples living under unfavourable conditions** that prevent their having a just or decent political and social regime.

- **Five types of societies in *The Law of Peoples*:** **reasonable liberal peoples, decent (hierarchical) peoples, outlaw states, societies burdened by unfavourable conditions, and benevolent absolutisms.**

- **Duty of assistance, not global difference principle:** Rawls holds that wealthy societies owe a *duty of assistance* to burdened societies until these can sustain just or decent institutions, but he **rejects a global difference principle** because (he argues) the wealth of a nation depends mostly on its **political culture** and not on the resources alone. This is the point on which cosmopolitan critics attack him.

### 9.3 Cosmopolitan Theories

**Immanuel Kant:** *Perpetual Peace* (1795): three 'definitive articles': (1) the civil constitution of every state should be **republican**; (2) the law of nations should be founded on a **federation of free states**; (3) **cosmopolitan right** shall be limited to conditions of **universal hospitality**.

**Charles Beitz:** *Political Theory and International Relations* (1979), the first major cosmopolitan critique of Rawls. He argued that the global distribution of natural resources is arbitrary from a moral point of view, and proposed an **international difference principle** and a **resource redistribution principle**. The global economy shows enough interdependence to apply justice at the global level.

**Thomas Pogge:** *Realizing Rawls* (1989), *World Poverty and Human Rights* (2002).

- Argues that the wealthy nations and the **global institutional order** (trade rules, borrowing privileges, resource privileges) **contribute to severe poverty** and thus violate a **negative duty not to harm** (not merely a positive duty to assist).

- Proposes the **Global Resource Dividend (GRD)** (a small tax on the use of natural resources, the proceeds to help the global poor) and the **Health Impact Fund** (to reward drug companies for health impact rather than patent monopoly).

- Holds that poverty is a *human rights deficit*; the global order is **institutional** and the harm is **foreseeable and avoidable**.

**Peter Singer:** 'Famine, Affluence and Morality' (1972), *One World* (2002), *The Life You Can Save* (2009). A **utilitarian** argument that if we can prevent something bad from happening without sacrificing anything of comparable moral importance, we ought to do so; **the drowning child analogy** (the moral irrelevance of distance). He is a leading figure in the effective altruism movement.

**Brian Barry:** *Humanity and Justice in Global Perspective* (1982), *Why Social Justice Matters* (2005): global egalitarianism.

**Martha Nussbaum:** *Frontiers of Justice* (2006): the capabilities approach extended to **disability, nationality and species membership**; every person is entitled to a threshold of central capabilities, no matter where they live.

**Amartya Sen:** *The Idea of Justice* (2009): avoids a single blueprint of 'perfectly just institutions' and defends **comparative, plural and open** public reasoning, with 'open impartiality' (inviting perspectives from beyond the borders of one's community) as the way to reduce **remediable injustice** in the world.

**Onora O'Neill:** *Bounds of Justice* (2000), a Kantian cosmopolitan approach; **Henry Shue:** *Basic Rights: Subsistence, Affluence and U.S. Foreign Policy* (1980), arguing that **security and subsistence** are *basic rights* since the enjoyment of every other right depends on them, and creating *correlative duties on everyone*.

**Nancy Fraser:** *Scales of Justice* (2008): the problem of **misframing** (who counts as a subject of justice); three dimensions: **redistribution, recognition and representation**. **Iris Marion Young:** *Responsibility for Justice* (2011): the **social connection model** of responsibility, in which people who contribute through their actions to structural injustice share responsibility for remedying it.

### 9.4 Statist, Nationalist and Communitarian Responses

- **Thomas Nagel**, 'The Problem of Global Justice' (2005): demands of **distributive justice arise only among those who are jointly subject to a sovereign state**, which imposes coercive laws in their name; across states there are only duties of **humanity** and minimal **justice**. He regards the world as lacking a global sovereign, so global justice is *'not yet' applicable*.

- **David Miller**, *National Responsibility and Global Justice* (2007): defends **nationality** and special obligations to compatriots, but accepts that **basic human rights** must be respected everywhere (a *'sufficiency'* threshold).

- **Michael Walzer**, *Spheres of Justice* (1983): different **social goods** are distributed by different principles, internal to the shared understandings of each community; *Just and Unjust Wars* (1977): the modern account of just war theory and the **moral reality of war**.

- **Michael Blake**: justice is triggered by coercion and the state's claim to authority.

- **Alasdair MacIntyre, Michael Sandel**: communitarian critics of Rawls's 'unencumbered self'.

### 9.5 Key Applications of Global Justice

**(a) Global poverty and economic justice:** trade rules (WTO), foreign debt (the **HIPC initiative**), tax havens, aid and the **UN target of 0.7 per cent of GNI for official development assistance**; TRIPS and access to medicines (see Topic 8).

**(b) Human rights and international criminal justice:**

- **Universal Declaration of Human Rights (1948); ICCPR and ICESCR (1966; in force 1976).**

- **Nuremberg (1945 to 1946) and Tokyo Tribunals; ICTY (1993) and ICTR (1994); the Rome Statute (1998; in force 2002) establishing the International Criminal Court (ICC)**. India is **not** a party to the Rome Statute.

- **Universal jurisdiction:** the Eichmann case (Israel, 1961); *R v Bow Street Metropolitan Stipendiary Magistrate, ex parte Pinochet (No. 3)* (1999, House of Lords): a former head of state has no immunity for torture.

- **Transitional justice:** truth commissions (South Africa's Truth and Reconciliation Commission), reparations and amnesties.

**(c) Humanitarian intervention and the Responsibility to Protect (R2P):**

- The **ICISS Report (2001)** 'The Responsibility to Protect', commissioned by Canada, introduced the idea that sovereignty implies **responsibility**.

- The **2005 World Summit Outcome Document** (paragraphs 138 and 139) adopted R2P: each state has a responsibility to protect its population from **genocide, war crimes, ethnic cleansing and crimes against humanity**, and the international community must act through the UN Security Council if the state manifestly fails. Applied (controversially) in **Libya** (Resolution 1973, 2011).

- **Just war theory:** *jus ad bellum* (just cause, legitimate authority, right intention, last resort, proportionality, reasonable prospect of success), *jus in bello* (discrimination, proportionality) and **jus post bellum**. Sources: Augustine, Aquinas, Grotius, Walzer.

**(d) Climate justice:**

- **Stockholm (1972); Rio (1992) and UNFCCC (1992); Kyoto Protocol (1997); Paris Agreement (2015)** (temperature limit well below 2 degrees Celsius, pursuing 1.5 degrees; Nationally Determined Contributions).

- **Principle of common but differentiated responsibilities and respective capabilities (CBDR-RC)** (Rio Principle 7): developed countries, which have contributed most to historical emissions, must take the lead. India has consistently argued for this principle and for **climate finance and technology transfer**.

- **Loss and damage:** a fund agreed at **COP27 (Sharm el-Sheikh, 2022)** and operationalised at **COP28 (Dubai, 2023)**.

- **Henry Shue** drew the distinction between **subsistence emissions** and **luxury emissions**.

- **Courts:** the **ICJ delivered an advisory opinion on states' obligations in respect of climate change (July 2025)** (verify the details for the exam); the Indian Supreme Court in *M.K. Ranjitsinh v Union of India* (2024) recognised a **right to be free from the adverse effects of climate change** as part of Articles 14 and 21.

**(e) Migration, refugees and statelessness:**

- **1951 Refugee Convention and 1967 Protocol**: the principle of **non-refoulement** (a refugee must not be returned to a place of persecution). India is **not a party**, but follows the principle in parts through the Constitution and customary law.

- **Joseph Carens**, 'Aliens and Citizens: The Case for Open Borders' (1987): argued for **open borders** on liberal-egalitarian grounds. **Walzer** defended the right of communities to decide on membership.

- **Indian cases:** *National Human Rights Commission v State of Arunachal Pradesh* (1996) (**Chakma refugees**; Article 21 protects non-citizens too); *Mohammad Salimullah v Union of India* (2021) (the Rohingya deportation case).

**(f) Global health and access to medicines:** the **TRIPS flexibilities** and the **Doha Declaration (2001)**; **WHO**; the debate over vaccine equity in the Covid-19 pandemic (**COVAX**; TRIPS waiver proposals).

**(g) Indian constitutional links:** **Article 51** (promotion of international peace and security, respect for international law and treaty obligations); **Article 253** (legislation to implement international agreements); *Vishaka* (1997) and *Apparel Export Promotion Council v A.K. Chopra* (1999) (international conventions read into domestic law where there is no conflict); *Jolly George Varghese v Bank of Cochin* (1980) (the ICCPR and imprisonment for debt).

## Chapter 10: Modernism and Post-Modernism

### 10.1 Modernism in Legal Thought

**Modernism** refers to the intellectual outlook that emerged from the **Enlightenment** (seventeenth and eighteenth centuries). Its main assumptions:

- **Reason and rationality** as the route to truth and progress.

- **Objective, universal knowledge**; the existence of facts independent of observers.

- **Grand narratives (metanarratives)** of progress, emancipation and totalising explanation (liberalism, Marxism, science).

- The **autonomous, rational subject** (the Cartesian and Kantian individual).

- **Law as a rational, neutral, coherent and autonomous system** that can be systematised, codified and applied objectively (Bentham's codification, Austin's science, Kelsen's pure theory, Hart's concept of law; legal formalism and liberal legalism).

- **Max Weber** described modernity as the **rationalisation** and 'disenchantment' of the world; modern law is *formally rational*.

**Modern jurisprudential schools** (natural law, positivism, historical, sociological, realist, Marxist) share the assumption that there is a coherent theory of law capable of explaining or justifying law. Even the realists and Marxists criticised law in the *name of a better reason or science*.

### 10.2 Post-Modernism: Core Ideas

Postmodernism is not a school with a single doctrine but a **cluster of critical attitudes** towards modernity. Main features:

- **Incredulity towards metanarratives** (Lyotard): suspicion of any single theory (including of justice, progress or reason) claiming universal truth.

- **Knowledge is constructed through language, discourse and power**, not discovered neutrally. Truth is local, contingent and plural.

- **Decentring of the subject:** the 'individual' is shaped by discourse and social forces (the 'death of the author' in Barthes).

- **Rejection of foundations:** no ultimate foundation for knowledge or morality.

- **Deconstruction of binary oppositions** (reason/emotion, public/private, male/female, law/politics, rule/exception).

- **Indeterminacy of meaning:** texts, including law, have no single fixed meaning.

- **Pluralism and difference**: emphasis on marginal voices, local knowledge and small narratives (*petits recits*).

- **Law as power, discourse and text**, not a neutral instrument of reason.

### 10.3 Key Post-Modern Thinkers (Highest Exam Yield)

**Jean-Francois Lyotard (1924 to 1998):** *The Postmodern Condition: A Report on Knowledge* (1979): defines the postmodern as **'incredulity towards metanarratives'**; **language games** (borrowed from Wittgenstein); knowledge legitimated through **performativity**. *The Differend* (1983): a **differend** is a conflict between two parties that cannot be resolved because no rule of judgment is applicable to both; the wrong suffered by the one who has no language to express it (this has been applied in law to the position of victims, minorities and indigenous peoples).

**Michel Foucault (1926 to 1984):**

- **Works:** *Madness and Civilization* (1961), *The Birth of the Clinic* (1963), *The Order of Things* (1966), *The Archaeology of Knowledge* (1969), ***Discipline and Punish*** (1975), ***The History of Sexuality*, Vol. I** (1976), *Society Must Be Defended* (lectures 1975 to 1976), and lectures on **governmentality**.

- **Power/knowledge:** power and knowledge are inseparable; knowledge produces power and vice versa; power is **productive**, not merely repressive; and it is **dispersed through society** (capillary power), not located only in the state or sovereign.

- **Sovereign (juridical) power versus disciplinary power:** pre-modern power was exercised as sovereign command and spectacle (public execution, as in the opening of *Discipline and Punish*), while modern power works through **discipline**, surveillance, examination and normalisation (prisons, schools, hospitals, barracks). Law has increasingly become one of many mechanisms of **normalisation**, and the legal 'subject' is produced by these practices.

- **The Panopticon:** Bentham's design for a prison, with a central tower from which inmates may be observed without knowing whether they are being watched; Foucault uses it as the **metaphor of modern disciplinary society** (self-regulation through the possibility of being watched).

- **Biopower and biopolitics:** the management of populations (births, health, sexuality) by the state.

- **Genealogy:** the historical analysis of how present practices and 'truths' emerged from contingent power struggles.

- Foucault urged political theory to 'cut off the king's head', that is, to look at the micro-physics of power rather than only at sovereign commands.

**Jacques Derrida (1930 to 2004):**

- **Deconstruction:** the reading of texts to reveal the hidden hierarchies, contradictions and instabilities in their binary oppositions; **differance** (difference and deferral of meaning); **logocentrism** (the privilege given to speech and presence); **aporia** (an impasse); **undecidability**.

- **Works:** *Of Grammatology* (1967), *Writing and Difference* (1967), *Dissemination*, **'Force of Law: The Mystical Foundation of Authority'** (1990).

- In *Force of Law* he argues that **law is deconstructible** (because it is a historical, constructed and changeable system), but **justice is not deconstructible** (it is an infinite, unconditional demand that law can never fully satisfy). The 'mystical foundation of authority': the founding act of law is itself neither legal nor illegal, a performative act of violence without prior legal justification. Justice is always **'to come'** (*a venir*); a decision is just only when it passes through the **ordeal of the undecidable**.

**Others:**

- **Jean Baudrillard:** *Simulacra and Simulation* (1981): **hyperreality**, where signs and simulations replace reality.

- **Richard Rorty:** *Philosophy and the Mirror of Nature* (1979), *Contingency, Irony and Solidarity* (1989): **pragmatic anti-foundationalism**; **liberal ironism**.

- **Stanley Fish:** *Is There a Text in This Class?* (1980): meaning is produced by **interpretive communities**; applied to legal interpretation (*Doing What Comes Naturally*).

- **Fredric Jameson:** *Postmodernism, or the Cultural Logic of Late Capitalism* (1991); **David Harvey:** *The Condition of Postmodernity* (1989).

- **Zygmunt Bauman:** *Legislators and Interpreters* (1987) and *Liquid Modernity* (2000); **Anthony Giddens:** *The Consequences of Modernity* (1990) (late or reflexive modernity); **Ulrich Beck:** *Risk Society* (1986).

- **Jurgen Habermas:** a defender of modernity: 'Modernity: An Unfinished Project' (1980); *The Philosophical Discourse of Modernity* (1985); *Between Facts and Norms* (1992): **discourse theory of law and democracy**, in which law's legitimacy rests on communicative rationality and the idea that norms are valid only if all those affected could agree in rational discourse. He criticised Foucault and Derrida for abandoning reason.

**Postmodern legal theorists:** **Costas Douzinas and Ronnie Warrington** (*Postmodern Jurisprudence*, 1991; Douzinas, *The End of Human Rights*, 2000); **Peter Fitzpatrick** (*The Mythology of Modern Law*, 1992); **Drucilla Cornell** (*The Philosophy of the Limit*, 1992); **Jack Balkin** (deconstruction and law); **Boaventura de Sousa Santos** (*Toward a New Legal Common Sense*; the idea of **interlegality** and the **cartographic** view of law); **Dragan Milovanovic.**

### 10.4 Modernism versus Post-Modernism in Law: A Comparison

| Aspect                  | Modernist jurisprudence                              | Post-modern jurisprudence                                       |
|-------------------------|------------------------------------------------------|-----------------------------------------------------------------|
| View of knowledge       | Objective, universal, rational                       | Constructed, contextual, plural                                 |
| Law                     | Coherent, neutral system; rule-bound                 | Indeterminate, a discourse linked to power                      |
| Subject                 | Autonomous rational individual                       | Decentred, produced by discourse                                |
| Theory                  | Grand theory (natural law, positivism, Marxism)      | Suspicion of grand theory; small narratives                     |
| Justice                 | Achievable by right reasoning or institutions        | Always deferred or undecidable (Derrida); a differend (Lyotard) |
| Method                  | Analysis, system-building, science                   | Deconstruction, genealogy, narrative                            |
| Power                   | Sovereign, centred in the state                      | Dispersed, productive, disciplinary (Foucault)                  |
| Representative thinkers | Kant, Bentham, Austin, Kelsen, Hart, Rawls, Habermas | Lyotard, Foucault, Derrida, Baudrillard, Rorty                  |

### 10.5 Critical Legal Studies (CLS)

**Origins:** CLS emerged in the United States in the **late 1970s** (a conference at the University of Wisconsin in 1977 is regarded as its founding event) among left-leaning law teachers influenced by **legal realism, Marxism, the Frankfurt School, structuralism and later post-structuralism**.

**Core tenets:**

- **Law is politics:** legal doctrine is not neutral and reflects power relations, especially those of class, race and gender.

- **Indeterminacy thesis:** legal rules and precedents do not compel a single result; judges can reach opposite conclusions from the same material, and the apparent determinacy is produced by ideology.

- **Contradiction:** liberal legal thought contains deep contradictions between **individualism and altruism** (Kennedy's *'fundamental contradiction'*), rules versus standards, and so on.

- **Legitimation:** law reproduces social hierarchy by making it appear natural and necessary (**reification**); **Gramsci's hegemony** is used.

- **Critique of rights:** rights are indeterminate, reinforce individual isolation and are used by the powerful (Mark Tushnet, Peter Gabel).

- **Trashing:** the critical exposure of the contradictions of legal doctrine (Mark Kelman).

**Key figures and works:**

- **Roberto Mangabeira Unger:** *Knowledge and Politics* (1975), *The Critical Legal Studies Movement* (1983), *False Necessity* (1987); he rejects 'false necessity' and proposes **'empowered democracy'** and **'deviationist doctrine'**.

- **Duncan Kennedy:** 'Form and Substance in Private Law Adjudication' (1976), *The Rise and Fall of Classical Legal Thought*, *Legal Education and the Reproduction of Hierarchy* (1983), *A Critique of Adjudication* (1997).

- **Morton Horwitz:** *The Transformation of American Law, 1780 to 1860* (1977).

- **Mark Tushnet, Karl Klare, Peter Gabel, Mark Kelman, Alan Hunt, Joseph Singer, Gerald Frug.**

- **Criticism:** CLS offers a critique but no constructive programme; **Owen Fiss** ('The Death of Law?', 1986) and **Paul Carrington** ('Of Law and the River', 1984, who suggested that CLS scholars should resign); the *indeterminacy thesis* is exaggerated; and rights discourse has also served the oppressed (the response of critical race scholars).

### 10.6 Feminist Jurisprudence

- **Liberal feminism:** equal rights, equal opportunities, formal equality (Mary Wollstonecraft: *A Vindication of the Rights of Woman*, 1792; John Stuart Mill: *The Subjection of Women*, 1869; Betty Friedan; Ruth Bader Ginsburg's litigation).

- **Cultural (difference) feminism:** women have a distinct moral voice. **Carol Gilligan**, *In a Different Voice* (1982): the **ethic of care** versus the male-associated **ethic of justice**; **Robin West**, 'Jurisprudence and Gender' (1988): the **connection thesis** (women are materially connected to others through pregnancy and nursing) versus the 'separation thesis' that underlies liberal legalism and male jurisprudence.

- **Radical (dominance) feminism:** **Catharine MacKinnon**, *Sexual Harassment of Working Women* (1979), *Feminism Unmodified* (1987), *Toward a Feminist Theory of the State* (1989): **gender is a hierarchy of power and dominance**, not merely difference; law is *male* because it is objective in the way that male power is objective; sexual harassment as sex discrimination; the campaign (with **Andrea Dworkin**) for civil rights anti-pornography ordinances.

- **Post-modern feminism:** **Judith Butler**, *Gender Trouble* (1990): gender is **performative** (constituted by repeated acts), not a natural fact; **Carol Smart**, *Feminism and the Power of Law* (1989): law is a **gendering strategy**, and feminists should be careful about turning to law as the answer; **Drucilla Cornell**.

- **Intersectionality:** **Kimberle Crenshaw**, 'Demarginalizing the Intersection of Race and Sex' (1989): discrimination based on overlapping identities (race, sex, class) cannot be understood by looking at each category separately.

- **Simone de Beauvoir**, *The Second Sex* (1949): 'One is not born, but rather becomes, a woman.'

- **Indian feminist legal landmarks:** *Mary Roy v State of Kerala* (1986) (equal inheritance rights of Syrian Christian women under the Indian Succession Act); *Mohd. Ahmed Khan v Shah Bano Begum* (1985) (maintenance for a divorced Muslim woman under Section 125 CrPC), followed by the **Muslim Women (Protection of Rights on Divorce) Act, 1986**, and *Danial Latifi v Union of India* (2001); *Vishaka v State of Rajasthan* (1997) and the **POSH Act, 2013**; *Anuj Garg v Hotel Association of India* (2008) (protective discrimination based on stereotypes is invalid); *Shayara Bano* (2017); *Joseph Shine* (2018); *Independent Thought v Union of India* (2017) (sexual intercourse with a wife under 18 is rape); *Vineeta Sharma v Rakesh Sharma* (2020) (daughters are **coparceners by birth** under the amended Hindu Succession Act, 2005); *Sabarimala* (2018). The **Nari Shakti Vandan Adhiniyam, 2023** (the 106th Constitutional Amendment) provides one-third reservation for women in the Lok Sabha and State Legislative Assemblies, to take effect after the next census and delimitation.

### 10.7 Critical Race Theory, Queer Theory, Post-Colonial and TWAIL Approaches

- **Critical race theory (CRT):** **Derrick Bell** (*Faces at the Bottom of the Well*, 1992; the **interest-convergence thesis**: progress for black Americans occurs only when it converges with white interests, as in *Brown v Board of Education*, 1954), **Kimberle Crenshaw, Richard Delgado, Mari Matsuda, Patricia Williams** (*The Alchemy of Race and Rights*, 1991), **Cheryl Harris** ('Whiteness as Property', 1993). Methods: **storytelling and counter-storytelling**; race as a social construct; critique of colour-blindness.

- **Queer theory:** **Judith Butler**, **Michel Foucault**, **Eve Sedgwick** (*Epistemology of the Closet*, 1990): identity categories are social constructs; heteronormativity.

- **Post-colonial theory:** **Edward Said**, *Orientalism* (1978); **Gayatri Spivak**, 'Can the Subaltern Speak?' (1988); **Homi Bhabha**; the **Subaltern Studies** collective (Ranajit Guha). In law: **Upendra Baxi**, **Ratna Kapur**, and **TWAIL** scholars (B.S. Chimni, Antony Anghie) who show how international law carries colonial structures.

- **Legal pluralism:** the coexistence of more than one legal order in the same social field. **Eugen Ehrlich** (living law); **John Griffiths**, 'What is Legal Pluralism?' (1986) distinguishes **strong** (genuine, social) from **weak** (state-recognised) pluralism; **Boaventura de Sousa Santos** and **Sally Engle Merry** (*global and local law*); **Gunther Teubner** ('Global Bukowina', 1997). **Niklas Luhmann** and **Teubner** developed **autopoiesis** (law as a self-referential social system) as a late-modern account of law.

### 10.8 Criticism of Post-Modernism

- **Relativism and nihilism:** it offers no basis to say that any legal arrangement is better than another.

- **Performative contradiction (self-refutation):** the claim that there are no universal truths seems itself a universal claim (Habermas).

- **Political paralysis:** rejection of metanarratives undermines the grounds for emancipatory politics, human rights and the rule of law.

- **Obscurity of language** and lack of empirical testing.

- **Defenders reply** that postmodernism does not abandon justice (Derrida), but demands more responsibility and attention to those whom 'universal' systems exclude (Lyotard's differend).

### 11.3 Ten Exam-Day Strategies for Jurisprudence MCQs

1.  **Do the match-the-list questions by elimination:** pin down the two items you are sure of; the rest usually follow.

2.  **Read 'NOT' and 'INCORRECT' twice**; the examiner often uses a minor swap (for example Bentham for Austin, Stammler for Kohler).

3.  **For statement questions**, look for absolute words ('always', 'only', 'never'); they usually signal a false statement.

4.  **Remember who attacks whom:** Hart attacks Austin; Dworkin attacks Hart; Fuller attacks Hart; Devlin versus Hart; Rawls versus utilitarians; Pogge and Beitz versus Rawls (on the global level); Habermas versus the postmodernists; Carrington and Fiss versus CLS.

5.  **Years matter:** *Ancient Law* 1861; *Province* 1832; *Concept of Law* 1961; *Theory of Justice* 1971; *Law's Empire* 1986; *Development as Freedom* 1999; *Idea of Justice* 2009; Declaration on the Right to Development 1986; SDGs 2015.

6.  **Indian cases are favoured in applied questions:** know the one-line holding of each landmark case, including whether it was later overruled or stayed.

7.  **Distinguish similar concepts:** ownership versus possession; right versus privilege (Hohfeld); fiction versus concession versus realist theory; strict versus absolute liability; legal versus moral duty.

8.  **Use the schools' 'five questions'** (nature, source of authority, law and morality, law and society, who finds the law) to place any unfamiliar proposition.

9.  **Where news is involved** (rivers, animals, EWS, privacy, climate), recall the **court, year and outcome** and any subsequent stay or reversal.

10. **Never leave a question blank**, since there is no negative marking.
