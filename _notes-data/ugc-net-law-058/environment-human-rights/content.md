# Environment and Human Rights: Detailed Notes for UGC NET Law (058)

## Chapter 0: Exam Pattern, Trends and Priority Map

Unit VIII is one of the most predictable units of UGC NET Law (code 058): the syllabus has exactly ten topics, the questions are mostly factual, and the same facts (years, Articles, section numbers, composition of bodies, landmark cases) are recycled every cycle. These notes cover every listed topic and sub-topic in detail.

### 0.1 Paper pattern (verify against the current NTA bulletin)

- Paper 2 (Law) has 100 objective questions of 2 marks each (200 marks); Paper 1 has 50 questions (100 marks). Both are taken in a single 3-hour sitting. The official notices state that there is no negative marking.

- The paper is divided into 10 units, so Unit VIII typically contributes roughly 8 to 12 questions. The split is not fixed, but it is always a mix of environment law and human rights law.

- Question formats seen in recent cycles: (a) direct one-line factual questions; (b) Statement I / Statement II (true-false pairs); (c) Match List I with List II (case with principle, convention with year, Article with subject, commission with statute); (d) "which of the following is/are correct" multi-statement questions; (e) chronological ordering of conferences or instruments; (f) occasional Assertion-Reason.

- Public question banks for this unit show that questions on the UN Human Rights Council, the UN Decade for Human Rights Education (1995–2004) and the body that constitutes the NHRC have been asked. Expect the same style of fact-recall again.

### 0.2 Trends you can plan around

1.  **The human rights half is steadier and more factual** (UDHR, ICCPR, ICESCR, Optional Protocols, Vienna Declaration 1993, the Protection of Human Rights Act 1993 and its amendments of 2006 and 2019). Questions on NHRC composition, appointment and removal recur almost every cycle.

2.  **The environment half rewards case law and dates.** Vellore Citizens, M.C. Mehta (several), Rural Litigation, Subhash Kumar, Indian Council for Enviro-Legal Action, A.P. Pollution Control Board v. Nayudu, T.N. Godavarman and the Kamal Nath public trust case are the usual match-the-following material.

3.  **The NGT is the single most examined environmental topic** because it is compact: Act of 2010, Sections 14 to 22, composition, jurisdiction, limitation, appeal to the Supreme Court within 90 days, the seven scheduled Acts.

4.  **International conferences are a perennial chronological question:** Stockholm 1972, Nairobi 1982, Brundtland 1987, Rio 1992, Kyoto 1997, Johannesburg 2002, Rio+20 2012, Paris 2015, Glasgow 2021.

5.  **Group rights questions are statute-driven**: Rights of Persons with Disabilities Act 2016, Maintenance and Welfare of Parents and Senior Citizens Act 2007, Juvenile Justice Act 2015, POCSO 2012, Protection of Women from Domestic Violence Act 2005, POSH Act 2013, the minorities commission statute of 1992, and Articles 338, 338A and 338B for the SC, ST and backward classes commissions.

6.  **Recent developments are a small but real slice.** Examples covered below: the Jan Vishwas amendments to the Environment (Protection) Act 1986 (penalty regime from 1 April 2024), the Van (Sanrakshan Evam Samvardhan) Adhiniyam, India's 2026–28 UN Human Rights Council term, and the Supreme Court's 2025 Vanashakti judgment and its recall.

## Chapter 1: Meaning and Concept of Environment and Environmental Pollution

The word “environment” comes from the French *environner*, to encircle or surround. In law, it means everything that surrounds a living being and influences its life: air, water, land, climate, flora and fauna, and the interactions between them and human society. Modern environmental law moved from a narrow, resource-by-resource approach (protect a river, control a factory) to a holistic, ecosystem approach in which protection of the environment is tied to human dignity, development and the rights of future generations. This shift is why the unit begins with definitions: almost every later topic (statutes, tribunals, conferences, cases) depends on how “environment” and “pollution” are defined.

### 1.1 Concept of environment

- **Components.** Abiotic (air, water, soil, sunlight, minerals, climate) and biotic (plants, animals, microorganisms, humans). The two interact through food chains, nutrient cycles and energy flow.

- **Natural and man-made environment.** Natural: forests, rivers, atmosphere. Man-made (built): cities, industries, roads, dams. Some writers add the social and cultural environment.

- **Segments (spheres).** Atmosphere (air), hydrosphere (water), lithosphere (land and soil), biosphere (life).

- **Ecology and ecosystem.** Ecology (term by Ernst Haeckel, 1866) studies relationships between organisms and surroundings. An ecosystem is a functional unit of living things and their physical environment.

- **Statutory definition: Environment (Protection) Act 1986, s.2(a).** “Environment” includes water, air and land and the inter-relationship which exists among and between water, air and land, and human beings, other living creatures, plants, micro-organisms and property. Note the three physical elements plus the *inter-relationship*, and the inclusion of property. This is the most-asked definition in the unit.

### 1.2 Environmental pollution

Pollution is the introduction into the environment of substances or energy in such quantity or concentration that they harm living things, ecosystems, property or the quality of the environment.

**Exam angle.** Noise was brought into the definition of “air pollutant” by the 1987 amendment to the Air Act. The Water Act’s definition of pollution is wider than the EP Act’s, because it speaks of a “nuisance” and of harm to uses of water. Do not confuse “environmental pollutant” (s.2(b)) with “hazardous substance” (s.2(e)).

### 1.3 Types and sources of pollution (conceptual)

- **Air pollution:** vehicles, industry, burning of fuels and biomass, construction dust. Key pollutants: particulate matter (PM10, PM2.5), sulphur dioxide, nitrogen oxides, carbon monoxide, ozone, lead.

- **Water pollution:** untreated sewage, industrial effluents, agricultural runoff (fertilisers, pesticides), oil spills, thermal discharge.

- **Soil and land pollution:** solid and hazardous waste, mining, agro-chemicals.

- **Noise pollution:** governed in India by the Noise Pollution (Regulation and Control) Rules 2000 under the EP Act; Article 21 and the case of *Church of God (Full Gospel) in India v. K.K.R. Majestic Colony Welfare Association* (2000) hold that loudspeaker noise cannot be justified by religious freedom.

- **Marine, radioactive, plastic, electronic waste and light pollution** are further categories.

- **Point-source versus non-point source** pollution is a common conceptual distinction: a factory pipe versus agricultural runoff.

### 1.4 Principles that explain what the law is protecting

- **Sustainable development:** development that meets the needs of the present without compromising the ability of future generations to meet their own needs (Brundtland Report, *Our Common Future*, 1987). Indian courts treat it as a part of the law of the land (*Vellore Citizens Welfare Forum*, 1996).

- **Precautionary principle:** where there are threats of serious or irreversible damage, lack of full scientific certainty must not be used as a reason to postpone measures to prevent degradation. Rio Declaration Principle 15. In India, the burden of proof lies on the person proposing the activity (*A.P. Pollution Control Board v. M.V. Nayudu*, 1999).

- **Polluter pays principle:** the polluter bears the cost of remedying the damage and restoring the environment (*Indian Council for Enviro-Legal Action v. Union of India*, 1996; *Vellore*). Rio Principle 16.

- **Public trust doctrine:** the State holds natural resources as trustee for the public (*M.C. Mehta v. Kamal Nath*, 1997).

- **Intergenerational equity:** present generations hold the planet in trust for the future.

- **Absolute liability:** hazardous industries are strictly and absolutely liable for harm, with no exceptions (*M.C. Mehta v. Union of India*, the Oleum Gas Leak case, 1986/87), extending beyond *Rylands v. Fletcher*.

- **Doctrine of eco-centrism versus anthropocentrism:** anthropocentrism values nature for human use; eco-centrism (deep ecology, Arne Næss) gives nature intrinsic value. The Supreme Court has leaned towards eco-centric language in *Animal Welfare Board v. A. Nagaraja* (2014) and in cases on rivers and glaciers’ legal personhood debates; the Uttarakhand High Court’s 2017 orders granting legal personhood to the Ganga and Yamuna were stayed by the Supreme Court.

- **Right to a clean environment** is a facet of the right to life under Article 21 (*Subhash Kumar v. State of Bihar*, 1991; *Rural Litigation and Entitlement Kendra v. State of U.P.*, 1985, the Doon Valley case, the first case in which the Supreme Court ordered the closure of limestone quarries on ecological grounds).

- **Climate rights:** in *M.K. Ranjitsinh v. Union of India* (2024), the Supreme Court recognised a right against the adverse effects of climate change as part of Articles 14 and 21.

### Memory Capsule: Chapter 1

- EP Act definitions: **pollutant (b), pollution (c), handling (d), hazardous substance (e), occupier (f)**.

- Water Act pollution is s.2(e); air pollutant is s.2(a) of the Air Act.

- “Environment” in the EP Act = **water, air, land + their inter-relationship with humans, other creatures, plants, micro-organisms and property**.

## Chapter 2: International Environmental Law and UN Conferences

International environmental law (IEL) is the body of treaties, customary rules, general principles and soft-law instruments that regulate how States use and protect the shared environment. It grew out of transboundary harm disputes (a river, a smelter, a fishery) and expanded into global concerns (ozone, climate, biodiversity) after the first great UN conference at Stockholm in 1972. For the exam, this topic is chronological: learn the conferences in order, the key documents each produced, and the principle each one is known for.

### 2.1 Sources of IEL

- **Treaties and conventions** (hard law): e.g. UNFCCC, CBD, Montreal Protocol.

- **Customary international law:** the duty to prevent transboundary harm, the duty to cooperate, the duty to notify and consult, and (increasingly) the duty to conduct environmental impact assessment.

- **General principles of law** and judicial decisions (Article 38, ICJ Statute).

- **Soft law:** declarations, action plans and guidelines such as the Stockholm Declaration, Rio Declaration, Agenda 21 and the Forest Principles. They are not binding but shape treaties and domestic law, and Indian courts use them to interpret statutes.

### 2.4 The Stockholm Declaration 1972 in detail

- Attended by 113 States. Indira Gandhi’s speech (“poverty is the greatest polluter”) set out the developing-country position that environment and development cannot be separated.

- **Principle 1:** Man has the fundamental right to freedom, equality and adequate conditions of life, in an environment of a quality that permits a life of dignity and well-being, and bears a solemn responsibility to protect and improve the environment for present and future generations.

- **Principle 21:** States have, in accordance with the UN Charter and principles of international law, the sovereign right to exploit their own resources pursuant to their own environmental policies, and the responsibility to ensure that activities within their jurisdiction or control do not cause damage to the environment of other States or of areas beyond national jurisdiction. This is the classic statement of the no-harm rule.

- **Principle 22:** States shall cooperate to develop international law on liability and compensation for victims of pollution.

- **Impact on India:** the 42nd Amendment 1976 inserted **Article 48A** (Directive Principle) and **Article 51A(g)** (Fundamental Duty). The Water (Prevention and Control of Pollution) Act 1974 was enacted by Parliament under Article 252 on resolutions from States, expressly to implement decisions taken at Stockholm. The Environment (Protection) Act 1986 was enacted under Article 253 and its preamble refers to Stockholm.

### 2.5 The Rio Earth Summit 1992 in detail

- **Rio Declaration** key principles: P1 humans are at the centre of concerns for sustainable development; P3 right to development must be fulfilled so as to equitably meet developmental and environmental needs of present and future generations; P4 environmental protection is an integral part of development; P7 common but differentiated responsibilities (CBDR); P10 public participation and access to information and justice; P15 precautionary approach; P16 internalisation of environmental costs and the polluter pays principle; P17 environmental impact assessment as a national instrument; P22 role of indigenous people.

- **Agenda 21:** a non-binding action programme for the twenty-first century with 40 chapters.

- **Forest Principles:** a non-legally binding authoritative statement of principles for a global consensus on forests.

- **UNFCCC** (entered into force 1994) and **CBD** (entered into force 1993) were opened for signature at Rio.

### 2.7 Climate regime milestones (COPs)

- COP1 Berlin 1995 (Berlin Mandate); COP3 Kyoto 1997; COP13 Bali 2007 (Bali Action Plan); COP15 Copenhagen 2009 (Copenhagen Accord, \$100 billion goal); COP16 Cancun 2010 (Green Climate Fund created); COP17 Durban 2011; COP21 Paris 2015; COP26 Glasgow 2021 (Glasgow Climate Pact; first COP text to mention phasing down unabated coal power; India announced its *Panchamrit* targets and net zero by 2070); COP27 Sharm El-Sheikh 2022 (agreement to set up a loss and damage fund); COP28 Dubai 2023 (call for transition away from fossil fuels; loss and damage fund operationalised); COP29 Baku 2024 (new climate finance goal of at least \$300 billion a year by 2035); COP30 Belem, Brazil, 2025. - **International Solar Alliance:** launched by India and France at COP21 (2015).

- **Human right to a healthy environment:** the UN Human Rights Council recognised the right to a clean, healthy and sustainable environment in 2021 (Res. 48/13), and the UN General Assembly followed in July 2022 (Res. 76/300).

### 2.8 Core principles of IEL (with legal sources)

- **Sovereignty over natural resources with the responsibility not to cause transboundary harm** (Stockholm P21; Rio P2).

- **Sustainable development** (Brundtland 1987; Rio P3, P4; *Gabcikovo-Nagymaros*).

- **Intergenerational and intra-generational equity.**

- **Precautionary principle** (Rio P15).

- **Polluter pays** (Rio P16).

- **Common but differentiated responsibilities** (Rio P7; UNFCCC Art. 3).

- **Common heritage of mankind** (deep seabed under UNCLOS Part XI; Moon Agreement).

- **Common concern of humankind** (climate change, biodiversity).

- **Public participation, access to information and EIA** (Rio P10, P17).

- **Duty of cooperation, notification and consultation.**

### 2.9 Institutions

- **UNEP** (1972, Nairobi); **UN Environment Assembly (UNEA)** is its governing body with universal membership; **Global Environment Facility** (1991) funds environmental projects; **IUCN** (1948) publishes the Red List; **IPCC**; **UNFCCC Secretariat** (Bonn); **ITLOS** (Hamburg, law of the sea disputes); **Commission on Sustainable Development** (1992), succeeded by the **High-Level Political Forum**.

### 2.10 India and international environmental law

- India’s negotiating stance is built on CBDR and equity, “right to development”, and climate finance and technology transfer by developed countries.

- India ratified the Kyoto Protocol (2002) and the Paris Agreement (2 October 2016), and is a party to the Montreal Protocol, Basel, CBD and CITES. It ratified the Kigali Amendment in 2021.

- Domestic implementation: the Environment (Protection) Act 1986 (Article 253), the Biological Diversity Act 2002 (CBD), the Wild Life (Protection) Act 1972 (CITES), the Ozone Depleting Substances (Regulation and Control) Rules 2000 (Montreal Protocol), and the Hazardous and Other Wastes Rules 2016 (Basel).

- Constitutional hooks: Article 51(c) (respect for international law and treaty obligations) and Article 253 (Parliament may legislate to implement international agreements). In *Vellore Citizens Welfare Forum* (1996) the Supreme Court said that the precautionary and polluter pays principles are part of the environmental law of the country, and that customary international law not contrary to domestic law is part of it.

- India’s Paris-era commitments (as updated in 2022): reduce emissions intensity of GDP by 45% from 2005 levels by 2030; about 50% of installed electric power capacity from non-fossil sources by 2030; net zero by 2070. *Mission LiFE* (Lifestyle for Environment) was launched in 2022.

## Chapter 3: Constitutional and Legal Framework for Environment Protection in India

India’s environmental protection rests on three layers. The first is constitutional: Articles 21, 48A and 51A(g), supported by the writ jurisdiction of the Supreme Court and High Courts. The second is statutory: a body of central Acts and rules that begins with the Water Act 1974 and reaches its umbrella form in the Environment (Protection) Act 1986. The third is judicial: the courts, through public interest litigation, have made environmental law more than the statute book by reading ecological rights into Article 21 and developing doctrines such as absolute liability, the precautionary principle and the public trust. Until the 1970s the Constitution did not mention the environment at all; the Stockholm Conference and the 42nd Amendment (1976) changed that.

**Judicial reading of Article 21 on the environment**

- *Rural Litigation and Entitlement Kendra v. State of U.P.* (1985), the Doon Valley case: first Supreme Court case to treat ecological balance as a constitutional concern; limestone quarrying in the Mussoorie hills was halted, balancing the right to livelihood against the right of people to a healthy environment.

- *Subhash Kumar v. State of Bihar* (1991): the right to live includes the right to enjoyment of pollution-free water and air.

- *Charan Lal Sahu v. Union of India* (1990): the Bhopal Gas Disaster (Processing of Claims) Act 1985 was upheld; the State acts as *parens patriae*.

- *Virender Gaur v. State of Haryana* (1995): hygienic environment is an integral facet of the right to healthy life.

- *Vellore Citizens’ Welfare Forum v. Union of India* (1996): tanneries in Tamil Nadu polluting the Palar river; sustainable development, precautionary principle and polluter pays are part of Indian environmental law; the burden of proof lies on the polluter; the Court also ordered setting up an authority under s.3(3) of the EP Act.

- *M.C. Mehta v. Kamal Nath* (1997): the Span Motel diverted the course of the Beas river; public trust doctrine applied; exemplary damages imposed.

- *Hinch Lal Tiwari v. Kamala Devi* (2001): village ponds and common lands are community resources and cannot be allotted for private use.

- *Narmada Bachao Andolan v. Union of India* (2000): the Court emphasised that dam projects must balance development and environment and approved rehabilitation requirements.

### 3.2 The Environment (Protection) Act 1986 (EPA)

**Background.** Enacted after the Bhopal Gas Tragedy (the night of 2–3 December 1984, leak of methyl isocyanate from the Union Carbide plant). It is an umbrella Act passed under Article 253 to implement the decisions of the Stockholm Conference. It extends to the whole of India, came into force on **19 November 1986**, has **26 sections in four chapters**, and fills gaps left by the Water and Air Acts.

### 3.3 The Water (Prevention and Control of Pollution) Act 1974

- **First environmental statute** after Stockholm. Enacted under Article 252 after resolutions from the State legislatures; later extended to all States.

- **Machinery:** Central Pollution Control Board (CPCB, constituted in September 1974) and State Pollution Control Boards (SPCBs). Powers and functions: CPCB (s.16), State Boards (s.17). The Boards advise on standards, plan programmes, inspect, collect samples (s.21) and test.

- **Consent mechanism:** s.25 (no new industry, outlet or discharge without prior consent of the State Board); s.24 (prohibition on using a stream or well for disposing of poisonous, noxious or polluting matter); s.32 (emergency measures); s.33A (power to give directions, added in 1988); s.49 (cognizance of offences; a complaint can follow 60 days’ notice by a citizen).

- **Amendments:** 1988 (stronger penalties and citizen complaint); the **Water (Prevention and Control of Pollution) Amendment Act 2024** removed imprisonment for many offences in favour of penalties decided by an adjudicating officer, gave the Central Government a say in prescribing how the Chair of a State Board is nominated, and allowed exemption of certain industrial categories from consent.

- **Water (Prevention and Control of Pollution) Cess Act 1977:** levy and collection of a cess on water consumed by industries and local authorities to augment Board resources.

### 3.4 The Air (Prevention and Control of Pollution) Act 1981

- Enacted under Article 253 to implement the Stockholm decisions on preserving air quality. Extends to the whole of India.

- **Machinery:** CPCB and SPCBs administer it. State Governments, in consultation with the State Board, may declare **air pollution control areas** (s.19). Consent for establishing or operating an industrial plant in such an area is needed (s.21). Emission standards: s.17 and s.22. Power to approach a court to restrain emissions: s.22A. Directions: s.31A (1987 amendment).

- **Noise** was included in the definition of air pollutant by the 1987 amendment.

- **Penalties:** s.37 (failure to comply with consent provisions: imprisonment from one and a half years to six years and fine, with extended terms for continuing offences) as strengthened in 1987; moved to a penalty-based approach, along with the EPA and the Public Liability Insurance Act, by the Jan Vishwas Act 2023 (check the amended text).

- **Commission for Air Quality Management in National Capital Region and Adjoining Areas Act 2021:** created CAQM, a statutory body that replaced the EPCA (Environment Pollution (Prevention and Control) Authority, set up in 1998 on the Supreme Court’s direction in M.C. Mehta litigation).

## Chapter 4: Environmental Impact Assessment and Control of Hazardous Waste in India

Environmental Impact Assessment (EIA) is the preventive tool of environmental law: it asks, before a project starts, what it will do to air, water, land, ecology and people, and whether the harm can be avoided or reduced. Hazardous waste control is the corrective and regulatory side: it governs how dangerous wastes are generated, stored, moved, treated and disposed of, from “cradle to grave”. Both are made under the Environment (Protection) Act 1986, so they are delegated legislation (a notification and a set of rules), not Acts of Parliament.

### 4.1 EIA: concept and origin

- **Definition.** A systematic process to identify, predict, evaluate and mitigate the biophysical, social and other relevant effects of proposed projects before major decisions are taken and commitments made.

- **Origin.** The U.S. National Environmental Policy Act 1969 was the first law to require EIA. Rio Declaration Principle 17 asks States to undertake EIA for proposed activities likely to have a significant adverse impact. The ICJ in *Pulp Mills* (2010) treated EIA as a requirement of general international law for activities with significant transboundary effects.

- **India.** Environmental appraisal began in 1976–77 with river valley projects. The EIA Notification of **27 January 1994** under s.3 EPA and Rule 5(3)(d) of the Environment (Protection) Rules 1986 made prior environmental clearance (EC) mandatory for listed projects. It was replaced by the **EIA Notification 2006 (S.O. 1533(E), 14 September 2006)**, which is the operative regime, as amended many times. A draft EIA Notification 2020 was widely criticised and has not replaced the 2006 Notification.

- **Objectives:** prevention, informed decision-making, public participation, sustainable development, and a record of project commitments (conditions).

### 4.2 The EIA Notification 2006: structure

**Projects.** The Schedule lists projects (39 items in eight broad groups, for example mining, power, primary processing, manufacturing, services, physical infrastructure, building and construction) that need prior EC. Each item has thresholds by size or capacity.

**Categories.**

| Category          | Appraised by                                    | EC granted by                                         |
|-------------------|-------------------------------------------------|-------------------------------------------------------|
| A (large impacts) | Expert Appraisal Committee (EAC) at the Centre  | MoEFCC (Central Government)                           |
| B (smaller)       | State Expert Appraisal Committee (SEAC)         | State Environment Impact Assessment Authority (SEIAA) |
| B1 (within B)     | Needs a full EIA report and public consultation |                                                       |
| B2 (within B)     | No EIA report or public hearing required        |                                                       |

Category B projects are classified as B1 or B2 at the screening stage, and a project located in a critically polluted area or an ecologically sensitive zone may be treated differently under the general conditions of the Notification.

**The four stages of the EC process.**

1.  **Screening** (only for Category B projects): decides whether an EIA is needed (B1) or not (B2).

2.  **Scoping:** the EAC/SEAC determines comprehensive Terms of Reference (ToR) for the EIA study, on the basis of Form 1 and a pre-feasibility report.

3.  **Public consultation:** a public hearing near the site (organised by the State Pollution Control Board) and written responses from people likely to be affected. Certain categories are exempt, such as the modernisation of irrigation projects and expansion of highways under specified limits, and projects in industrial estates (see the Notification).

4.  **Appraisal and decision:** the EAC/SEAC examines the EIA report and public consultation record and recommends grant or rejection; the regulatory authority decides, normally by accepting the recommendation. The prescribed timeline is about 105 days in total (60 days for appraisal, 45 days for the decision).

- **After clearance:** the project proponent must submit compliance reports on conditions (half-yearly), and the authority can monitor and revoke. EC validity differs by project type, with longer periods for mining and river valley projects, and has been extended in later amendments; check the current text.

- **Contents of an EIA report:** project description, baseline environmental data, prediction and evaluation of impacts, analysis of alternatives, Environmental Management Plan (EMP), environmental monitoring plan, risk assessment, disaster management plan, cost-benefit analysis, and summary.

### 4.3 Related tools and concepts

- **Strategic Environmental Assessment** (policies, plans and programmes) versus project EIA; **Cumulative impact assessment**; **Social Impact Assessment** under the Right to Fair Compensation and Transparency in Land Acquisition, Rehabilitation and Resettlement Act 2013; **Environmental audit**; **Carrying capacity studies**.

- **Coastal Regulation Zone (CRZ) Notification 2019** (under the EPA) regulates activity along coastlines (CRZ-I to IV) and requires clearance for permitted projects.

- **Eco-Sensitive Zones** around protected areas are notified under the EPA; **critically polluted areas** and the Comprehensive Environmental Pollution Index are used to restrict new industries.

- **Forest clearance** (under the Van Adhiniyam 1980) and **wildlife clearance** run alongside EC; the EC is an additional, not a substitute, requirement.

### 4.4 EIA: judicial approach and controversies

- *Lafarge Umiam Mining v. Union of India* (2011): environmental clearances must be given on the basis of sustainable development, precaution and intergenerational equity; courts accept a role for expert bodies and for independent regulators.

- *Utkarsh Mandal v. Union of India* (2009, Delhi High Court) on the public-notice requirement for amendments to the Notification.

- *Hanuman Laxman Aroskar v. Union of India* (2019): EIA is not a formality; the Court held that the committee must examine cumulative impact and quashed a clearance for the Mopa airport (Goa) for want of proper appraisal and sent it back.

- *Common Cause v. Union of India* (2017) and *Alembic Pharmaceuticals v. Rohit Prajapati* (2020): ex post facto (retrospective) clearance is incompatible with the precautionary principle, save in exceptional cases.

- *Vanashakti v. Union of India* (May 2025) struck down the 2017 notification and 2021 office memorandum on ex post facto clearances; a three-judge bench recalled the judgment on 18 November 2025 by a 2:1 majority (Justice Bhuyan dissenting) and asked that the matter be heard afresh. The position remains under consideration.

- **Recent changes:** a January 2025 notification exempted industrial sheds, educational institutions and hostels from EC up to a built-up area of 1,50,000 square metres (examined by the Supreme Court in 2025); late 2025 proposals sought to exempt common effluent treatment plants and common municipal solid-waste facilities; the Jan Vishwas reforms changed penalties for non-compliance. Treat all of these as developing law.

### 4.5 Hazardous waste: concept and framework

- **Hazardous waste** (Rule 3 of the 2016 Rules): any waste which, by reason of its physical, chemical, reactive, toxic, flammable, explosive or corrosive characteristics, causes danger or is likely to cause danger to health or environment, whether alone or in contact with other wastes or substances. It includes wastes from processes listed in **Schedule I**, wastes containing constituents in **Schedule II** above prescribed concentration limits, and wastes in **Schedule III** (Parts A and B, relating to Basel Convention lists). **Schedule IV** lists “other wastes” such as waste tyres, paper waste, metal scrap and used electronic items.

- **Legal chain:** Hazardous Wastes (Management and Handling) Rules 1989 → 2003 amendment → Hazardous Wastes (Management, Handling and Transboundary Movement) Rules 2008 → **Hazardous and Other Wastes (Management and Transboundary Movement) Rules, 2016** (HOWM Rules, in force 4 April 2016, replaced the 2008 Rules).

- **Key features of the 2016 Rules:** emphasis on waste minimisation, reuse, recycling and recovery before disposal; the **occupier** (generator) is responsible for safe storage, packaging, labelling and disposal; authorisation from the SPCB is required for generating, handling and disposing of waste; the **manifest** system tracks waste from generation to disposal; storage limited to a period not exceeding 90 days (reflected in the 2024 amendment); **import of hazardous waste for disposal is prohibited**; import for recycling or reuse requires permission from the Ministry; **Prior Informed Consent** applies to transboundary movement, in line with the Basel Convention; the CPCB and SPCBs maintain inventories and develop guidelines; treatment, storage and disposal facilities (TSDFs) must meet standards; **Extended Producer Responsibility** (EPR) applies to waste tyres and used oil.

- **Recent amendments:** the 2024 Amendment Rules revised EPR deadlines for waste tyres and used oil, set price limits for EPR certificates and permitted import of certain pre-consumer polyethylene waste subject to conditions; the **2025 Amendment Rules (in force 1 April 2026)** added a new Chapter VIII on EPR for scrap of non-ferrous metals (aluminium, copper, zinc and alloys), with recycling targets rising from 10% in 2026–27 towards 75% by 2032–33, EPR certificates, a CPCB portal and environmental compensation for non-compliance.

## Chapter 5: National Green Tribunal (NGT)

The National Green Tribunal is a specialised statutory forum for the speedy and effective disposal of cases relating to environmental protection, conservation of forests and other natural resources, enforcement of legal rights relating to the environment, and relief and compensation for damage to persons and property. It was created by the **National Green Tribunal Act 2010** (Act 19 of 2010) and began functioning on **18 October 2010**. India became the third country in the world, after Australia and New Zealand, to set up a specialised environmental tribunal, and the first developing country to do so. Because the statute is short and has numbered sections, this is the most examinable topic in the environmental half of the unit.

### 5.1 Why the NGT was created

- Environmental litigation was clogging the High Courts and the Supreme Court, which were deciding technical questions without scientific expertise. In *M.C. Mehta v. Union of India* (1986), *Indian Council for Enviro-Legal Action v. Union of India* (1996) and *A.P. Pollution Control Board v. M.V. Nayudu* (1999) the Supreme Court recommended specialised environmental courts with judicial and technical members. The Law Commission of India (186th Report, 2003) made the same proposal.

- Earlier statutes had failed: the **National Environment Tribunal Act 1995** (strict liability tribunal) was never brought into operation, and the **National Environment Appellate Authority Act 1997** set up the NEAA, which handled appeals against environmental clearances. The NGT Act 2010 repealed both.

- Constitutional anchor: Article 21 (right to a healthy environment), supported by Articles 48A and 51A(g). The Act says in its preamble that it is for effective and expeditious disposal of environmental cases and for giving effect to the Stockholm Declaration 1972 and the Rio Declaration 1992 (to which India is committed).

### 5.2 Composition and constitution (ss.3–9)

- **Establishment (s.3):** by notification of the Central Government. Principal Bench at **New Delhi**; regional (zonal) benches at **Bhopal, Pune, Kolkata and Chennai**; circuit benches sit at other places (for example Shimla, Shillong, Jodhpur and Kochi).

- **Composition (s.4):** a full-time **Chairperson** plus full-time **Judicial Members** and **Expert Members**, numbering not less than ten and not more than twenty of each kind, as notified (the combined figure of 10 to 20 members is how the section is commonly summarised; check the bare Act for the wording and later changes). A bench comprises at least one judicial member and one expert member.

- **Qualifications:** the Chairperson is a sitting or retired Judge of the Supreme Court or Chief Justice of a High Court. A Judicial Member is a sitting or former Judge of a High Court. An Expert Member has a qualifying degree (science, engineering or technology or equivalent) and substantial experience (about fifteen years, with a part in environmental or forest matters), or administrative experience in the field.

- **Appointment:** by the Central Government on the recommendation of a Selection Committee. **Term:** originally five years, not eligible for reappointment; later tribunal-reform legislation standardised service conditions and age limits (70 for the Chairperson and 67 for members); check the current provision.

- **Specialised members:** the principle behind the NGT is that **expert members sit with judicial members** so that science and law are brought together.

**Not in Schedule I:** the Wild Life (Protection) Act 1972, the Indian Forest Act 1927, and State laws on the preservation of trees. This omission is often tested, and the Supreme Court has said that where a case needs to be heard on such laws, the High Court or the Supreme Court may entertain it (and the NGT can still deal with the questions that arise under a Schedule I Act). The NGT may also not entertain questions that have no “substantial question relating to environment”.

**“Substantial question relating to environment”** (explained in s.2(m)) is involved where (a) there is a direct violation of a specific statutory environmental obligation by which the community at large (other than an individual or group of individuals) is affected or likely to be affected, or the gravity of damage to environment or property is substantial, or the damage to public health is broadly measurable; and (b) the environmental consequences relate to a specific activity or a point source of pollution.

### 5.4 Liability, procedure and powers (ss.17–22)

- **s.17 Liability:** the Tribunal awards relief and compensation where death, injury or damage to property results from an accident or adverse impact of an activity under the Schedule I enactments; it applies the principle of **no fault**. The scale of compensation follows the framework of the Public Liability Insurance Act.

- **s.18 Procedure:** applications for relief may be made by the person injured, the owner of the damaged property, their legal representatives, any person aggrieved including a representative body or organisation, the Central or State Government or a local authority. The Tribunal must **endeavour to dispose of an application or appeal within six months** of filing (s.18(3)). The Supreme Court has held that the NGT can act **suo motu** (*Municipal Corporation of Greater Mumbai v. Ankita Sinha*, 2021).

- **s.19 Powers:** the Tribunal is **not bound by the Code of Civil Procedure 1908 or the Indian Evidence Act 1872** but is guided by **principles of natural justice**. It has the powers of a civil court for discovery, summoning, receiving evidence on affidavit, issuing commissions, reviewing its own decisions, and dismissing or setting aside ex parte decisions. It regulates its own procedure.

- **s.20 Principles to apply:** the Tribunal must apply the principles of **sustainable development, the precautionary principle and the polluter pays principle**.

- **s.21 Execution:** an order is executable as a decree of a civil court.

- **s.22 Appeal:** to the **Supreme Court within 90 days** from the date of communication of the award, decision or order of the Tribunal.

- **s.26 Penalty for non-compliance:** imprisonment up to three years or fine up to Rs 10 crore, or both; for a company, fine up to Rs 25 crore; a continuing contravention attracts an additional fine of up to Rs 25,000 per day.

- **s.29 Bar of civil courts:** no civil court can entertain matters that the Tribunal can decide.

- Rules: National Green Tribunal (Practice and Procedure) Rules 2011.

### 5.5 Key features and doctrines

- **Not bound by the Evidence Act and CPC** (s.19), so it can take up cases quickly and use experts.

- **Expert and judicial members together**; the experts are not mere advisers but members of the bench.

- **Quick disposal target of six months** (s.18(3)).

- **Polluter pays and no-fault liability** embedded in the Act (ss.17 and 20).

- **Relief to victims and restitution of the environment** (s.15), so the NGT is a remedial forum as well as an enforcement forum.

- **Environmental compensation:** the NGT has awarded compensation on the basis of the polluter pays principle in many cases, for example the 2016 order imposing Rs 5 crore on the Art of Living Foundation for damage to the Yamuna floodplain.

- **Supreme Court decisions on the NGT:** the NGT Act was upheld; the Court said that environmental matters should be heard by the NGT in the first instance (*Bhopal Gas Peedith Mahila Udyog Sangathan v. Union of India*, 2012); the Court set aside the NGT’s order permitting reopening of the Sterlite copper smelter (Tamil Nadu Pollution Control Board v. Sterlite, 2019); *Ankita Sinha* confirmed suo motu power.

## Chapter 6: Concept and Development of Human Rights

Human rights are the basic rights and freedoms to which every person is entitled simply by being human, regardless of nationality, sex, race, religion, language or status. The idea moved from religious and philosophical thought (natural law), through the great constitutional documents of the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries, to its modern international form in the UN Charter (1945) and the Universal Declaration of Human Rights (1948). For the exam, you need three things: the definitions (particularly the statutory definition in India), the theories and generations of rights, and the historical timeline with its dates.

### 6.1 Definition and characteristics

- **Statutory definition (Protection of Human Rights Act 1993, s.2(1)(d)):** “human rights” means the rights relating to **life, liberty, equality and dignity of the individual** guaranteed by the Constitution or embodied in the International Covenants and **enforceable by courts in India**. Under s.2(1)(f), “International Covenants” means the **ICCPR and the ICESCR**, adopted by the UN General Assembly on 16 December 1966. Note that the Indian definition does not list the UDHR.

- **Characteristics:** inherent (not granted by the State), inalienable, universal, equal and non-discriminatory, indivisible, interdependent and interrelated (Vienna Declaration 1993), and (for many rights) enforceable against the State.

- **Human rights versus fundamental rights:** human rights are moral and international in origin and universal; fundamental rights are those the Constitution (Part III) guarantees and courts enforce under Articles 32 and 226. Some fundamental rights are available only to citizens (Articles 15, 16, 19, 29, 30), while human rights belong to everyone.

- **Derogable and non-derogable rights:** ICCPR Article 4(2) lists rights that cannot be suspended even in a public emergency: right to life (Art. 6), freedom from torture (Art. 7), freedom from slavery (Art. 8(1) and (2)), freedom from imprisonment for inability to fulfil a contractual obligation (Art. 11), no retrospective penalties (Art. 15), recognition as a person before the law (Art. 16), and freedom of thought, conscience and religion (Art. 18).

- **Jus cogens and erga omnes:** prohibition of genocide, slavery, torture and racial discrimination are peremptory norms; obligations *erga omnes* are owed to the international community as a whole (*Barcelona Traction* case, ICJ 1970).

### 6.5 The UN Charter and human rights

- **Preamble:** faith in fundamental human rights, in the dignity and worth of the human person, in the equal rights of men and women.

- **Article 1(3):** one purpose of the UN is promoting and encouraging respect for human rights and fundamental freedoms for all without distinction as to race, sex, language or religion.

- **Article 13(1)(b):** General Assembly shall make recommendations for assisting in the realisation of human rights.

- **Articles 55 and 56:** UN shall promote universal respect for human rights; all members pledge themselves to take joint and separate action in cooperation with the UN.

- **Article 62(2) and 68:** ECOSOC may make recommendations on human rights and shall set up commissions for the promotion of human rights.

- **Article 2(7)** (domestic jurisdiction) is the usual counterargument to international scrutiny.

### 6.6 UN human rights machinery

- **UN Human Rights Council (UNHRC):** created in 2006 by General Assembly Resolution 60/251 to replace the Commission on Human Rights; seats in Geneva; **47 members** elected by the General Assembly for three-year terms on the basis of regional distribution; periodic review of all UN member States through the **Universal Periodic Review** (UPR); special procedures (special rapporteurs and working groups); complaint procedure. India was elected for the 2026–28 term, its seventh term on the Council.

- **Office of the High Commissioner for Human Rights (OHCHR):** post created in 1993 (after the Vienna conference); seat in Geneva.

- **Treaty bodies:** Human Rights Committee (ICCPR; 18 members), Committee on Economic, Social and Cultural Rights, CERD, CEDAW, Committee against Torture, Committee on the Rights of the Child, Committee on Migrant Workers, Committee on the Rights of Persons with Disabilities, Committee on Enforced Disappearances.

- **Others:** General Assembly (Third Committee), ECOSOC, Security Council (human rights as a threat to peace), ICJ, International Criminal Court, UNHCR, UNICEF, ILO, UN Women.

- **Regional systems:** Europe (European Court of Human Rights, Strasbourg); the Americas (Inter-American Commission in Washington and Court at San Jose, Costa Rica); Africa (African Commission, Banjul, and the African Court); the Arab Charter (2004). Asia has no region-wide court.

### 6.7 India’s human rights tradition

- **Pre-constitutional:** the Constitution of India Bill 1895; Nehru Report 1928; the **Karachi Resolution 1931** on fundamental rights and economic programme.

- **Constitution:** Part III (Articles 12–35) and Part IV (Articles 36–51); the Preamble secures justice, liberty, equality and fraternity assuring the dignity of the individual. India voted for the UDHR in 1948 (Hansa Mehta and Lakshmi Menon took part in the debates).

- **International treaties:** India ratified CERD (1968), the ICCPR and ICESCR (10 April 1979), CEDAW (1993), CRC (1992) and CRPD (2007). It has signed but not ratified the Convention against Torture (signed 1997); it is not a party to the Optional Protocols to the ICCPR and has not ratified the Refugee Convention.

- **Domestic implementation:** Article 51(c); Article 253; the Protection of Human Rights Act 1993 (see section 10). In *Vishaka v. State of Rajasthan* (1997) the Court used CEDAW to fill a legislative gap; in *Jolly George Varghese* (1980) the Court read the ICCPR in aid of the Constitution; in *Nilabati Behera v. State of Orissa* (1993), compensation was awarded as a public-law remedy for custodial death.

- **Judicial landmarks:** *A.K. Gopalan* (1950) to *Maneka Gandhi v. Union of India* (1978), which read due process into Article 21 and made the doctrine of the right to life expansive; *Hussainara Khatoon* (1979, speedy trial), *Sunil Batra* (1978/80, prisoners), *Francis Coralie Mullin* (1981, dignity), *D.K. Basu v. State of West Bengal* (1997, arrest guidelines), *Justice K.S. Puttaswamy v. Union of India* (2017, privacy as a fundamental right), *Navtej Singh Johar* (2018).

## Chapter 7: Universalism and Cultural Relativism

The central philosophical dispute in international human rights is whether human rights are the same everywhere (universalism) or whether their meaning and validity depend on the culture in which they operate (cultural relativism). The debate is not purely academic. It determines whether a State can defend a practice (a punishment, a dress code, a family law rule, a restriction on speech) as “our culture” against international criticism, and it explains reservations to human rights treaties, regional charters, and the Asian values and Islamic human rights declarations. For the exam, know the definitions, the main arguments on each side, the instruments (Vienna Declaration 1993, Bangkok Declaration 1993, Cairo Declaration 1990), the thinkers, and the compromise positions.

### 7.1 Universalism

- **Core claim:** human rights belong to every human being simply as a human being, everywhere and at all times, because of shared human dignity. They are not created by States or cultures, so no culture or government can legitimately deny them.

- **Foundations:** natural law and natural rights (Locke, Kant on dignity), the idea of a common humanity, the moral minimum necessary for a decent life.

- **Textual support:** the UDHR (1948) claims to be “a common standard of achievement for all peoples and all nations”; the **Vienna Declaration and Programme of Action 1993** (Part I, paragraph 1) says the universal nature of human rights is beyond question, and paragraph 5 says all human rights are **universal, indivisible, interdependent and interrelated**, that national and regional particularities and different historical, cultural and religious backgrounds must be borne in mind, but that it is the duty of States, whatever their political, economic and cultural systems, to promote and protect all human rights and fundamental freedoms.

- **Evidence of universality:** near-universal ratification of the Convention on the Rights of the Child; wide acceptance of the ICCPR and ICESCR; the status of some norms as customary law or *jus cogens* (prohibition of genocide, torture, slavery); the Universal Periodic Review of every State.

- **Arguments for universalism:** (a) cultures are not monolithic and have internal dissenters (women, minorities, dissidents) whose rights relativism would silence; (b) relativism is often invoked by ruling elites rather than by the people; (c) practices such as female genital mutilation, honour killings, child marriage and slavery cannot be justified by tradition; (d) every major culture contains ideas of dignity and justice, so rights are not purely “Western”; (e) a rejection of universality leads to moral relativism with no basis to condemn atrocities.

### 7.2 Cultural relativism

- **Core claim:** moral values and rights are products of particular cultures, so there is no culture-independent standard by which one culture may judge another; the universal claim of rights is a form of cultural imperialism or ethnocentrism.

- **Origins:** the American Anthropological Association submitted a statement to the UN Commission on Human Rights in 1947 (drafted by Melville Herskovits) warning that a declaration reflecting only one culture’s values would not command respect; the same logic underlies the abstentions at the UDHR vote (Soviet bloc States, South Africa, Saudi Arabia).

- **Types:** **radical (strong) relativism** holds culture is the sole source of validity of a moral right or rule. **Moderate (weak) relativism** accepts a core of universal rights but allows cultural variation in interpretation and implementation.

- **Arguments for relativism:** (a) the UDHR was drafted mostly by Western States and reflects individualism; many societies stress community, family and duties; (b) colonial history and selective use of human rights to pursue political interests (“double standards”); (c) priority of economic development and collective rights in the developing world (Declaration on the Right to Development 1986); (d) respect for the right of peoples to cultural self-determination.

- **Instruments associated with relativist positions:**

  - **Bangkok Declaration 1993** (Asian States, before Vienna): human rights are universal, but must be considered in the context of a dynamic and evolving process of norm-setting, bearing in mind national and regional particularities and different historical, cultural and religious backgrounds; stresses the right to development and non-interference.

  - **Cairo Declaration on Human Rights in Islam 1990** (Organisation of the Islamic Conference): rights are subject to the Islamic Shari’ah (Articles 24 and 25).

  - **African Charter on Human and Peoples’ Rights 1981 (Banjul Charter):** recognises peoples’ rights and imposes duties on individuals towards family, society and the State.

  - **Arab Charter on Human Rights 2004; ASEAN Human Rights Declaration 2012.**

  - **“Asian values”** (Lee Kuan Yew of Singapore, Mahathir Mohamad of Malaysia): emphasis on community, order, family, and economic development ahead of civil and political liberties.

  - **Reservations** to human rights treaties (for example to Articles 2 and 16 of CEDAW) based on religious or cultural law; reservations incompatible with the object and purpose of the treaty are invalid under Article 19 of the Vienna Convention on the Law of Treaties and General Comment 24 of the Human Rights Committee (1994).

### 7.4 Related concepts

- **Ethnocentrism** (judging other cultures by one’s own), **cultural imperialism**, **moral relativism**, **cultural pluralism**, **cultural rights** (Article 27 UDHR; Article 15 ICESCR; UNESCO Universal Declaration on Cultural Diversity 2001; UN Declaration on the Rights of Indigenous Peoples 2007).

- **Indivisibility** (Western States prioritised civil-political rights, socialist and developing States prioritised socio-economic rights; the two Covenants in 1966 were the compromise).

- **Cultural defence** in criminal law (courts accept cultural background as relevant to mitigation, not as a justification).

### 7.5 The Indian perspective

- India’s official position favours universality along with indivisibility and the right to development, plus respect for cultural diversity; at Vienna (1993) India argued against using human rights conditionally to pressure developing States.

- Constitutional approach: the Constitution is itself a universalist rights document adapted to a plural society. It protects religious freedom (Articles 25–28) and minority cultural rights (Articles 29–30) but subordinates religious and cultural practices to public order, morality, health and the other fundamental rights.

- Courts and culture:

  - *Commissioner of Hindu Religious Endowments v. Sri Lakshmindra Thirtha Swamiar of Sri Shirur Mutt* (1954) and the later “essential religious practices” test.

  - *Mohd. Ahmed Khan v. Shah Bano Begum* (1985): maintenance for a divorced Muslim woman under s.125 CrPC; the legislative response (Muslim Women (Protection of Rights on Divorce) Act 1986) shows the cultural-rights tension.

  - *Shayara Bano v. Union of India* (2017): instant triple talaq (talaq-e-biddat) held unconstitutional.

  - *Indian Young Lawyers Association v. State of Kerala* (2018, Sabarimala): restriction on women of menstruating age held unconstitutional by majority; the dissent argued for judicial restraint in matters of faith. A larger bench reference on the scope of religious freedom was pending after the 2019 review reference.

  - *Navtej Singh Johar v. Union of India* (2018): constitutional morality prevails over social morality.

  - *Independent Thought v. Union of India* (2017): sexual intercourse with a wife below 18 is rape (children’s rights over cultural acceptance of child marriage).

  - Statutory interventions against customary practices: abolition of untouchability (Article 17 and the Protection of Civil Rights Act 1955), Commission of Sati (Prevention) Act 1987, Child Marriage Restraint Act 1929 replaced by the Prohibition of Child Marriage Act 2006, the Dowry Prohibition Act 1961.

## Chapter 8: International Bill of Rights

The “International Bill of Rights” (more formally the International Bill of Human Rights) is the core of the UN human rights system. It consists of the **Universal Declaration of Human Rights (UDHR) 1948**, the **International Covenant on Civil and Political Rights (ICCPR) 1966** with its two Optional Protocols, and the **International Covenant on Economic, Social and Cultural Rights (ICESCR) 1966** (with its Optional Protocol of 2008). The UN Commission on Human Rights under Eleanor Roosevelt originally planned a single three-part “Bill” (declaration, covenant, measures of implementation). Cold War divisions over civil-political versus socio-economic rights led to two separate covenants. This is one of the highest-yield topics in the unit because every fact in it is a number, a date or an Article.

### 8.1 Universal Declaration of Human Rights, 1948

**Adoption and drafting**

- Adopted by the UN General Assembly on **10 December 1948** (Resolution 217 A (III)) at the Palais de Chaillot, Paris, by **48 votes in favour, none against and 8 abstentions** (the Soviet Union, Byelorussian SSR, Ukrainian SSR, Czechoslovakia, Poland, Yugoslavia, Saudi Arabia and South Africa). Two members were absent (Honduras and Yemen). 10 December is celebrated as **Human Rights Day**.

- Drafted by the Commission on Human Rights (chair: **Eleanor Roosevelt**). The eight-member drafting committee included Roosevelt, **René Cassin** (France), **Charles Malik** (Lebanon), **P.C. Chang** (China) and **John Humphrey** (Canada, Director of the UN Division of Human Rights, whose first draft was the working text), Hernán Santa Cruz (Chile), Alexandre Bogomolov (USSR) and Charles Dukes (UK). India’s **Hansa Mehta** is credited with changing “all men are born free and equal” to “all human beings are born free and equal”.

- Structure: a **Preamble and 30 Articles**.

- **Legal status:** a declaration, not a treaty, so not binding in itself. It has acquired strong legal significance: the 1968 Proclamation of Teheran stated that it states a common understanding of the peoples of the world and constitutes an obligation for the members of the international community; many of its provisions are regarded as customary international law; it has been incorporated into national constitutions and cited by courts (including the Indian Supreme Court).

| 3–11     | Individual civil rights                | Life, liberty and security (3); no slavery (4); no torture (5); recognition before the law (6); equality before the law (7); effective remedy (8); no arbitrary arrest (9); fair and public hearing (10); presumption of innocence and no retroactive penalty (11)                                                                                       |
| 12–17    | Relations to civil society             | Privacy (12); movement (13); asylum (14); nationality (15); marriage and family (16); property (17)                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                      |

### 8.2 International Covenant on Civil and Political Rights, 1966

- Adopted on **16 December 1966** (GA Resolution 2200A (XXI)); in force **23 March 1976** (three months after the 35th ratification). It has a preamble and **53 Articles in six Parts**.

- **Article 1:** right of all peoples to self-determination (common to ICCPR and ICESCR); permanent sovereignty over natural wealth and resources.

- **Part II (Arts. 2–5):** States undertake to **respect and ensure** the rights to all individuals within their territory and subject to their jurisdiction (Art. 2(1)); effective remedy (Art. 2(3)); equal rights of men and women (Art. 3); **derogation in public emergency** (Art. 4), with no derogation from Arts. 6, 7, 8 (paragraphs 1 and 2), 11, 15, 16 and 18.

- **Part III (Arts. 6–27), the substantive rights:**

| Article | Right                                                                                                                          |

### 8.3 International Covenant on Economic, Social and Cultural Rights, 1966

- Adopted on **16 December 1966**; in force **3 January 1976**. It has a preamble and **31 Articles in five Parts**.

## Chapter 9: Group Rights: Women, Children, Persons with Disabilities, Elderly, Minorities and Weaker Sections

“Group rights” here means the rights of groups that are historically disadvantaged, numerically small or otherwise vulnerable, and which need special protection beyond the general guarantee of equality. The legal idea is **substantive equality**: treating unequals equally can itself be discrimination, so the Constitution (Articles 14 to 16, 46) and international law (non-discrimination plus “special measures”) permit and sometimes require protective discrimination. The syllabus covers six groups. For each group, learn four layers: constitutional provisions, international instruments, statutes, and landmark cases.

### 9.0 Common constitutional scheme for protective discrimination

- **Article 15(3):** special provisions for women and children. **Art. 15(4):** advancement of socially and educationally backward classes, SC and ST. **Art. 15(5):** reservation in admission to educational institutions, including private unaided (except minority institutions) (93rd Amendment 2005). **Art. 15(6):** up to 10% reservation for economically weaker sections (103rd Amendment 2019).

- **Art. 16(4), 16(4A), 16(4B):** reservation in public employment, promotion and carry-forward; **Art. 16(6):** 10% EWS reservation in posts.

- **Art. 17:** abolition of untouchability. **Art. 23:** prohibition of trafficking and forced labour. **Art. 24:** no child below 14 in hazardous work.

- **Art. 29 and 30:** cultural and educational rights of minorities. **Art. 46:** State shall promote with special care the educational and economic interests of SC, ST and other weaker sections and protect them from social injustice and exploitation.

- **Art. 330, 332, 334:** reservation of seats for SC and ST in the House of the People and State Legislative Assemblies (extended to 2030 by the 104th Amendment 2019/2020). **Art. 243D and 243T:** reservation of seats in Panchayats and Municipalities for SC, ST and women (at least one-third for women).

- **Constitution (106th Amendment) Act 2023** (Nari Shakti Vandan Adhiniyam): one-third reservation for women in the Lok Sabha, State Legislative Assemblies and the Delhi Assembly (new Arts. 330A, 332A, 239AA); it will operate after the next census and delimitation.

- **Art. 38, 39(a)–(f), 41, 42, 43, 45, 47:** Directive Principles on welfare, equal pay, protection of children, work and assistance in old age and disablement, maternity relief.

### 9.2 Children

**International framework**

- Geneva Declaration of the Rights of the Child 1924; **UN Declaration of the Rights of the Child 1959**; **Convention on the Rights of the Child (CRC)**: adopted **20 November 1989**, in force **2 September 1990**, 54 Articles, the most widely ratified human rights treaty. “Child” means every human being below 18 unless majority is attained earlier (Art. 1). Four guiding principles: **non-discrimination (Art. 2), best interests of the child (Art. 3), right to life, survival and development (Art. 6), respect for the views of the child (Art. 12)**.

- Monitoring: Committee on the Rights of the Child (18 experts). **Optional Protocols (2000):** on the involvement of children in armed conflict and on the sale of children, child prostitution and child pornography (India ratified both in 2005); third Protocol on a communications procedure (2011; not ratified by India). **India ratified the CRC on 11 December 1992.**

- Other instruments: ILO Convention 138 (minimum age) and 182 (worst forms of child labour), both ratified by India in 2017; Hague Convention on Intercountry Adoption 1993 (India party since 2003); UNICEF (1946).

**Constitutional provisions:** Art. 15(3); **Art. 21A** (free and compulsory education for children 6 to 14 years, inserted by the 86th Amendment 2002); **Art. 24**; **Art. 39(e) and (f)** (health and strength of children, opportunities and facilities for healthy development); **Art. 45** (early childhood care and education below six); **Art. 51A(k)** (duty of parents or guardians to provide opportunities for education for children six to fourteen).

### 9.3 Persons with disabilities

**International framework**

- Declaration on the Rights of Disabled Persons 1975; International Year of Disabled Persons 1981; UN Decade of Disabled Persons 1983–92; World Programme of Action 1982; Standard Rules on the Equalization of Opportunities for Persons with Disabilities 1993. ### 9.4 Elderly persons

**International framework:** Vienna International Plan of Action on Ageing 1982 (First World Assembly on Ageing); **UN Principles for Older Persons 1991** (GA Resolution 46/91: independence, participation, care, self-fulfilment, dignity); International Year of Older Persons 1999; **Madrid International Plan of Action on Ageing 2002**; International Day of Older Persons on **1 October**; Open-ended Working Group on Ageing (2010); UN Decade of Healthy Ageing 2021–2030; the Inter-American Convention on Protecting the Human Rights of Older Persons 2015 is the first binding regional treaty on the subject.

**Constitutional provisions:** Art. 41 (public assistance in old age); Art. 21; Art. 47; Seventh Schedule, Concurrent List Entry 24 (welfare of labour, social security and social insurance).

**Maintenance and Welfare of Parents and Senior Citizens Act 2007 (MWPSC Act)**

- **Senior citizen:** a citizen of India aged **60 years or above**. **Parent** includes a biological, adoptive or step-father or mother, whether or not a senior citizen.

- **Duty of maintenance:** children (and relatives who inherit the property of a childless senior citizen) must maintain parents and senior citizens so that they can lead a normal life. A senior citizen may apply to a **Maintenance Tribunal** (constituted at the sub-division level, usually headed by the Sub-Divisional Officer), with appeal to an **Appellate Tribunal** (District Magistrate). Tribunals are to decide within 90 days (extendable once by 30 days).

- **Maintenance allowance:** the 2007 Act capped the monthly allowance at Rs 10,000; the 2019 Amendment Bill (not enacted) proposed removing the cap and widening the definition of children; check the present position.

- **Protection of property and life:** s.23 renders a transfer of property by a senior citizen void if the transferee fails to provide basic amenities and physical needs (applied in *Sudesh Chhikara v. Ramti Devi*, 2022); s.22 requires State action to protect life and property; s.19 requires State Governments to establish old age homes (at least one in each district); s.24 punishes abandonment of a senior citizen (imprisonment up to three months, or fine up to Rs 5,000, or both).

- Related laws: s.125 CrPC (now the corresponding BNSS provision) and s.20 of the Hindu Adoptions and Maintenance Act 1956 (maintenance of parents).

- *S. Vanitha v. Deputy Commissioner, Bengaluru Urban District* (2020): the Supreme Court harmonised the remedies under the 2007 Act and the Protection of Women from Domestic Violence Act, and recognised a senior citizen’s right to seek eviction from a shared household in appropriate cases.

- **Policies and schemes:** National Policy on Older Persons 1999; Integrated Programme for Older Persons; National Programme for Health Care of the Elderly; Indira Gandhi National Old Age Pension Scheme (National Social Assistance Programme); Rashtriya Vayoshri Yojana; Pradhan Mantri Vaya Vandana Yojana; Ayushman Vay Vandana (health cover for persons aged 70 and above).

### 9.5 Minorities

**Concept and definition**

- The Indian Constitution uses the word “minorities” in Arts. 29 and 30 and in the headings of Arts. 350A and 350B but **does not define** it. The courts have said that minorities are determined **State-wise** (for linguistic minorities, *Re Kerala Education Bill*, 1958 and *D.A.V. College v. State of Punjab*, 1971; for religious minorities, *T.M.A. Pai Foundation v. State of Karnataka*, 2002, by 11 judges).

- International definitions: **Capotorti (1977):** a group numerically inferior to the rest of the population, in a non-dominant position, whose members, being nationals of the State, have ethnic, religious or linguistic characteristics different from the rest and show a sense of solidarity directed towards preserving their culture, traditions, religion or language. **Deschenes (1985)** is a similar definition.

**International instruments:** **ICCPR Art. 27** (persons belonging to ethnic, religious or linguistic minorities shall not be denied the right, in community with others, to enjoy their culture, profess and practise their religion, or use their language); **UN Declaration on the Rights of Persons Belonging to National or Ethnic, Religious and Linguistic Minorities, 1992** (GA Resolution 47/135, 18 December 1992); the Working Group on Minorities (1995–2007), then the Forum on Minority Issues (2007); the UN Special Rapporteur on minority issues (2005); the Council of Europe’s Framework Convention for the Protection of National Minorities (1995). India observes **18 December as Minorities Rights Day**.

**Cases on minority rights**

- *Re Kerala Education Bill* (1958): the Art. 30(1) right is subject to reasonable regulation to secure educational standards.

- *S. Azeez Basha v. Union of India* (1968): AMU was held not to be established by the Muslim minority; the issue was reopened in *Aligarh Muslim University v. Naresh Agarwal* (2024), where a seven-judge bench by majority overruled *Azeez Basha*, holding that an institution does not lose minority status merely because a statute gave it legal form, and left the actual status of AMU for a regular bench.

- *St. Xavier’s College v. State of Gujarat* (1974): affiliation is not a matter of choice; the right is not absolute.

- *T.M.A. Pai Foundation* (2002): the State is the unit for identifying minorities; no right to maladminister; regulatory measures are allowed; *P.A. Inamdar v. State of Maharashtra* (2005): no reservation can be imposed on private unaided minority institutions.

- *Bal Patil v. Union of India* (2005): Jains were not treated as a national minority at that time (and were later notified in 2014).

- *Pramati Educational and Cultural Trust v. Union of India* (2014): the RTE Act does not apply to minority schools.

- *Waqf (Amendment) Act 2025*: a recent amendment to the law on Muslim endowments, litigated before the Supreme Court, which passed an interim order in September 2025 on certain provisions. Treat as a current-affairs item and check the latest position.

- Criminal law: provisions on promoting enmity between groups (IPC s.153A) and deliberate acts to outrage religious feelings (s.295A), with their BNS counterparts; Places of Worship (Special Provisions) Act 1991.

### 9.6 Weaker sections: Scheduled Castes, Scheduled Tribes, backward classes, EWS and others

## Chapter 10: Protection and Enforcement of Human Rights in India: NHRC and National Commissions

Human rights in India are protected by four overlapping mechanisms: (1) constitutional remedies (writs under Articles 32 and 226 and the creative use of public interest litigation and compensation); (2) the statutory human rights machinery under the **Protection of Human Rights Act 1993 (PHRA)**: the National Human Rights Commission, State Human Rights Commissions and Human Rights Courts; (3) specialised commissions for particular groups (minorities, women, SC, ST, backward classes, children and others); and (4) other legal institutions (legal services authorities, the Right to Information Act, and the Lok Adalats). The syllabus lists six bodies: **NHRC, NCM, NCW, NCSC, NCST and NCBC**. The recurring exam questions are about composition, appointment, removal, term, limitation and the binding or recommendatory nature of their orders.

### 10.1 Judicial enforcement of human rights

- **Article 32** (Dr. B.R. Ambedkar called it the “heart and soul” of the Constitution) and **Article 226** give the right to move the Supreme Court and High Courts for enforcement of rights through writs (habeas corpus, mandamus, prohibition, certiorari and quo warranto). The Supreme Court can act also on letters and on its own motion.

- **Public interest litigation:** the relaxation of locus standi in *S.P. Gupta v. Union of India* (1981, the Judges Transfer case), *Hussainara Khatoon v. State of Bihar* (1979, undertrial prisoners), *Bandhua Mukti Morcha v. Union of India* (1984) and *People’s Union for Democratic Rights v. Union of India* (1982).

- **Compensation as a public-law remedy:** *Rudul Sah v. State of Bihar* (1983, first award for illegal detention), *Bhim Singh v. State of J&K* (1985), *Sebastian Hongray v. Union of India* (1984), *Nilabati Behera v. State of Orissa* (1993), *D.K. Basu v. State of West Bengal* (1997).

- **Custodial rights and police:** *D.K. Basu* (1997) laid down arrest and detention guidelines; *Joginder Kumar v. State of U.P.* (1994) on arrest; *Prakash Singh v. Union of India* (2006) on police reforms; *People’s Union for Civil Liberties v. State of Maharashtra* (2014) on the procedure for investigating police encounters.

- **Armed forces:** *Naga People’s Movement of Human Rights v. Union of India* (1997) upheld the Armed Forces (Special Powers) Act 1958; *Extra Judicial Execution Victim Families Association v. Union of India* (2016) held that there is no absolute immunity for excesses in AFSPA areas.

- **Human Rights Courts (PHRA ss.30–31):** the State Government, with the concurrence of the Chief Justice of the High Court, may specify a Court of Session in each district as a Human Rights Court for speedy trial of offences arising from violations of human rights, and appoint a Special Public Prosecutor.

### 10.2 International standard: the Paris Principles

- The **Principles relating to the Status of National Institutions (Paris Principles)**, endorsed by the UN General Assembly in 1993 (Resolution 48/134, 20 December 1993), require national human rights institutions to have a broad mandate, independence (appointment and tenure), pluralism, adequate resources and investigative powers.

- Accreditation is carried out by the **Global Alliance of National Human Rights Institutions (GANHRI)** through its Sub-Committee on Accreditation: **A status** means full compliance and **B status** partial compliance. India’s NHRC has held A status, but its re-accreditation was deferred in 2023 and the sub-committee reportedly recommended downgrading in April 2025; check the latest status.

### 10.3 The National Human Rights Commission (NHRC)

**Origin.** The NHRC was constituted on **12 October 1993** under an Ordinance, and the **Protection of Human Rights Act 1993** received assent on 8 January 1994 (deemed in force from 28 September 1993). The Act has been amended in **2006** (changes to the structure and working of the Commissions) and in **2019** (composition, tenure and eligibility). The first Chairperson was **Justice Ranganath Misra**. Its seat is in New Delhi. The Chairperson since December 2024 is Justice V. Ramasubramanian (check for later changes).

**Definition clauses (s.2).** “Human rights” (s.2(1)(d)) and “International Covenants” (s.2(1)(f)), as in section 6 above; “armed forces” means the naval, military and air forces and includes any other armed forces of the Union (s.2(1)(a)).

**Composition (s.3, as amended in 2019)**

| Category                    | Members                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                         |
|-----------------------------|---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------|
| Chairperson                 | A person who has been the **Chief Justice of India or a Judge of the Supreme Court** (the pre-2019 text required a former Chief Justice of India)                                                                                                                                                                               |
| Full-time Members           | One person who is, or has been, a **Judge of the Supreme Court**; one person who is, or has been, a **Chief Justice of a High Court**; and **three Members**, of whom **at least one must be a woman**, with knowledge of or practical experience in matters relating to human rights (before 2019 there were two such Members) |
| Deemed Members (ex officio) | Chairpersons of the **National Commission for Minorities, SC, ST, Women, Backward Classes and Protection of Child Rights**, and the **Chief Commissioner for Persons with Disabilities** (before 2019, only the first four commissions)                                                                                         |
| Officers                    | **Secretary-General** (Chief Executive Officer, who exercises the powers of the Commission as delegated)                                                                                                                                                                                                                        |

**Appointment (s.4).** The Chairperson and Members are appointed by the **President** on the recommendation of a committee consisting of: **the Prime Minister (Chairperson)**, the **Speaker of the Lok Sabha**, the **Minister in charge of the Ministry of Home Affairs**, the **Leader of the Opposition in the Lok Sabha**, the **Leader of the Opposition in the Rajya Sabha**, and the **Deputy Chairman of the Rajya Sabha**. A sitting Judge of the Supreme Court or Chief Justice of a High Court can be appointed only after consultation with the Chief Justice of India. No appointment is invalid merely because of a vacancy in the committee.

**Term and eligibility (s.6).** The Chairperson and Members hold office for **three years or until the age of 70, whichever is earlier** (before 2019: five years or 70), and are **eligible for reappointment**. On ceasing to hold office, they are ineligible for further employment under the Central or State Government.

**Removal (s.5).** The President may remove the Chairperson or a Member for **proved misbehaviour or incapacity** only after the **Supreme Court**, on a reference by the President, has held an inquiry and reported that the person ought to be removed. The President may also remove by order a person who (a) is adjudged insolvent; (b) engages during his term in paid employment outside the duties of his office; (c) is unfit to continue by reason of infirmity of mind or body; (d) is of unsound mind and so declared by a competent court; or (e) is convicted and sentenced to imprisonment for an offence which, in the President’s opinion, involves moral turpitude. Resignation is by a letter addressed to the President. The model is the same as for removal of a Judge, except that the Supreme Court makes the inquiry on the President’s reference.

**Functions (s.12).** The Commission shall:

- \(a\) inquire, **suo motu** or on a petition by a victim or a person on his behalf, or on a direction or order of any court, into a complaint of **violation of human rights or abetment thereof**, or **negligence in the prevention of such violation by a public servant**;

- \(b\) **intervene** in any proceeding involving an allegation of violation of human rights pending before a court, with the approval of that court;

- \(c\) **visit** jails and other institutions under the control of the State Government where persons are detained or lodged for treatment, reformation or protection, and study the living conditions of inmates (under intimation to the State Government);

- \(d\) review the safeguards for the protection of human rights under the Constitution or any law and recommend measures for effective implementation;

- \(e\) review the factors, including acts of terrorism, that inhibit the enjoyment of human rights and recommend remedial measures;

- \(f\) study treaties and other international instruments on human rights and make recommendations for their effective implementation;

- \(g\) undertake and promote research in the field of human rights;

- \(h\) spread human rights literacy among various sections of society and promote awareness of the safeguards;

- \(i\) encourage the efforts of non-governmental organisations and institutions working in the field of human rights;

- \(j\) perform such other functions as it considers necessary for the promotion of human rights.

**Powers and procedure.** Powers of a civil court under the CPC while trying a suit (summoning and examining witnesses, discovery and production of documents, receiving evidence on affidavits, requisitioning public records, issuing commissions) (s.13); the Commission may use the services of any officer or investigating agency of the Central or State Government, with concurrence (s.14); it may regulate its own procedure. It deals with a complaint free of charge and in any language.

**Limits.**

- **Limitation (s.36(2)):** the Commission may not inquire into a matter **after one year** from the date on which the alleged act was committed.

- **Pending matters (s.36(1)):** it shall not inquire into a matter pending before a State Commission or any other Commission duly constituted under any law.

- **Armed forces (s.19):** for complaints of violation by members of the armed forces, the Commission may only seek a report from the Central Government and make recommendations; it has no power to investigate independently.

- **Recommendatory nature:** the Commission’s findings are **not binding**. Under s.18, it may recommend prosecution or other action, approach the Supreme Court or High Court for directions or writs, recommend **interim relief** to victims, and publish its report. The concerned Government must forward its comments and the action taken on the recommendation within **one month** (or such longer time as the Commission allows). The Commission submits an **annual report** to the Central Government, which is laid before Parliament with a memorandum of action taken (s.20).

- NHRC Procedure Regulations, 1994 list grounds on which complaints are ordinarily not entertained, such as anonymous or vague complaints, matters that are sub judice, service matters, and matters older than one year.

**Notable NHRC and court interactions:** the Supreme Court referred the case of mass illegal cremations in Punjab to the NHRC (*Paramjit Kaur v. State of Punjab*, 1999); the NHRC itself moved the Supreme Court to protect Chakma refugees in Arunachal Pradesh (*NHRC v. State of Arunachal Pradesh*, 1996); the NHRC issued guidelines on reporting of encounter deaths (2003, revised in 2010).

### 10.5 National Commission for Women (NCW)

- **Statute:** National Commission for Women Act 1990; constituted on **31 January 1992**. First Chairperson: Jayanti Patnaik.

- **Composition (s.3):** a Chairperson committed to the cause of women; **five Members** nominated by the Central Government from persons of ability, integrity and standing with experience in law or legislation, trade unionism, management of an industry employing women, women’s voluntary organisations, administration, economic development, health, education or social welfare; **at least one Member from SC and one from ST**; a **Member-Secretary** (an expert in management, organisational structure or sociological movement, or an officer of the civil service). The Chairperson and Members hold office for a term not exceeding **three years** (s.4).

- **Functions (s.10):** investigate and examine the safeguards provided for women under the Constitution and other laws; present annual and special reports to the Central Government; review existing laws and suggest amendments; take up cases of violation of the provisions of the Constitution and other laws relating to women with the appropriate authorities; **look into complaints and take suo motu notice** of the deprivation of women’s rights or non-implementation of protective laws; participate and advise on the socio-economic development planning process; inspect jails, remand homes and other places of custody; fund litigation involving issues affecting a large body of women; and undertake promotional and educational research.

- **Powers (s.8):** civil court powers while investigating matters (summoning, discovery, evidence on affidavits, public records, commissions).

- **Nature:** a recommendatory body; it cannot adjudicate or impose penalties. Chairperson since October 2024: Vijaya Rahatkar (check).

### 10.6 National Commission for Minorities (NCM)

- **Statute:** National Commission for Minorities Act 1992. The Minorities Commission was first set up by a Government resolution on 12 January 1978; it became statutory in 1992 and the NCM began functioning on **17 May 1993**. It is under the Ministry of Minority Affairs.

- **Notified minorities:** Muslims, Christians, Sikhs, Buddhists and Zoroastrians (Parsis) (1993); **Jains** (2014).

- **Composition (s.3):** a **Chairperson, a Vice-Chairperson and five Members**, nominated by the Central Government from persons of eminence, ability and integrity; five of the seven (including the Chairperson) must be from the minority communities. **Term:** three years (s.4).

- **Functions (s.9):** evaluate the progress of the development of minorities under the Union and the States; monitor the working of the constitutional and legal safeguards; make recommendations for their effective implementation; look into specific complaints regarding deprivation of rights and safeguards and take them up with the authorities; cause studies and research on discrimination; suggest measures; make periodical and special reports to the Central Government.

### 10.7 National Commission for Scheduled Castes (NCSC) and National Commission for Scheduled Tribes (NCST)

- **History:** The original Art. 338 provided a **Special Officer** (Commissioner for SC and ST). The **65th Amendment 1990** replaced it with a multi-member **National Commission for SC and ST** (from 1992). The **89th Amendment 2003** split it into the **NCSC (Art. 338)** and the **NCST (Art. 338A)**, effective from 19 February 2004. The Ministry of Tribal Affairs was created in 1999.

- **Composition:** each Commission has a **Chairperson, Vice-Chairperson and three other Members**, appointed by the **President by warrant under his hand and seal**; conditions of service and tenure are determined by rules made by the President (three-year terms in practice).

- **Functions (Art. 338(5) and Art. 338A(5)):** investigate and monitor all matters relating to the safeguards provided for SC (or ST) under the Constitution or any other law or Government order and evaluate their working; inquire into specific complaints about deprivation of rights; participate and advise on the planning process of socio-economic development of SC (or ST) and evaluate progress; present **annual reports** to the President (laid before Parliament with a memorandum of action taken, Art. 338(6)); recommend measures for effective implementation of safeguards and for welfare and development; and discharge other functions as the President may specify. NCST also has functions regarding forest rights, minor forest produce, and displacement of tribal communities.

- **Powers (Art. 338(8) and 338A(8)):** the Commission has all the powers of a **civil court** while investigating or inquiring into complaints (summoning, discovery, evidence on affidavits, requisitioning public records, issuing commissions).

- **Consultation (Art. 338(9)):** the Union and every State Government must **consult the Commission on all major policy matters** affecting SC (or ST).

### 10.8 National Commission for Backward Classes (NCBC)

- **History:** The First Backward Classes Commission (**Kaka Kalelkar**, 1953) and the Second (**B.P. Mandal**, 1979; report 1980; 3,743 castes and communities identified; recommended 27% reservation, implemented in 1990 by the V.P. Singh Government) were set under **Art. 340**. After *Indra Sawhney* (1992), the **National Commission for Backward Classes Act 1993** created a statutory NCBC, with the task of examining requests for inclusion in and complaints of over-inclusion or under-inclusion in the lists of backward classes.

- **Constitutional status:** the **102nd Constitution Amendment Act 2018** inserted **Art. 338B** (NCBC as a constitutional body, in force from 15 August 2018), **Art. 342A** (President specifies the socially and educationally backward classes for each State and Union Territory, Parliament may amend the list), and **Art. 366(26C)**; the 1993 Act was repealed. The **105th Amendment 2021** restored the power of States and Union Territories to prepare their own lists of SEBCs.

- **Composition:** Chairperson, Vice-Chairperson and three other Members, appointed by the **President** by warrant under hand and seal.

- **Functions (Art. 338B(5)):** investigate and monitor safeguards for socially and educationally backward classes; inquire into specific complaints; participate and advise on the planning process of their socio-economic development; present annual reports to the President; recommend measures; and discharge other functions. It also has the civil court powers (Art. 338B(8)). The Centre and States must consult the NCBC on major policy matters (this requirement was removed for State lists by the 105th Amendment).

- **Sub-categorisation:** the **Rohini Commission** (appointed under Art. 340 in 2017, chaired by Justice G. Rohini, a former Chief Justice of the Delhi High Court) examined sub-categorisation of OBCs and submitted its report in July 2023.

### 10.10 Criticisms and reform ideas

- Recommendatory, non-binding orders; dependence on government for investigation; one-year limitation; restricted jurisdiction over the armed forces; limited funds and staff; delay and low representation of civil society; and the appointment committee being dominated by the ruling side. Reform proposals include binding force for recommendations, an independent investigation staff, a longer limitation period, and a more balanced selection process (the Paris Principles on pluralism and independence).
