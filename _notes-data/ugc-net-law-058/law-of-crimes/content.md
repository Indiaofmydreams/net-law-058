# Law of Crimes: Detailed Notes for UGC NET Law (058)

## Chapter 0: Scope, Law Used and Exam Pattern

The criminal substantive law of India is now the **Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita, 2023 (BNS)**, in force from **1 July 2024**; it replaced the Indian Penal Code, 1860 (IPC). Procedure moved to the **Bharatiya Nagarik Suraksha Sanhita, 2023 (BNSS)** (replacing CrPC 1973) and evidence to the **Bharatiya Sakshya Adhiniyam, 2023 (BSA)** (replacing the Evidence Act 1872). Every topic below gives the BNS section first and the old IPC section in brackets, because UGC NET options still mix both numbering systems and almost all case law was decided under the IPC.

### 0.1 BNS at a glance: high-yield numbers

- 358 sections in 20 Chapters; the IPC had 511 sections in 23 Chapters.

- Chapter I = preliminary and definitions (s.1-3; s.2 is the definitions section); Chapter II = punishments (s.4-13); Chapter III = general exceptions (s.14-44); Chapter IV = abetment, criminal conspiracy and attempt (s.45-62); Chapter V = offences against women and children (s.63-99); Chapter VI = offences affecting the human body (s.100-146); Chapter VII = offences against the State (s.147-158); Chapter VIII = offences relating to the Army, Navy and Air Force (s.159-168); Chapter IX = offences relating to elections (s.169-177); Chapter X = coin, bank notes, currency notes and Government stamps (s.178-188); Chapter XI = offences against public tranquillity (s.189-197); Chapter XVII = offences against property (s.303-334).

- New offences added: **organised crime (s.111)**, **petty organised crime (s.112)**, **terrorist act (s.113)**, **mob lynching (s.103(2))**, **snatching (s.304)**, **hit and run (s.106(2))**, **sexual intercourse by deceitful means or false promise of marriage (s.69)**, and **acts endangering sovereignty, unity and integrity of India (s.152)**.

- Removed or dropped: **sedition (IPC 124A)** (replaced in substance by s.152), **adultery (IPC 497)** (already struck down in *Joseph Shine v. Union of India*, 2018), **unnatural offences (IPC 377)** (consensual part read down in *Navtej Singh Johar*, 2018; BNS has no equivalent), and **attempt to commit suicide (IPC 309)** (replaced by a narrow s.226, attempt to commit suicide to compel or restrain a public servant, punished with community service).

- **Community service** is a new punishment (s.4(f)), provided for small offences such as petty theft, defamation, attempted suicide to coerce a public servant and public intoxication.

- Rape (s.63) is still a male-offender, female-victim offence; the age of consent stays at 18 years.

## Chapter 1: General Principles of Criminal Liability

Criminal liability in India rests on one idea: a person is punished only when a **prohibited act or omission (actus reus)** is joined with a **blameworthy mind (mens rea)**, unless the statute says otherwise. The maxim is *actus non facit reum nisi mens sit rea* (an act does not make a person guilty unless the mind is also guilty). The BNS does not define "crime", but its definitions in s.2 (IPC ss.6-52A) build mens rea into most offences through words such as *intentionally, knowingly, voluntarily, dishonestly, fraudulently, with reason to believe*.

### 1.1 Elements of a crime (four-step structure)

1.  **Human being** under a legal duty to act or abstain (a juristic person such as a company is also a "person", BNS s.2(26); IPC s.11).

2.  **Mens rea**: the guilty mind.

3.  **Actus reus**: the prohibited conduct, its circumstances and its result.

4.  **Injury** to another or to society, recognised by law.

### 1.2 Actus reus

- **Meaning**: the external, physical part of a crime: the act or illegal omission, the surrounding circumstances and (for result crimes) the consequence. The act must be **voluntary** (a reflex, sleepwalking or a seizure is not an act in law).

- **Types**: conduct crimes (offence complete on doing the act, e.g., perjury), result crimes (need a consequence, e.g., murder needs death), and state-of-affairs crimes (possession offences, e.g., possession of counterfeit notes or drugs).

- **Omission**: liable only where a **legal duty** exists (statute, contract, assumed responsibility, creating a dangerous situation, parent-child). BNS definitions say "act" includes a series of acts and "illegal" covers acts or omissions that are offences or prohibited by law (IPC ss.32-33, 43). Examples: a mother starving her child to death; a railway gateman not closing the gate.

- **Causation**: the act must be the **proximate** cause. The "but for" test is the starting point; the chain breaks only by a *novus actus interveniens* (new, independent, unforeseeable intervening act). Cases: *R v. Jordan* (grossly abnormal medical treatment broke the chain), *R v. Smith* (negligent treatment did not break it because the original wound was still operating), *R v. White* (no causation where the poison was not the cause of death, so only attempt). The "thin skull" rule applies: take the victim as found.

- **Concurrence**: actus reus and mens rea must coincide in time, but the courts treat a **continuing series of acts** as one transaction (*Thabo Meli v. R*; *Fagan v. Metropolitan Police Commissioner* for continuing act).

### 1.3 Mens rea

- **Meaning**: the mental element required by the definition of the offence: intention, knowledge, recklessness (rarely named) and in limited cases negligence.

- **Intention**: the aim or purpose to bring about a result; a person is taken to intend the natural and probable consequences of the act. **Motive** is different (the reason behind the intention) and is generally irrelevant to guilt but relevant to proof and sentence.

- **Knowledge**: awareness that a result is certain or likely.

- **Recklessness**: conscious taking of an unjustified risk; in India shades into "knowledge that the act is likely to cause" death or hurt (see culpable homicide).

- **Negligence**: failure to take the care a reasonable person would take. Under s.106(1) BNS (IPC 304A) the standard is **gross or culpable negligence**, not civil negligence (*Jacob Mathew v. State of Punjab*, 2005, which also set protections for doctors; negligence must be "gross").

- **Key mental words in the BNS**: *voluntarily* (intending the result or knowing it likely), *dishonestly* (intention to cause wrongful gain or wrongful loss), *fraudulently* (intent to defraud, i.e., deceit plus injury or risk), *reason to believe* (sufficient cause to believe, not mere suspicion), *good faith* (due care and attention, IPC s.52, BNS s.2).

- **Transferred malice (transmigration of motive)**: if the accused intends to harm A but harms B, the intent is transferred; BNS s.102 (IPC s.301) applies to culpable homicide. Case: *R v. Latimer*.

- **Malice and motive** appear in tort and defamation but are not general criminal law elements.

### 1.4 Exceptions to the mens rea requirement (strict and absolute liability)

- **Strict liability** offences require no proof of mens rea for at least one element. They are typically **public welfare offences** (food adulteration, drugs, traffic, weights and measures, essential commodities).

- **Tests** (Gammon (Hong Kong) Ltd v. AG of Hong Kong, 1985): presumption of mens rea; displaced only if the statute clearly or by necessary implication says so; issue must be of social concern; strict liability must promote the statute's objects (by encouraging greater vigilance).

- **Indian cases**: *State of Maharashtra v. M.H. George* (1965) (FERA; mens rea not needed for importing gold into India under the notification, so George was liable); *Nathulal v. State of M.P.* (1966) (Essential Commodities Act; mens rea required, since the offence involved possession of stock without a licence but a dealer who had not delivered under the statutory time got a benefit of doubt); *Ravula Hariprasada Rao v. State* (1951) (Essential Supplies Act; mens rea is not essential in a regulatory statute); *Hariprasada Rao* and *George* are the two most quoted.

- **English parallels**: *R v. Prince* (1875) (taking a girl under 16 from her father's possession; honest belief in her age was no defence); *Sweet v. Parsley* (1970) (presumption of mens rea revived: unless Parliament clearly excludes it, mens rea is required).

- **Vicarious liability** in crime exists only by statute (employer for acts of a servant under Excise, Food Safety and Standards, Motor Vehicles laws) or by delegation.

### 1.5 Stages and persons

- **Age of criminal responsibility** (BNS ss.20-21; IPC ss.82-83): nothing is an offence if done by a child **under 7**; a child **above 7 and under 12** is protected if of immature understanding (not capable of judging the nature and consequences). Juveniles aged 16-18 charged with heinous offences can be tried as adults after preliminary assessment under the **Juvenile Justice (Care and Protection of Children) Act, 2015**.

- **Corporate criminal liability**: BNS s.2(26) includes a company in "person". Cases: *Standard Chartered Bank v. Directorate of Enforcement* (2005) (a company can be prosecuted even for offences with mandatory imprisonment; the court can impose a fine); *Iridium India Telecom Ltd v. Motorola Inc.* (2011) (a company can have mens rea through its directing mind; can be liable for cheating); *Sunil Bharti Mittal v. CBI* (2015) (the directors' acts can be attributed to the company, but the company's liability does not automatically make directors liable: personal role needed); *Aneeta Hada v. Godfather Travels* (2012) (Negotiable Instruments Act s.141: directors can be prosecuted only if the company is also arraigned).

### 1.6 Individual, group and constructive liability

**Individual (direct) liability**: a person who personally does the act with the required mind is the principal offender. Several offenders can be "joint" principals when each does part of the act.

**Group liability** arises when the offence is done by many; Indian law has two main provisions plus conspiracy:

#### A. Common intention: BNS s.3(5) (IPC s.34)

- **Text in essence**: when a criminal act is done by several persons in furtherance of the **common intention of all**, each is liable as if he alone did it.

- **Nature**: s.3(5) does **not** create a substantive offence; it lays down a **rule of evidence / principle of joint liability**. It is read with the main offence (e.g., murder by s.103 read with s.3(5)).

- **Ingredients**: (1) a criminal act done by several persons; (2) a **common intention** shared by all; (3) **participation** of all in doing the act. Common intention needs a **prior meeting of minds** (can be formed on the spot, even just before the act: *Pandurang v. State of Hyderabad*, 1955). It cannot be proved by mere presence or association.

- **Cases**: *Barendra Kumar Ghosh v. King Emperor* (1925; accused standing guard while another shot the postmaster; Privy Council: "they also serve who only stand and wait"; presence and a part in the plan suffice); *Mahbub Shah v. King Emperor* (1945; common intention differs from **similar intention**; requires a pre-arranged plan; Privy Council); *Pandurang v. State of Hyderabad* (1955); *Krishna Govind Patil v. State of Maharashtra* (1963); *Ramaswami Ayyangar v. State of Tamil Nadu* (1976); *Virendra Singh v. State of M.P.* (2010).

- **Number**: two or more persons.

- **Overt act**: ordinarily needed (some act in furtherance, even standing guard); in certain cases omission suffices, but mere presence is not enough.

#### B. Common object: BNS s.190 (IPC s.149)

- **Text in essence**: if an offence is committed by any member of an **unlawful assembly** in prosecution of the **common object** of that assembly, or such as the members knew to be likely to be committed in prosecution of that object, every person who is a member of that assembly at the time is guilty.

- **Nature**: a **specific offence**-creating rule of **constructive (vicarious) liability** (the liability exists by membership of the assembly). Unlawful assembly itself is BNS s.189 (IPC s.141): five or more persons with a common object falling within the five listed heads (overawe the Government by criminal force; resist execution of law; commit mischief or criminal trespass; obtain property by criminal force; compel a person to do what he is not legally bound to do).

- **Cases**: *Masalti v. State of U.P.* (1965; mere presence in an unlawful assembly is enough for liability if the member shares the common object; but courts need caution when many accused are named so that innocent bystanders are not convicted); *Lalji v. State of U.P.* (1989) (common object can arise on the spot); *Charan Singh v. State of U.P.* (2004); *Yeshwant v. State of Maharashtra* (1972) (members can be liable under s.149 even if the offence is outside the original object but within "likely to be committed").

- **Number**: **five or more**. If a court acquits all but two, s.149 cannot be applied unless it is clear that others unidentified were also there; if only two or three are convicted and the rest unnamed, the court looks at whether the evidence shows five.

#### C. Common intention vs common object (very frequent exam point)

| Point                              | Common intention (s.3(5); IPC 34)                | Common object (s.190; IPC 149)                                                      |
|------------------------------------|--------------------------------------------------|-------------------------------------------------------------------------------------|
| Nature                             | Rule of evidence; no separate offence            | Creates a specific liability tied to unlawful assembly                              |
| Minimum persons                    | Two or more                                      | Five or more                                                                        |
| Mental element                     | Prior meeting of minds; **intention** of all     | A **common object** shared with the assembly; knowledge of what is likely           |
| Participation                      | Active participation needed                      | Membership suffices; no overt act needed                                            |
| Liability                          | For the act done in furtherance of the intention | For acts in prosecution of the object **or** likely to be committed                 |
| Which can exist without the other? | Yes; s.34 may be applied where s.149 fails       | Yes; one may apply alternatively (*Willie Slaney*, 1955 on alternative application) |

#### D. Other forms of group or constructive liability

- **Criminal conspiracy**: BNS s.61 (IPC s.120A-B); covered in topic 2.

- **Joint liability for certain acts**: e.g., dacoity with murder (BNS s.310(3); IPC s.396) makes every member of the gang liable; house-breaking at night by many persons where death or grievous hurt is caused (BNS s.331; IPC s.460).

- **Abetment**: liability for aiding, instigating or conspiring (topic 2).

- **Statutory vicarious liability**: employer, partner, director (NI Act s.141; Companies Act; Food Safety and Standards Act; Environment Protection Act).

- **Organised crime** (BNS s.111): a new group offence for continuing unlawful activity by a syndicate (see topic 4/5).

- **Mob lynching** (BNS s.103(2)): five or more persons acting together and committing murder on grounds of race, caste, community, sex, place of birth, language, personal belief or similar grounds; each member liable with death or life imprisonment and fine.

### Memory Capsule: Chapter 1

- **"S.34 is evidence, s.149 is offence-by-membership."**

- **2 vs 5**: intention (2+), object (5+).

- *Barendra Kumar Ghosh* = "they also serve who only stand and wait"; *Mahbub Shah* = common intention vs similar intention; *Masalti* = s.149 and presence.

- **Strict liability** = *M.H. George*, *Hariprasada Rao*, *Sweet v. Parsley*, *Gammon*.

- **Corporate liability** = *Standard Chartered* (fine for mandatory imprisonment offences), *Iridium*, *Sunil Bharti Mittal*.

- **Negligence under s.106(1) BNS** = *Jacob Mathew*: gross negligence.

- **Age**: below 7 absolute immunity; 7-12 conditional.

- Statements often tested: common intention can be formed **on the spot**; s.34 can apply **without** an overt act (rare); s.149 applies **only** to unlawful assembly members; motive is **not** an element of crime; mens rea is not required in **strict liability** offences.

## Chapter 2: Stages of Crime and Inchoate Crimes: Abetment, Conspiracy, Attempt

**Inchoate (incomplete) crimes** are offences that are punishable even though the final harm has not occurred: abetment, criminal conspiracy and attempt. The BNS places them together in **Chapter IV (ss.45-62)**: abetment ss.45-60, criminal conspiracy s.61, attempt s.62. This is a compact, high-scoring topic.

### 2.1 Four stages of a crime

1.  **Intention**: the mental decision to commit the crime. **Not punishable** (no actus reus; thought alone is never an offence).

2.  **Preparation**: arranging the means (buying a weapon, collecting poison). **Generally not punishable**, because the act is equivocal and the person may desist. Exceptions where preparation itself is punishable: preparing to wage war against the Government (BNS s.149; IPC s.122), preparation to commit dacoity (BNS s.310(4)-(5); IPC ss.399-402), making or possessing instruments for counterfeiting coin, currency notes or stamps, and possessing explosives or arms under special laws.

3.  **Attempt**: a direct movement towards commission after preparation is complete. **Punishable** (BNS s.62 and offence-specific sections).

4.  **Commission (accomplishment)**: the offence is completed; punishment is for the full offence. If the final result is not achieved only because of external factors, the conduct is an attempt.

**Remember**: the line between preparation and attempt is a question of fact; the Indian courts focus on whether the accused has done an act that is a *proximate step* to the offence.

### 2.2 Abetment (BNS ss.45-60; IPC ss.107-120)

#### A. Definition (s.45; IPC s.107)

A person abets the doing of a thing who:

1.  **Instigates** any person to do that thing;

2.  **Engages with one or more persons in a conspiracy** for the doing of it, if an act or illegal omission takes place in pursuance of that conspiracy and in order to the doing of that thing; or

3.  **Intentionally aids**, by any act or illegal omission, the doing of that thing.

**Key explanations**: (a) wilful misrepresentation or wilful concealment of a material fact which one is bound to disclose, by which a person voluntarily causes or procures a thing to be done, is instigation; (b) a person who, either before or at the time of the offence, does anything to facilitate its commission aids the doing of it.

- **Instigation** means to goad, urge, provoke, incite or encourage. It needs **active suggestion or stimulation**, usually positive acts; mere acquiescence or passive consent is not instigation. Cases: *Amritlal Hazra v. Emperor* (1915); *Ramesh Kumar v. State of Chhattisgarh* (2001) (an angry outburst such as "go and die" may be a mere remark without mens rea); *Sanju @ Sanjay Singh Sengar v. State of M.P.* (2002) (a casual "go and die" remark in a quarrel did not amount to instigation of suicide); *Chitresh Kumar Chopra v. State (Govt. of NCT Delhi)* (2009) (instigation requires a mens rea to bring about the act); *M. Mohan v. State* (2011); *Amalendu Pal v. State of W.B.* (2010); *Ude Singh v. State of Haryana* (2019).

- **Conspiracy as a mode of abetment** needs an **act or illegal omission in pursuance** of the agreement (unlike s.61 criminal conspiracy, which needs agreement only for an offence).

- **Aid** must be **intentional**, and it must be given before or during the offence (help after the offence is not abetment, it may be an accessory-after-the-fact offence such as harbouring).

- **The offence abetted need not be committed** (s.46, Explanation 2; IPC s.108, Explanation 2). The person abetted need not be capable of committing the offence or have the same guilty mind (Explanation 4 and 5), and abetment of abetment is also abetment (Explanation 3).

- **Abettor in India for offence outside India** (BNS s.47; IPC s.108A) and **abettor outside India for offence in India** (BNS s.48, new) are also covered.

- **Abetment of suicide**: BNS s.108 (IPC s.306): imprisonment up to **10 years** and fine; BNS s.107 (IPC s.305) covers abetment of suicide of a **child or a person of unsound mind** (the heavier punishment includes death in IPC 305; check the BNS text for the exact wording). **Attempt to commit suicide** (IPC s.309) is no longer an offence in general; BNS s.226 punishes only an attempt to commit suicide to compel or restrain exercise of lawful power by a public servant (community service or simple imprisonment up to one year).

- **Related cases**: *Gian Kaur v. State of Punjab* (1996) (right to life does not include right to die; abetment of suicide remains punishable; s.309 upheld at that time, overruling *P. - **Dowry death** (BNS s.80; IPC s.304B) involves a presumption in the Evidence law (BSA s.118; Evidence Act s.113B); **abetment of suicide of a married woman** within seven years raises a presumption (BSA s.117; Evidence Act s.113A).

### 2.3 Criminal conspiracy (BNS s.61; IPC ss.120A-120B)

- **Definition (s.61(1); IPC s.120A)**: when two or more persons **agree** to do, or cause to be done, (1) an **illegal act**, or (2) an act which is **not illegal by illegal means**, the agreement is called a criminal conspiracy. **Proviso**: no agreement except to commit an offence amounts to criminal conspiracy unless some act besides the agreement is done by one or more parties in pursuance of it. In other words, an **agreement to commit an offence** needs **no overt act**; an agreement to do a non-offence illegal act needs an overt act.

- **Punishment (s.61(2); IPC s.120B)**: (a) conspiracy to commit an offence punishable with **death, life imprisonment or rigorous imprisonment for two years or more** is punished as if the conspirator had **abetted** the offence (and no express provision exists); (b) any **other** criminal conspiracy: imprisonment up to **six months**, or fine, or both.

- **Nature**: a separate, substantive and **continuing** offence; liability does not depend on achieving the object. The essence is **agreement** and the agreement is rarely proved by direct evidence; it is inferred from circumstances.

- **Number of parties**: at least two; if all except one are acquitted, the remaining one cannot be convicted of conspiracy (*Topan Das v. State of Bombay*, 1955), unless others are unknown or not tried.

- **Evidence**: the acts and statements of a conspirator in reference to the common design are admissible against all conspirators (BSA s.8; Evidence Act s.10).

- **Landmark cases**: *Kehar Singh v. State (Delhi Administration)* (1988) (Indira Gandhi assassination; conspiracy is hatched in secrecy and proved by circumstantial evidence); *State (NCT of Delhi) v. Navjot Sandhu* (2005) (Parliament attack; Afzal Guru); *State v. Nalini* (1999) (Rajiv Gandhi assassination; conspiracy as a continuing offence; chain and wheel conspiracy); *Ajay Agarwal v. Union of India* (1993) (a conspirator need not know all details; conspiracy is continuing); *Mohd. Khalid v. State of W.B.* (2002); *Yakub Abdul Razak Memon v. State of Maharashtra* (2013) (1993 Bombay blasts); *Firozuddin Basheeruddin v. State of Kerala* (2001) (each conspirator need not know the other; the common design links them).

- **Types**: *wheel conspiracy* (one hub linked to many separate spokes) and *chain conspiracy* (each link is connected to the next). **Rim of the wheel** requires each spoke to share the common design.

### 2.4 Attempt (BNS s.62; IPC s.511; and specific attempts)

- **Definition**: the BNS and IPC do not define attempt. It is an act done with intent to commit an offence, going beyond mere preparation, but falling short of completion.

- **General provision** (s.62; IPC s.511): whoever attempts to commit an offence punishable with **imprisonment for life or imprisonment**, or to cause such an offence to be committed, and in such attempt does any act towards the commission, shall (if no express provision) be punished with up to **one-half of the longest term** of imprisonment provided for the offence, or with the prescribed fine, or both (death-punishable offences are within the ambit of life-imprisonment terms). **Illustrations**: (a) pulling out an empty box in an attempt to steal jewels (the conduct is attempted theft, even though nothing was inside); (b) breaking open a box to steal jewels.

- **Specific attempts** with their own punishment: attempt to murder (BNS s.109; IPC s.307), attempt to commit culpable homicide (BNS s.110; IPC s.308), attempt to commit suicide to compel a public servant (BNS s.226), attempt to commit robbery when armed with a deadly weapon (IPC s.398), attempt to commit rape (punished through the sexual-offence sections, e.g., assault to outrage modesty s.74).

- **Tests of attempt**:

  - **Last act test** (*R v. Eagleton*, 1855): the accused must have done the last act within his power.

  - **Proximity test**: the act must be so near to the completed offence as to be *proximate*; adopted by Indian courts. *Abhayanand Mishra v. State of Bihar* (1961): the applicant, who submitted a false application to the university for admission to an examination claiming a degree, was guilty of attempt to cheat once the application was sent; preparation ended and attempt began.

  - **Equivocality test**: the act is clearly unambiguous as to its purpose.

  - **Unequivocal act / substantial step** (modern approach).

- **Preparation versus attempt in cases**: *Malkiat Singh v. State of Punjab* (1970) (carrying paddy to a place near Delhi from Punjab, with Delhi limits not yet crossed, was only preparation); *State of Maharashtra v. Mohd. Yakub* (1980) (silver being taken in a truck to the sea-shore for export: the proximate stage; attempt to export); *Aman Kumar v. State of Haryana* (2004) (attempt in context of a direct movement toward commission); *Sudhir Kumar Mukherjee v. State of W.B.* (1973) (attempt to murder); *Om Prakash v. State of Punjab* (1961) (attempt to murder: the act must be done with intention or knowledge so as to cause death).

- **Impossible attempt**: **factual impossibility** is not a defence under the Indian illustrations (the empty box example); the English House of Lords reached the same conclusion in *R v. Shivpuri* (1986), overruling *Anderton v. Ryan*. **Legal impossibility** (the act is not an offence at all) is not an attempt.

- **Attempt to murder** (BNS s.109(1)): imprisonment up to **10 years** and fine; if **hurt** is caused, up to **life imprisonment** (or up to ten years). A **life convict** who attempts murder and causes hurt can be punished with **death or imprisonment for life** (s.109(2) in BNS, IPC s.307 second part). The accused needs the *intention or knowledge* that would make the act murder if death resulted; the actual injury need not be fatal and **no hurt** is needed.

### Memory Capsule: Chapter 2

- **Abetment = I-C-A**: Instigation, Conspiracy (with act), Aid.

- **Abetment is complete even if the offence is not committed**; the abetted person need not be guilty.

- **Conspiracy**: two or more; an agreement; no overt act needed for offences; punishment same as abetment for serious offences; six months for others.

- **Attempt**: proximity test; empty-pocket/empty-box illustration; half of the longest term.

- **Cases to connect**: *Kehar Singh* (circumstantial proof), *Nalini* (chain and continuing), *Abhayanand Mishra* (false application = attempt), *Malkiat Singh* (preparation only), *Gian Kaur* (no right to die), *Topan Das* (one cannot conspire alone).

## Chapter 3: General Exceptions (BNS ss.14-44; IPC ss.76-106)

General exceptions are defences that apply to **every offence** in the Sanhita (and, by BNS s.2/Chapter I rules, to other penal laws unless excluded). The accused who pleads an exception bears the **burden of proof** (BSA s.108-type provision; Evidence Act s.105), but only to the **standard of preponderance of probabilities**; the prosecution must still prove the offence beyond reasonable doubt, and a reasonable doubt raised by the defence also benefits the accused.

**Classification**: *Justifiable* acts (the act is right in law): judicial acts, acts in execution of law, private defence, necessity (partly). *Excusable* acts (the act is wrong but the actor is not blamed): mistake of fact, accident, infancy, insanity, intoxication, consent, duress, trifles.

### 3.2 Mistake of fact (ss.14, 17; IPC 76, 79)

- **Maxim**: *ignorantia facti excusat, ignorantia juris non excusat*. A mistake **of fact** made in **good faith** gives protection; a mistake **of law** does not.

- **Conditions**: the mistake must be of **fact** (not law), must be **bona fide**, and the accused must have believed himself *bound by law* (s.14) or *justified by law* (s.17) to do the act.

- **Cases**: *Chirangi v. State of M.P.* (1952) (the accused killed a man in the dark believing he was a tiger attacking his child; acquitted); *State of Orissa v. Ram Bahadur Thapa* (1960) (the accused killed a woman believing her to be a witch; a mistaken belief that killing a witch is lawful is a mistake of law, so no protection); *Waryam Singh v. Emperor* (1926).

- **Related English cases**: *R v. Tolson* (1889) (bigamy; honest and reasonable belief in husband's death was a defence).

### 3.3 Judicial acts (ss.15-16; IPC 77-78)

- A **judge** is protected for acts done in the exercise of power given by law (or reasonably believed to be given), and persons acting under a **court order** are protected for acts done under it even if the court lacked jurisdiction, provided they believed in good faith it had.

- Linked laws: **Judicial Officers Protection Act, 1850**; BNSS provisions on protection of judges and public servants.

### 3.4 Accident (s.18; IPC 80)

- An act is excused when it is the result of **accident or misfortune** and done **without criminal intention or knowledge**, in the doing of a **lawful act**, in a **lawful manner**, by **lawful means**, and with **proper care and caution**. All five are cumulative.

- **Illustration**: A is at work with a hatchet; the head flies off and kills a man nearby. If there was no want of proper caution, A has committed no offence.

- **Examination point**: *proper care and caution* is the key phrase; an accident arising from a quarrel or from an unlawful act (e.g., a brawl where the blow was deliberate) is not covered.

### 3.5 Necessity (s.19; IPC 81)

- An act is not an offence merely because it is done with the knowledge that it is likely to cause harm, if it is done **without criminal intention to cause harm**, **in good faith**, to **prevent other harm** to a person or property.

- The question is whether the harm to be prevented was of such a nature and so imminent as to justify the risk (fact finding).

- **Illustration**: a ship captain who cannot save his vessel from collision with a boat full of passengers changes course and sinks a boat with two persons; no offence.

- **English contrast**: *R v. Dudley and Stephens* (1884): necessity is no defence to murder (killing a cabin boy to survive at sea).

### 3.6 Infancy (ss.20-21; IPC 82-83)

- **Under 7**: absolute immunity (*doli incapax*, conclusive presumption). **7 to 12**: immunity only if of **immature understanding** (capacity to judge nature and consequences), decided case by case. **12 and above**: ordinarily full responsibility under the Sanhita, but **children below 18** are dealt with by the **Juvenile Justice (Care and Protection of Children) Act, 2015**; a child **16 to 18** who commits a **heinous offence** (minimum punishment of seven years or more) may be tried as an adult after a preliminary assessment by the Juvenile Justice Board (*Shilpa Mittal v. State of NCT of Delhi*, 2020 treated offences with minimum less than 7 years but maximum above 7 as "serious", not "heinous").

- **Nirbhaya case effect** (2012): the 2015 Act was passed after the public debate over a juvenile offender.

### 3.7 Insanity (s.22; IPC 84)

- **Text in essence**: nothing is an offence which is done by a person who, at the time of doing it, by reason of **unsoundness of mind**, is **incapable of knowing the nature of the act**, or that he is doing what is **either wrong or contrary to law**.

- **Origin**: **M'Naghten rules** (1843), after *Daniel M'Naghten* killed Edward Drummond (he suffered delusions). Indian law follows the M'Naghten rule, with "contrary to law" added.

- **Legal vs medical insanity**: law requires **cognitive incapacity** at the **time of the act**; not every mental illness qualifies. **Irresistible impulse** and **volitional incapacity** are not recognised.

- **Burden**: on the accused; the standard is **preponderance of probabilities**; the court considers conduct before, at and after the offence (motive, preparation, concealment, escape). Concealing the weapon or fleeing shows knowledge of wrongfulness.

- **Cases**: *Dahyabhai Chhaganbhai Thakker v. State of Gujarat* (1964) (the burden and that unsoundness must exist at the time of the act); *Ratan Lal v. State of M.P.* (1971); *Sheralli Wali Mohammad v. State of Maharashtra* (1973); *Hari Singh Gond v. State of M.P.* (2008) (legal insanity versus medical insanity); *Surendra Mishra v. State of Jharkhand* (2011); *Bapu @ Gajraj Singh v. State of Rajasthan* (2007); *Elavarasan v. State* (2011).

- **Procedure**: BNSS provisions (the successor to CrPC ss.328-339) govern trial of persons of unsound mind; a person found insane is detained in a mental health facility or released to a relative on security.

### 3.8 Intoxication (ss.23-24; IPC 85-86)

- **Involuntary intoxication** (the drink or drug was administered **without his knowledge or against his will**) is a defence if, by reason of it, the accused was **incapable of knowing the nature of the act** or that it was wrong or contrary to law.

- **Voluntary intoxication** is **no defence**; but for offences needing a **specific intent or knowledge** (e.g., murder under s.101), the accused is treated as having the **same knowledge as a sober person**, unless the intoxicant was administered without his knowledge or against his will. However, intent can be negated by evidence that **intoxication was so great that he lacked the intent** (*Basdev v. State of PEPSU*, 1956: the court must consider whether the accused had the necessary intent; drunkenness is only a factor).

- **English cases**: *DPP v. Beard* (1920), *DPP v. Majewski* (1977) (voluntary intoxication is no defence to crimes of basic intent).

### 3.9 Consent and related protections (ss.25-31; IPC 87-93)

- **s.25 (IPC 87)**: a person **above 18** can consent to an act **not intended** or **known to be likely** to cause death or **grievous hurt**; e.g., fencing, boxing.

- **s.26 (IPC 88)**: act **not intended to cause death**, done in **good faith for the person's benefit** with consent, even if death or grievous hurt may result (e.g., a surgeon performing a risky operation with consent).

- **s.27 (IPC 89)**: act in good faith for the benefit of a child under **12** or a person of unsound mind with **guardian's consent** (but not intentional death, or an act the doer knows likely to cause death except to prevent death or grievous hurt, etc.).

- **s.28 (IPC 90)**: consent is **not valid** if given under **fear of injury or misconception of fact** (known to the doer), or by a person of **unsound mind or intoxicated**, or by a **child under 12** unless the context requires otherwise.

- **s.29 (IPC 91)**: consent is no excuse for acts that are offences **independently of harm** (e.g., abortion in violation of the law).

- **s.30 (IPC 92)**: act in good faith for a person's benefit **without consent** (emergency surgery on an unconscious patient where no guardian is available in time).

- **s.31 (IPC 93)**: a **communication in good faith** is not an offence merely because it causes harm (a doctor telling a patient he will die, which causes shock and death).

- **Cases**: *Poonai Fattemah v. Emperor* (1869) (snake-charmer; death by snake bite was not excusable because the act was likely to cause death); *Dr. Suresh Gupta v. Govt. of NCT of Delhi* (2004) and *Jacob Mathew v. State of Punjab* (2005) (medical negligence only criminal if gross).

### 3.10 Duress (s.32; IPC 94)

- Protects a person compelled to do an act by **threats that reasonably cause apprehension of instant death** if he refuses. **Excludes murder** and offences **against the State punishable with death**. The accused must **not have voluntarily placed himself** in a situation in which he became subject to the compulsion (e.g., joining a gang knowingly).

### 3.11 Trifles (s.33; IPC 95)

- *De minimis non curat lex*: an act causing or intended to cause **slight harm** that **no person of ordinary sense and temper would complain of** is not an offence. Case: *Veeda Menezes v. Yusuf Khan* (1966) (the Court treated the test as objective and fact-specific, looking at the nature of the injury, the intention and the circumstances). Not applicable to serious offences or where the harm is more than trivial.

### 3.12 Right of private defence (ss.34-44; IPC 96-106)

**Principle**: the State cannot protect each person at every moment, so the law gives each person a right to defend body and property. It is a **right of defence, not retribution**, and rests on **necessity**.

| Section (BNS / IPC) | What it says                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                            |
|---------------------|-----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------|

| 35 / 97             | Right to defend one's own and another's **body** against any offence affecting the human body; and **property** (movable or immovable) against theft, robbery, mischief, criminal trespass or attempts                                                                                                                                                                                  |
| 36 / 98             | Right exists **against persons of unsound mind, children, or acting under a mistake**, even if they are not guilty                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                      |
| 37 / 99             | **Limits**: no right against acts of a **public servant** acting in good faith under colour of office (unless reasonable apprehension of death or grievous hurt); none where there is **time to have recourse to public authorities**; and the right **never extends to inflicting more harm than necessary**                                                                           |

**Key doctrines and cases**

- **Reasonable apprehension**, not actual danger, is the test; the court weighs the situation as seen by a person of ordinary prudence in the moment (*Vidhya Singh v. State of M.P.*, 1971; the court does not weigh the right in "golden scales").

- **No right against more harm than needed**; if exceeded, the accused loses the full protection and is liable under **BNS s.101, Exception 2** (IPC s.300, Exception 2): culpable homicide **not amounting to murder** if death is caused by exceeding the right in good faith and without premeditation.

- **Aggressor** cannot claim it.

- **Right is not available where there is time to approach authorities** (*Jai Dev v. State of Punjab*, 1963).

- **Burden**: accused need not plead it expressly if circumstances show; the court may infer it (*Munshi Ram v. Delhi Administration*, 1968; *Rizan v. State of Chhattisgarh*, 2003).

- **Summary of principles**: *Darshan Singh v. State of Punjab* (2010) is the leading modern summary (the right is preventive, not punitive; every citizen can use it; defendant need not run away; injuries on accused and prosecution witness are evidence; a person faced with imminent peril need not measure the force with nicety).

- **Other cases**: *Puran Singh v. State of Punjab* (1975); *Mohinder Pal Jolly v. State of Punjab* (1979) (a factory owner who fired at a rioting crowd of workers; the Court examined how far the right of private defence extends against a mob), *Deo Narain v. State of U.P.* (1973) (the right continues as long as the danger).

### Memory Capsule: Chapter 3

- **Insanity**: M'Naghten (1843); legal not medical; time of act; burden on accused.

- **Infancy**: 7 and 12; JJ Act 2015 for 16-18 heinous offences.

- **Duress**: not for murder and offences against State punishable with death.

- **Intoxication**: voluntary = no defence; involuntary = defence.

- **Private defence**: s.34-44 BNS; death only for the listed grave offences; no aggressor; no retaliation.

- **Consent**: valid age 18 for s.25; child under 12 for s.27/28.

## Chapter 4: Offences Against the Human Body (BNS ss.100-146)

This is the **most question-dense** part of the unit. Chapter VI deals with offences affecting life (culpable homicide, murder, negligence, suicide), hurt, wrongful restraint and confinement, criminal force and assault, kidnapping and abduction, and trafficking. (Sexual offences, dowry and offences against children sit in Chapter V; see topic 7.)

### 4.1 Culpable homicide and murder

#### A. Culpable homicide (s.100; IPC s.299)

Whoever causes death by doing an act

1.  with the **intention of causing death**, or

2.  with the **intention of causing such bodily injury as is likely to cause death**, or

3.  with the **knowledge that he is likely by such act to cause death**,

commits culpable homicide. **Explanations**: causing bodily injury to a person suffering from a disease or infirmity that accelerates death counts; causing death by bodily injury where proper remedies and skilful treatment might have prevented death counts; causing the death of a child in the mother's womb is not homicide, but causing death of a living child, even if only part of it has been brought forth, may amount to culpable homicide.

#### B. Murder (s.101; IPC s.300)

Culpable homicide is **murder** if the act is done:

- **(a / Firstly)** with the **intention of causing death**;

- **(b / Secondly)** with the intention of causing **such bodily injury as the offender knows to be likely to cause the death** of the person to whom the harm is caused;

- **(c / Thirdly)** with the intention of causing **bodily injury to any person**, and the bodily injury intended is **sufficient in the ordinary course of nature to cause death**;

- **(d / Fourthly)** with the **knowledge that the act is so imminently dangerous that it must in all probability cause death**, or such bodily injury as is likely to cause death, and commits it **without any excuse for incurring the risk**.

#### C. Exceptions (culpable homicide not amounting to murder) in s.101 (IPC s.300)

1.  **Grave and sudden provocation**: the offender, deprived of the power of self-control by grave and sudden provocation, causes the death of the person who gave the provocation or of any other person by mistake or accident. **Provisos**: the provocation must not be sought or voluntarily provoked as an excuse; not given by anything done in obedience to law or by a public servant in lawful exercise of powers; not given by the lawful exercise of the right of private defence. *Test*: whether a reasonable man, placed in the same position, would have been so provoked (objective test). **Case**: *K.M. Nanavati v. State of Maharashtra* (1962): the accused shot his wife's lover after her confession; the Supreme Court held there was a time gap (cooling time), so the provocation was not **sudden**; the case ended the jury system in India (jury trial was abolished in Bombay after this case).

2.  **Exceeding the right of private defence** in good faith, without premeditation and without intention of doing more harm than necessary.

3.  **Public servant** (or person aiding) exceeding the powers given by law, in good faith, for advancement of public justice, in the belief the act is lawful and necessary.

4.  **Sudden fight** in the heat of passion upon a sudden quarrel, **without premeditation**, and without the offender having taken **undue advantage** or acted in a **cruel or unusual manner**. *Cases*: *Surinder Kumar v. Union Territory, Chandigarh* (1989); *Sandhya Jadhav v. State of Maharashtra* (2006).

5.  **Consent**: the person whose death is caused, being **above 18**, suffers death or takes the risk of death with his own **consent** (e.g., euthanasia-type cases, suicide pact survivors).

#### D. Distinction (very important)

| Point                 | Culpable homicide (s.100)                                                                                                                                                                                                        | Murder (s.101)                                                                                                                        |
|-----------------------|----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------|---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------|
| Relationship          | **Genus**                                                                                                                                                                                                                        | **Species**                                                                                                                           |
| Intention             | Intention of causing death or *likely* to cause death                                                                                                                                                                            | Intention of death, or injury known to be likely to cause death, or injury sufficient in the ordinary course of nature to cause death |
| Degree of probability | "**Likely**" to cause death (lower)                                                                                                                                                                                              | "**Must in all probability**" or "**sufficient in ordinary course of nature**" (higher)                                               |
| Punishment            | s.105: Part I: imprisonment for life, or imprisonment of not less than five years up to ten years, plus fine (where the act is done with intention); Part II: up to ten years, or fine, or both (where done with knowledge only) | s.103(1): **death or imprisonment for life** and fine                                                                                 |

- **Case law**: *Reg v. Govinda* (1876) (the accused knocked down his wife and, kneeling on her chest, struck her; since he did not intend to cause the bodily injury that resulted in death, it was culpable homicide not amounting to murder); *Virsa Singh v. State of Punjab* (1958) (the **four-step test** for clause (c) / thirdly: (i) bodily injury is present; (ii) its **nature** is proved; (iii) there was an **intention to inflict that particular injury**, i.e., not accidental; (iv) it is **sufficient in the ordinary course of nature** to cause death, an objective inquiry); *State of A.P. v. R. Punnayya* (1976) (clearly distinguishes culpable homicide from murder and lays down an approach of three stages); *Anda v. State of Rajasthan* (1966); *Rajwant Singh v. State of Kerala* (1966) (clause (d)); *Kesar Singh v. State of Haryana* (2008); *Pulicherla Nagaraju v. State of A.P.* (2006) (factors for deciding intention: nature of weapon, part of the body, manner of attack, circumstances).

- **Transferred malice**: s.102 (IPC s.301): if the offender causes the death of a person other than the one he intended, the liability is as if he had caused the death of the intended person.

- **Murder by life convict**: s.104 (IPC s.303): death or imprisonment for life (the old IPC s.303 mandatory death was struck down in *Mithu v. State of Punjab*, 1983).

- **Mob lynching** (s.103(2), **new**): when a group of **five or more persons** acting in concert commits murder on the ground of **race, caste or community, sex, place of birth, language, personal belief** or any other similar ground, each member shall be punished with **death or imprisonment for life** and fine. This follows *Tehseen S. Poonawalla v. Union of India* (2018) (guidelines to prevent mob lynching).

- **Attempt to murder** s.109 (IPC 307); **attempt to commit culpable homicide** s.110 (IPC 308): see topic 2.

### 4.2 Death by negligence, hit and run, suicide, dowry death

- **Causing death by negligence**: s.106(1) (IPC s.304A): death by **rash or negligent act** not amounting to culpable homicide: imprisonment up to **five years** and fine (IPC: two years); **registered medical practitioners** in the course of medical procedure: up to **two years** and fine. **Cases**: *Jacob Mathew v. State of Punjab* (2005) (only *gross* negligence is criminal; medical negligence); *Dr. Suresh Gupta* (2004); *Kurban Hussein Mohammedali Rangwalla v. State of Maharashtra* (1965) (rash and negligent act must be the **proximate** and direct cause of death); *Syad Akbar v. State of Karnataka* (1980) (*res ipsa loquitur*).

- **Hit and run**: s.106(2) (**new**): a driver who causes death by rash and negligent driving not amounting to culpable homicide, and **escapes without reporting** to a police officer or magistrate, shall be punished with up to **ten years** and fine. *(Its coming into effect was deferred after transporters' protests in January 2024; verify the present status before the exam.)*

- **Abetment of suicide**: s.108 (IPC s.306): up to ten years and fine; **child or person of unsound mind** s.107 (IPC s.305). **Attempt to commit suicide** (IPC s.309) is not an offence in the BNS except s.226 (to compel a public servant).

- **Dowry death** (s.80; IPC s.304B): death of a woman **within seven years of marriage**, caused by burns or bodily injury or occurring otherwise than under **normal circumstances**, where **soon before her death** she was subjected to **cruelty or harassment** by her husband or his relative **for, or in connection with, any demand for dowry**: such death is deemed a dowry death and the husband or relative **deemed to have caused her death**. **Punishment**: imprisonment of **not less than seven years** that may extend to **life**. **Presumption**: BSA s.118 (Evidence Act s.113B). **Cases**: *Kans Raj v. State of Punjab* (2000) (*soon before* means a proximate and live link, not a remote event); *Hira Lal v. State (Govt. of NCT of Delhi)* (2003); *Satbir Singh v. State of Haryana* (2021) (in cases of 304B, the court must presume; the words "soon before" are flexible); *Baijnath v. State of M.P.* (2016).

### 4.3 Hurt and grievous hurt (ss.114-125; IPC ss.319-338)

- **Hurt** (s.114; IPC s.319): causing **bodily pain, disease or infirmity** to any person.

- **Grievous hurt** (s.116; IPC s.320): the eight designations: (1) **emasculation**; (2) **permanent privation of sight of either eye**; (3) **permanent privation of hearing of either ear**; (4) **privation of any member or joint**; (5) **destruction or permanent impairing of the powers of any member or joint**; (6) **permanent disfiguration of the head or face**; (7) **fracture or dislocation of a bone or tooth**; (8) any hurt which **endangers life**, or causes the sufferer to be, during the space of **twenty days**, in severe bodily pain or unable to follow ordinary pursuits. *Mnemonic*: *E-E-E-M-M-F-F-20* (Emasculation, Eye, Ear, Member/joint privation, Member/joint impairing, Face disfiguration, Fracture, 20 days).

- **Voluntarily causing hurt** s.115(2) (IPC 323): up to one year or fine up to ₹10,000 (changed from the IPC's ₹1,000). **Voluntarily causing grievous hurt** s.117(2) (IPC 325): up to seven years and fine; **causing grievous hurt resulting in a permanent disabled or persistent vegetative state** s.117(3): rigorous imprisonment not less than ten years up to life (**new**).

- **Hurt by dangerous weapons or means** s.118 (IPC 324, 326); **hurt to extort property or to constrain to illegal act** s.119 (IPC 327, 329); **hurt to extort confession** s.120 (IPC 330, 331); **hurt to deter a public servant** s.121 (IPC 332, 333); **hurt on provocation** s.122 (IPC 334, 335); **by poison** s.123 (IPC 328).

- **Acid attack**: s.124(1) (IPC s.326A): causing **permanent or partial damage or deformity** by acid: imprisonment **not less than ten years** extending to **life** and fine (fine to be just and reasonable to meet medical expenses and paid to the victim); s.124(2) (IPC s.326B): **throwing or attempting to throw** acid: imprisonment **five to seven years** and fine. **Cases**: *Laxmi v. Union of India* (2014) (regulation of sale of acid and compensation); *Parivartan Kendra v. Union of India* (2015) (state compensation of ₹3 lakh).

- **Act endangering life or personal safety of others** s.125 (IPC 336-338): rash or negligent acts; causing hurt or grievous hurt.

### 4.4 Wrongful restraint and confinement (ss.126-127; IPC 339-348)

- **Wrongful restraint** (s.126; IPC 339): voluntarily **obstructs** a person so as to prevent him from proceeding in **any direction in which he has a right to proceed**. Punishment up to **one month** or fine up to ₹5,000 (BNS) or both. *Exception*: private way over land where a bona fide claim of right exists.

- **Wrongful confinement** (s.127; IPC 340): wrongfully restrains a person in such a manner as to prevent him from proceeding **beyond certain circumscribing limits**. Punishment rises with the period (three days or more: up to two years; ten days or more: higher), and for confinement **in secret**, **to extort property**, **to compel a confession**, etc. (ss.127(3)-(9)).

- **Distinction**: restraint is obstruction in **one direction**; confinement is restraint in **all directions** (complete).

- **Criminal force** s.130 (IPC 350): intentionally uses force on a person without consent to commit an offence or to cause (or knowing likely to cause) injury, fear or annoyance. **Assault** s.131 (IPC 351): making any gesture or preparation intending or knowing likely to cause any person present to apprehend that criminal force is about to be used; **words alone** do not amount to assault, but words may qualify the gesture. **Assault or criminal force to deter a public servant** s.132 (IPC 353); **to woman with intent to outrage modesty** s.74 (IPC 354); **with intent to dishonour** s.133 (IPC 355).

### 4.5 Kidnapping and abduction (ss.137-140; IPC 359-366)

- **Kidnapping** (s.137; IPC 359) has two forms: (1) **kidnapping from India**: conveying a person beyond the limits of India without that person's (or legally authorised person's) consent; (2) **kidnapping from lawful guardianship**: taking or enticing a **minor** (male under **16**, female under **18**) or a person of **unsound mind**, out of the keeping of the lawful guardian, without the guardian's consent.

- **Abduction** (s.138; IPC 362): compelling any person by **force**, or inducing by **deceitful means**, to go from any place. Abduction is **not an offence by itself**; it is punishable only when done with a particular object (to murder, to wrongfully confine, to compel marriage, to subject to grievous hurt or slavery, for illicit intercourse etc.).

- **Differences**: kidnapping needs a **minor or unsound person** (or conveying out of India); abduction applies to **any person**. Kidnapping **by taking or enticing** vs abduction **by force or deceit**. In kidnapping the **consent of the minor is immaterial**; in abduction the **consent of the abducted person** (if free) is material. Kidnapping is not a continuing offence while abduction is a continuing offence (under older case law).

- **Cases**: *S. Varadarajan v. State of Madras* (1965) (the girl voluntarily left her father's house and joined the accused; mere passive assistance is not *taking*; *taking* requires some active part by the accused); *State of Haryana v. Raja Ram* (1973) (enticement of minor = inducement through allurement); *Thakorlal D. Vadgama v. State of Gujarat* (1973).

- **Specific offences**: **kidnapping or maiming a minor for purposes of begging** s.139 (IPC 363A); **kidnapping or abducting in order to murder** s.140(1) (IPC 364); **kidnapping for ransom** s.140(2) (IPC 364A): **death or imprisonment for life** and fine; **kidnapping to subject a person to grievous hurt, slavery** s.140(3)-(4); **procuration of a minor girl** (IPC 366A) and **importation of girl from foreign country** (IPC 366B) are now in the BNS provisions on women and children.

### 4.6 Trafficking of persons (s.143; IPC s.370, 370A)

- Whoever **recruits, transports, harbours, transfers or receives** a person by using **threats, force, coercion, abduction, fraud, deception, abuse of power, or inducement** (including payment) for the purpose of **exploitation** commits trafficking. **Exploitation** includes **prostitution, other forms of sexual exploitation, slavery, servitude, forced removal of organs**. **Consent of the victim is immaterial**.

- **Punishment** rises with the gravity: **rigorous imprisonment of seven to ten years** and fine for the basic offence; **ten years to life** for trafficking of more than one person or of a **minor** (more than one minor: **fourteen years to life**); life imprisonment for a **repeat offender** or **public servant/police officer** involved. **Related law**: **Immoral Traffic (Prevention) Act, 1956**; *Vishal Jeet v. Union of India* (1990).

### Memory Capsule: Chapter 4

- **Murder = intention or probability "must"; culpable homicide = intention or "likely"**.

- **Exceptions to murder (5)**: provocation, private defence, public servant, sudden fight, consent.

- **Nanavati** = provocation/cooling time; **Virsa Singh** = four tests; **Mithu** = mandatory death struck down; **Jacob Mathew** = gross negligence; **Kans Raj** = soon before.

- **New BNS**: mob lynching (s.103(2)), hit and run (s.106(2)), permanent vegetative state (s.117(3)), organised crime (s.111, see topic 5).

- **Grievous hurt = 8 kinds** (20 days rule).

- **Kidnapping age**: male under 16, female under 18; **kidnapping for ransom** = death or life.

- **Restraint vs confinement**: partial vs total obstruction.

## Chapter 5: Offences Against the State and Terrorism

This topic covers (1) the classic offences against the State in BNS **Chapter VII (ss.147-158; IPC ss.121-130)**, (2) the **new BNS provisions on organised crime, petty organised crime and terrorism (ss.111-113)**, and (3) **special anti-terror laws** (UAPA 1967, NIA Act 2008, earlier TADA and POTA). Expect factual and "what is new in the BNS" questions.

### 5.1 Offences against the State (Chapter VII)

| **Waging, or attempting or abetting waging, war against the Government of India**                                                                                                         | 147     | 121                       | **Death or life imprisonment** and fine |
| Conspiracy to commit offences punishable by s.147                                                                                                                                         | 148     | 121A                      | Life, or up to 10 years, and fine       |
| **Collecting arms, etc., with the intention of waging war**                                                                                                                               | 149     | 122                       | Life, or up to 10 years, and fine       |

| **Assaulting President, Governor, etc., with intent to compel or restrain the exercise of lawful power**                                                                                  | 151     | 124                       | Up to 7 years and fine                  |

#### A. Waging war (s.147; IPC s.121)

- **Meaning**: an organised attempt, by force or numbers, to overthrow or overawe the Government, or to coerce it by force of arms; mere riot or an attack with no design to challenge the government's authority is not waging war. **Test**: the **purpose and the manner** (arms, organised, intention to usurp governmental powers), not the number of persons or success.

- **Cases**: *Mohd. Ajmal Mohammad Amir Kasab v. State of Maharashtra* (2012) (26/11 Mumbai attacks; a group of foreign nationals who attacked to challenge the sovereign authority of India were guilty of waging war; the Court explained the **"collective intention and design"** test); *State (NCT of Delhi) v. Navjot Sandhu @ Afzal Guru* (2005) (Parliament attack; waging war inferred from the planned assault on a seat of sovereignty); *Nazir Khan v. State of Delhi* (2003) (**hijacking** of an aircraft can be an act of waging war only if it is intended to wage war against the Government, otherwise not).

- **Constitutional protections**: the **President** and **Governor** are protected under BNS s.151.

#### B. Sedition and the new s.152

- **Old law (IPC s.124A)**: bringing or attempting to bring into **hatred or contempt**, or exciting or attempting to excite **disaffection**, towards the Government established by law; punishment life or up to three years, with or without fine.

- **Kedar Nath Singh v. State of Bihar** (1962): the Supreme Court **upheld** s.124A but read it down to apply only to acts involving **incitement to violence or intention or tendency to create public disorder** by acts of violence. **Balwant Singh v. State of Punjab** (1995): casual sloganeering ("Khalistan Zindabad") without any incitement was not sedition. **Shreya Singhal v. Union of India** (2015) distinguished **discussion and advocacy** from **incitement** and struck down s.66A of the IT Act. **Dr. Binayak Sen v. State of Chhattisgarh** (2011): possession of literature or sympathy with a cause does not by itself amount to sedition. **S.G. Vombatkere v. Union of India** (2022): the Supreme Court put the **sedition law in abeyance** and asked the Government to re-examine it; the BNS then dropped the word "sedition".

- **BNS s.152**: whoever, **purposely or knowingly**, by words (spoken or written), signs, visible representation, **electronic communication**, **use of financial means** or otherwise, **excites or attempts to excite secession, armed rebellion or subversive activities**, or **encourages feelings of separatist activities**, or **endangers the sovereignty or unity and integrity of India**, shall be punished with **imprisonment for life or up to seven years** and fine. **Explanation**: comments expressing disapproval of Government measures or administrative action **by lawful means** without exciting the above do not amount to an offence. Differences from sedition: the **term "sedition" and "disaffection" are gone**, the **mens rea "purposely or knowingly"** is explicit, **punishment has risen** (minimum is no longer three years; the maximum is life or seven years), and **electronic communication and financial means** are added. Critics argue it may be wider and vaguer.

### 5.2 Organised crime and terrorism in the BNS (ss.111-113)

These sections sit in Chapter VI (between culpable homicide and hurt) but are best learnt as a group.

#### A. Organised crime (s.111; modelled on MCOCA, 1999)

- **Definition**: any **continuing unlawful activity** (kidnapping, robbery, vehicle theft, extortion, land grabbing, contract killing, economic offences, **cyber-crimes**, trafficking in persons, drugs, weapons or illicit goods/services, human trafficking for prostitution or ransom) by a person or group **acting singly or jointly, as a member of an organised crime syndicate or on behalf of such syndicate**, by use of **violence, threat of violence, intimidation, coercion or other unlawful means**, with the objective of gaining **direct or indirect material benefit**, including **financial benefit**.

- **Continuing unlawful activity**: an activity prohibited by law, punishable with imprisonment of **three years or more**, undertaken by a person acting individually or jointly as a syndicate member, in respect of which **more than one charge-sheet** has been filed before a competent court **within the preceding ten years** and the court has taken cognizance.

- **Organised crime syndicate**: a group of **two or more persons** who, acting singly or collectively as a syndicate or gang, indulge in any continuing unlawful activity.

- **Punishment**: if the offence results in **death**: **death or imprisonment for life** and fine of **not less than ₹10 lakh**; in other cases: imprisonment of **five years to life** and fine of **not less than ₹5 lakh**. Abetting, attempting, conspiring, being a **member of a syndicate**, **harbouring** and possessing property derived from organised crime are separately punishable (typically **three to ten years** and fine of ₹5 lakh or more; membership at least five years).

- **Background**: Maharashtra Control of Organised Crime Act, 1999 (**MCOCA**), Karnataka Control of Organised Crimes Act, 2000, Gujarat Control of Terrorism and Organised Crime Act, 2015 (**GCTOC**), UP Control of Organised Crime Act, 2017. **State of Maharashtra v. Bharat Shanti Lal Shah** (2008) upheld the validity of MCOCA, including its provisions on interception of communications and confessions to police.

#### B. Petty organised crime (s.112)

- Any act of **theft, snatching, cheating, unauthorised selling of tickets, unauthorised betting or gambling, selling of public examination question papers**, or any similar criminal act by a **group or gang** that causes general feelings of insecurity among citizens. **Punishment**: imprisonment of **one to seven years** and fine. (Marked as a **new** offence aimed at street-level gangs.)

#### C. Terrorist act (s.113; modelled on UAPA)

- **Intent**: to **threaten or likely to threaten the unity, integrity, sovereignty, security or economic security of India**, or to **strike terror or likely to strike terror** in the people or any section of the people in India or any foreign country.

- **Means and outcomes**: by using **bombs, dynamite, explosives, inflammable substances, firearms, lethal weapons, poisonous or noxious gases, chemicals or any hazardous substance (biological, radioactive, nuclear)**, to cause or likely cause (i) **death or injuries**; (ii) **loss, damage or destruction of property**; (iii) **disruption of supplies or services essential to the life of the community**; (iv) **damage to property used for defence** or government purposes; or by (b) **overawing** by criminal force or show of criminal force or **causing the death of a public functionary**; or (c) **detaining, kidnapping or abducting** any person and **threatening to kill or injure** in order to **compel** the Government or any other person to do or abstain from doing any act.

- **Economic security**: the Explanation covers smuggling or circulating **high-quality counterfeit Indian currency** and similar acts damaging the monetary stability of India.

- **Punishment**: if the act results in the **death of any person**: **death or imprisonment for life (the remainder of natural life)** and fine; **in other cases**: imprisonment of **not less than five years up to life** and fine. **Conspiring, attempting, advocating, abetting, advising or facilitating** a terrorist act, **organising a terrorist camp**, **recruiting**, **harbouring** and **being a member of a terrorist organisation** have separate punishments (typically **five years to life**; **harbouring three years to life**; membership **life**).

## Chapter 6: Offences Against Property

BNS **Chapter XVII (ss.303-334)** covers theft, snatching, extortion, robbery, dacoity, criminal misappropriation, criminal breach of trust, receiving stolen property, cheating, fraudulent deeds, mischief and criminal trespass. **Chapter XVIII (ss.335-350)** covers forgery and property marks. UGC NET repeatedly tests the **distinctions** among theft, extortion, robbery, dacoity, misappropriation, criminal breach of trust and cheating.

### 6.1 Theft and snatching (s.303-306; IPC 378-382)

- **Theft (s.303(1); IPC s.378)**: whoever, **intending to take dishonestly** any **movable property** **out of the possession of any person without that person's consent**, **moves** that property in order to such taking, is said to commit theft.

- **Five ingredients**: (1) **dishonest intention** (to cause wrongful gain or wrongful loss); (2) the property must be **movable** (things attached to earth are movable once severed); (3) it must be **taken out of the possession** of another person (a person's possession can be constructive); (4) taken **without consent**; (5) there must be some **moving** of the property.

- **Punishment**: s.303(2): imprisonment up to **three years**, or fine, or both; **repeat offenders**: **rigorous imprisonment of one to five years** and fine. **Community service** where the value is **less than ₹5,000**, the accused is a **first-time convict** and has **returned or restored** the value (new).

- **Aggravated forms**: theft in a **dwelling house**, means of transportation or place of worship (s.305; IPC 380); theft by **clerk or servant** (s.306; IPC 381); theft after **preparation to cause death, hurt or restraint** (s.307; IPC 382).

- **Snatching (s.304; new)**: theft becomes snatching when, **to commit theft**, the offender **suddenly or quickly or forcibly seizes, secures, grabs or takes away** any movable property from a **person** or from his possession. Punishment: up to **three years** and fine.

- **Cases**: *K.N. Mehra v. State of Rajasthan* (1957) (two pilots, taking an aeroplane from the aerodrome with a dishonest intention to use it for a flight and not to return it; the moving of the aircraft amounted to theft); *Pyare Lal Bhargava v. State of Rajasthan* (1963) (a government servant removing a file from the custody of an office temporarily for a bribe **committed theft**; the temporary taking is enough when dishonest intention exists); *Pyare Lal* is the standard answer on the **temporary taking** question. Distinguish **taking** (theft) and **taking by consent induced by fear** (extortion).

### 6.2 Extortion (s.308; IPC ss.383-389)

- **Definition (s.308(1); IPC s.383)**: whoever **intentionally puts any person in fear of any injury** to that person or any other, and thereby **dishonestly induces** the person so put in fear to **deliver** to any person any **property**, **valuable security**, or anything signed or sealed which may be converted into a valuable security, commits extortion.

- **Elements**: (1) **intentionally putting a person in fear of injury**; (2) the fear is of injury to **that person or another**; (3) **dishonestly inducing** the person to deliver; (4) **delivery** of property or valuable security. If the person is not actually induced to deliver, there is **attempt to extort** (s.308(3) type provisions).

- **Punishment** (illustrative): extortion up to **seven years** or fine or both; putting a person in fear of **death or grievous hurt** in order to extort: up to **ten years** and fine; fear of an **accusation** of an offence punishable with death, life or ten years: up to ten years and fine. (Check sub-section letters in the Act.)

- **Case**: *R.S. Nayak v. A.R. Antulay* (1986) (the Court noted that the essence of extortion is the **inducement to deliver**; mere demand is not extortion).

### 6.3 Robbery and dacoity (ss.309-313; IPC 390-402)

- **Robbery (s.309(1); IPC s.390)**: **all robbery has either theft or extortion**. **Theft is robbery** if, in committing the theft or in carrying away or attempting to carry away the property, the offender **voluntarily causes or attempts to cause death, hurt or wrongful restraint, or fear of instant death, instant hurt or instant wrongful restraint**. **Extortion is robbery** if the offender, at the time of committing the extortion, is **in the presence of the person put in fear** and commits the extortion by putting that person in **fear of instant death, instant hurt or instant wrongful restraint** to that person or another, and thereby induces the person **then and there** to deliver up the thing extorted.

- **Punishment**: robbery: **rigorous imprisonment up to ten years** and fine; if on the **highway between sunset and sunrise**: up to **fourteen years**; robbery with hurt or attempts to cause death or grievous hurt carry higher punishments (up to **life**). The **minimum punishment of seven years** applies where the robber uses a deadly weapon (IPC 397: **not less than seven years**).

- **Dacoity (s.310(2); IPC s.391)**: when **five or more persons** conjointly commit or attempt to commit a robbery, or where the **whole number of persons conjointly committing or attempting to commit a robbery, and persons present and aiding such commission or attempt, amount to five or more**, every person so committing, attempting or aiding is said to commit dacoity. **Punishment**: **life imprisonment or rigorous imprisonment up to ten years** and fine. **Dacoity with murder (s.310(3); IPC 396)**: **death, life imprisonment, or rigorous imprisonment not less than ten years**, and fine, for **every member** of the gang if any one commits murder. **Preparation to commit dacoity**, **assembling for the purpose** and **belonging to a gang of dacoits** (IPC 399, 400, 402) are separately punishable, as is **belonging to a gang of thieves** (IPC 401; see also BNS provisions on gangs).

- **Cases**: *Venu v. State of Punjab* (2008) (the force must be used at the time of theft in order to constitute robbery); *Ram Lakhan v. State of U.P.* (1983); *Shyam Behari v. State of U.P.* (1957) (when the offence is dacoity, all members are liable for the death caused; common intention).

### 6.4 Distinguishing theft, extortion, robbery and dacoity

| Point               | Theft                                       | Extortion                                            | Robbery                                                      | Dacoity                             |
|---------------------|---------------------------------------------|------------------------------------------------------|--------------------------------------------------------------|-------------------------------------|
| Core                | Taking movable property **without consent** | Property **delivered** because of **fear of injury** | Theft or extortion plus **violence or fear of instant harm** | Robbery by **five or more** persons |
| Consent             | **No** consent                              | Consent is **obtained by fear** (no free consent)    | Consent absent or by fear of instant harm                    | As robbery                          |
| Property            | Movable only                                | Movable, valuable security                           | Movable                                                      | Movable                             |
| Presence of victim  | Not necessary                               | Victim may be absent (threat can be of future harm)  | Victim must be **present** and fear must be **instant**      | As robbery                          |
| Number of offenders | One or more                                 | One or more                                          | One or more                                                  | **Five or more**                    |
| Fear                | None                                        | Fear of **any injury**                               | Fear of **instant** death, hurt, restraint                   | As robbery                          |

### 6.5 Criminal misappropriation and criminal breach of trust (ss.314-317; IPC 403-414)

- **Criminal misappropriation (s.314; IPC s.403)**: dishonestly **misappropriates** or converts to his own use any **movable property**. The property **comes into possession innocently** (e.g., found property), and dishonest intention arises later. Punishment: imprisonment of **six months to two years** and fine (the BNS prescribes a minimum, unlike the IPC's maximum of two years only). *Property of a deceased person* s.315 (IPC 404).

- **Criminal breach of trust (s.316(1); IPC s.405)**: whoever, being in any manner **entrusted with property**, or with **dominion over property**, **dishonestly misappropriates or converts to his own use** that property, or **dishonestly uses or disposes of** it **in violation of any direction of law** prescribing the mode in which such trust is to be discharged, or of any legal contract, express or implied, which he has made touching the discharge of such trust, or **wilfully suffers any other person** to do so, commits criminal breach of trust.

- **Punishment**: s.316(2) general CBT: up to **five years** or fine or both; **carrier, wharfinger or warehouse-keeper** (IPC 407); **clerk or servant** (IPC 408): up to **seven years** and fine; **public servant, banker, merchant, factor, broker, attorney or agent** (IPC 409): **imprisonment for life or up to ten years** and fine.

- **Differences**: *Misappropriation*: property **comes innocently**; *CBT*: property **entrusted** (a special relationship of trust) and misappropriated. **CBT is aggravated misappropriation**.

- **Cases**: *R.K. Dalmia v. Delhi Administration* (1962) (entrusted property can include a company's funds; directors of a company can commit CBT); *Velji Raghavji Patel v. State of Maharashtra* (1965) (**a partner** who misappropriates partnership property is not guilty of CBT unless the property is entrusted separately; the partner is a co-owner); *Jaswantrai Manilal Akhaney v. State of Bombay* (1956); *Ram Narayan Popli v. CBI* (2003) (CBT; public servant and bank officers); *S.W. Palanitkar v. State of Bihar* (2001) (CBT vs cheating).

- **Stolen property (s.317; IPC 410-414)**: property transferred by **theft, extortion, robbery, criminal misappropriation or CBT** is "stolen property". **Dishonestly receiving** stolen property: up to **three years**; **habitually dealing**: **life or up to ten years**; **assisting in concealment**: up to three years.

### 6.6 Cheating (s.318; IPC ss.415-420)

- **Definition (s.318(1); IPC s.415)**: whoever, by **deceiving any person**, **fraudulently or dishonestly induces** the person deceived to **deliver any property** to any person, or to **consent that any person shall retain any property**, or **intentionally induces** the person deceived to **do or omit to do anything which he would not do or omit if he were not so deceived**, and which act or omission causes or is **likely to cause damage or harm** to that person in **body, mind, reputation or property**, is said to cheat.

- **Essentials**: (1) **deception** (by words, conduct or **concealment** of facts); (2) **fraudulent or dishonest inducement**; (3) **delivery** of property or an act/omission; (4) **damage or harm** (body, mind, reputation, property). **Mens rea at the time of making the promise** is critical.

- **Punishment**: s.318(2): up to **three years** or fine or both; s.318(3): cheating a person whose **interest the offender is bound to protect**: up to **five years**; s.318(4) (IPC 420): cheating and **dishonestly inducing delivery of property** or making/altering a valuable security: up to **seven years** and fine.

- **Cheating by personation** s.319 (IPC 416, 419): pretending to be some other person; up to **five years** (online personation too).

- **Case law**: *Hridaya Ranjan Prasad Verma v. State of Bihar* (2000) (distinction between **mere breach of contract** and **cheating**: cheating needs **dishonest intention at the beginning**); *Iridium India Telecom v. Motorola* (2011); *Indian Oil Corporation v. NEPC India Ltd* (2006) (a civil dispute should not be dressed as a criminal case); *Ram Jas v. State of U.P.* (1970).

### 6.7 Mischief (s.324; IPC ss.425-440)

- **Mischief**: intention or knowledge of **likely to cause wrongful loss or damage** to the public or any person, **causing the destruction of any property or any such change in it** that its **value or utility is destroyed or diminished**. *Wrongful loss or gain need not be dishonest*; the **ownership** of the property is irrelevant (even the owner can commit mischief against a co-owner).

- **Aggravated mischief**: by **fire or explosive** (IPC 435-436), mischief to **dwelling** or **a vessel**, mischief by injury to **public road, bridge**, or **inundation**. Punishments rise from **six months** (simple) to **ten years or life** in the gravest forms.

### 6.8 Criminal trespass and house-breaking (ss.329-334; IPC 441-462)

- **Criminal trespass (s.329(1); IPC s.441)**: entering into or upon property in possession of another **with intent to commit an offence** or to **intimidate, insult or annoy** the person in possession, or **remaining unlawfully** with that intent. **House-trespass** (IPC 442): criminal trespass by entering into a **building, tent or vessel used as a human dwelling**, or as a place of worship or custody of property. **Lurking house-trespass** (IPC 443): taking precautions to conceal; **house-breaking** (s.331; IPC 445): **six ways** (e.g., through an opening made by the trespasser; by removal of a fastening). **Lurking house-trespass or house-breaking by night** (IPC 456-457) is punished more severely; *by night* means **sunset to sunrise**.

- **Specific offences**: **house-trespass to commit an offence punishable with death, life imprisonment, or imprisonment** (s.332; IPC 449-451); **house-trespass after preparation for hurt, assault or wrongful restraint** (s.333; IPC 452); **dishonestly breaking open a receptacle containing property** (s.334; IPC 461).

- ***Illustration: entering another's house with intent to commit theft is*** house-trespass.

### 6.9 Forgery and related offences (ss.335-350; IPC 463-489)

- **Making a false document (s.335; IPC s.464)**: dishonestly or fraudulently making, signing, sealing or executing a document or electronic record, or part of it, with the **intention of causing it to be believed that it was made by the authority of a person** who did not make it, or by altering the document **without lawful authority**, or by causing a person of unsound mind or intoxicated to sign it. **Forgery (s.336; IPC s.463)**: making a false document or false electronic record **with intent to cause damage or injury** to the public or any person, or to support any claim or title, or to cause any person to part with property, or to enter into any express or implied contract, or **with intent to commit fraud**. **Punishment**: up to **two years** or fine or both (basic); forgery of a **valuable security or will**, **for the purpose of cheating** and **record of a court** attract higher punishments (up to **seven years** to **life** in some forms).

- **Case**: *Mohammed Ibrahim v. State of Bihar* (2009) (making a false document means **making** with the **intention of forgery**; **mere false statement** in a sale deed is not forgery).

### Memory Capsule: Chapter 6

- **Theft**: s.303; dishonest intention; movable; **moving**. **Snatching**: s.304; **community service** for small first-time theft.

- **Extortion = delivery under fear**; **Robbery = theft or extortion + instant fear**; **Dacoity = five or more**.

- **Misappropriation = innocent possession, then dishonesty; CBT = entrustment first**.

- **Cheating** needs **dishonest intention from the start**; breach of contract alone is not cheating.

- **Cases**: *Pyare Lal Bhargava* (temporary taking = theft), *K.N. Mehra*, *R.S. Nayak* (extortion), *Velji Raghavji Patel* (partner), *Hridaya Ranjan Prasad Verma* (cheating vs breach), *Mohammed Ibrahim* (forgery).

- **Punishment hooks**: CBT by public servant/banker = **life or ten years**; dacoity = **life or ten years**; dacoity with murder = **death/life/10+ years**.

## Chapter 7: Offences Against Women and Children

Chapter V of the BNS (ss.63-99) gathers offences against women and children: **rape and related sexual offences (ss.63-73)**, **sexual harassment and related offences (ss.74-79)**, **dowry death and cruelty (ss.80, 85-86)**, **marriage-related offences (ss.81-84)**, **miscarriage (ss.88-92)** and **offences against children (ss.93-99)**. This is the **most current-affairs-heavy** topic: look for recent Supreme Court judgments and BNS changes.

### 7.1 Rape and sexual offences (ss.63-73; IPC 375-376E)

#### A. Definition (s.63; IPC s.375)

A man commits rape if he **(a)** penetrates his penis, to any extent, into the **vagina, mouth, urethra or anus** of a woman or makes her do so with him or another person; **(b)** inserts, to any extent, any **object or a part of the body, not being the penis**, into the vagina, urethra or anus; **(c)** **manipulates** any part of the body of a woman so as to cause penetration; or **(d)** applies his **mouth** to the vagina, anus or urethra of a woman or makes her do so with him or another person, under any of **seven descriptions**:

1.  **Against her will**.

2.  **Without her consent**.

3.  **With her consent obtained by putting her, or any person in whom she is interested, in fear of death or hurt**.

4.  With her consent, when the man **knows he is not her husband** and her consent is given because she believes he is another man to whom she is or believes herself to be lawfully married.

5.  With her consent, when at the time of giving consent, by reason of **unsoundness of mind or intoxication** or the administration by him personally or through another of any **stupefying or unwholesome substance**, she is unable to understand the nature and consequences of that to which she gives consent.

6.  With or without her consent, when she is **under eighteen years** of age.

7.  When she is **unable to communicate consent**.

- **Explanation 2 (consent)**: **unequivocal voluntary agreement** by words, gestures or any form of verbal or non-verbal communication; mere **lack of physical resistance** is not consent.

- **Exception 1**: a medical procedure or intervention is not rape. **Exception 2**: sexual intercourse or sexual acts by a man with his **own wife**, the wife **not being under eighteen years** of age, is not rape (the **marital rape exception** remains). *Independent Thought v. Union of India* (2017) read down the IPC exception to **raise the age from 15 to 18**. A challenge to the exception itself (*RIT Foundation v. Union of India*) produced a **split verdict in the Delhi High Court (2022)** and is pending before the Supreme Court.

State of Gujarat* (1983) (testimony of the victim needs no corroboration, as long as it is credible); *State of Punjab v. State of Goa* (2023) (the Court criticised delays in implementing the POSH Act).

### 7.3 Cruelty, dowry and domestic violence

- **Cruelty by husband or relatives** (s.85; IPC s.498A): husband or relative subjects a woman to **cruelty**: up to **three years** and fine. **Cruelty** (s.86) = (a) **wilful conduct likely to drive the woman to suicide** or to cause **grave injury or danger to life, limb or health** (mental or physical); (b) **harassment** with a view to coercing her or any related person to meet an **unlawful demand for property or valuable security**. It is **cognizable and non-bailable** under the CrPC/BNSS schedule, and **non-compoundable** (in ordinary cases).

- **Cases**: *Arnesh Kumar v. State of Bihar* (2014) (no **automatic arrest**; police must record reasons under the notice-of-appearance procedure); *Sushil Kumar Sharma v. Union of India* (2005) (upheld the constitutionality; the Court said **misuse** cannot invalidate the law); *Rajesh Sharma v. State of U.P.* (2017) (**Family Welfare Committees**; later modified in *Social Action Forum for Manav Adhikar v. Union of India*, 2018); *Preeti Gupta v. State of Jharkhand* (2010) (cautioned against **exaggerated allegations** against distant relatives); *Kahkashan Kausar v. State of Bihar* (2022) (omnibus allegations against in-laws should be examined carefully); in **Dara Lakshmi Narayana v. State of Telangana** (2024), the Supreme Court again warned of the **misuse** of s.498A and s.85 BNS.

- **Dowry death** (s.80; IPC 304B): see topic 4. **Dowry Prohibition Act, 1961**: giving, taking or abetting dowry: **five years** and fine not less than **₹15,000** or the value of the dowry, whichever is more; **demanding** dowry: six months to two years.

- **Protection of Women from Domestic Violence Act, 2005** (civil remedies: protection, residence, monetary relief, custody); *Hiral P. Harsora v. Kusum Narottamdas Harsora* (2016) (the words "adult male" struck down so that women can also be **respondents**); *D. Velusamy v. D. Patchaiammal* (2010) (live-in relationship in the nature of marriage).

### 7.4 Offences relating to marriage (ss.81-84; IPC 493-498)

- **Cohabitation by a man deceitfully inducing belief of lawful marriage** (s.81; IPC 493): up to **ten years**. **Bigamy** (s.82; IPC 494, 495): marrying again during the lifetime of the spouse: up to **seven years** and fine; **concealing** the former marriage: up to **ten years**. **Marriage ceremony fraudulently gone through** without lawful marriage (s.83; IPC 496): up to **seven years**. **Enticing a married woman** (s.84; IPC 498): up to **two years**.

- **Exceptions to bigamy**: the first marriage has been declared void by a court; the spouse has been **continuously absent for seven years** and not known to be alive. **Cases**: *Sarla Mudgal v. Union of India* (1995) (conversion to Islam only to contract a second marriage does not end the first marriage; **bigamy** is punishable); *Lily Thomas v. Union of India* (2000) (same).

- **Adultery (IPC 497)** was **struck down** by *Joseph Shine v. Union of India* (2018) and is **absent** from the BNS.

### 7.5 Miscarriage and related offences (ss.88-92; IPC 312-318)

- **Causing miscarriage** (s.88; IPC 312): up to **three years**; if the woman is **quick with child**: up to **seven years**. **Without the woman's consent** (s.89; IPC 313): **life** or up to ten years. **Death caused by an act done with intent to cause miscarriage** (s.90). **Act done to prevent the child being born alive** (s.91). **Causing death of a quick unborn child** by an act amounting to culpable homicide (s.92).

- **Medical Termination of Pregnancy Act, 1971** (amended 2021): termination allowed up to **20 weeks** on the opinion of **one** registered medical practitioner and up to **24 weeks** for special categories (survivors of rape or incest, minors, change of marital status during pregnancy, physically disabled women, mentally ill women, foetal malformation, emergency) on the opinion of **two** practitioners; **no upper limit** where a **Medical Board** diagnoses **substantial foetal abnormalities**. **Cases**: *Suchita Srivastava v. Chandigarh Administration* (2009) (reproductive choice is part of **personal liberty**); *X v. Principal Secretary, Health and Family Welfare Department, Govt. of NCT of Delhi* (2022) (the Court held that **unmarried women** are also entitled to 24-week abortion and that **marital rape** is relevant for the purposes of the MTP Act); *K.S. Puttaswamy v. Union of India* (2017) (right to privacy includes reproductive choices).

- **Exposure and abandonment of a child under twelve** by parent or person in charge (s.93; IPC 317): up to **seven years**. **Concealment of birth by secret disposal of a dead body** (s.94; IPC 318): up to **two years**.

### 7.6 Offences against children in the BNS (ss.95-99; also s.139)

- **Hiring, employing or engaging a child to commit an offence** (s.95; **new**): **three to ten years** and fine. **Procuration of a child** (s.96; IPC 366A): up to **ten years**. **Selling a minor for prostitution** (s.98; IPC 372): **five to fourteen years**. **Buying a minor for prostitution** (s.99; IPC 373): **seven to fourteen years**. **Kidnapping or maiming a child for begging** (s.139; IPC 363A).

- **Gang rape of a woman under 18** (s.70(2)): **life (natural life) or death**.

### 7.7 POCSO Act, 2012 (Protection of Children from Sexual Offences)

- **Child** = any person **below 18** (gender-neutral). Consent of a child is **immaterial**. Offences: **penetrative sexual assault** (ss.3-4: **ten years to life**; **under 16**: **twenty years to life**); **aggravated penetrative sexual assault** (ss.5-6: **twenty years to life or death**, after the **2019 amendment**); **sexual assault** (ss.7-8: three to five years); **aggravated sexual assault** (ss.9-10: five to seven years); **sexual harassment of a child** (ss.11-12: up to three years); **use of child for pornography** (ss.13-14); **storage of child pornography** (s.15).

- **Procedure**: **mandatory reporting** (s.19; failure punishable under s.21), **Special Courts** (s.28), **presumption of guilt** (s.29, rebuttable), **child-friendly procedure**, **in-camera trial**, **identity protection** (s.23).

- **Recent cases**: *Attorney General for India v. Satish Ekanathrao Ghoge* (2021) (the "**skin-to-skin**" contact requirement of the Bombay High Court was set aside; **sexual intent**, not skin contact, is the test); *Just Rights for Children Alliance v. S. Harish* (2024) (**storing or viewing child pornography** is an offence under POCSO s.15 and IT Act s.67B; the term "child pornography" should be replaced by **child sexual exploitative and abuse material (CSEAM)**); *Independent Thought* (2017); *Sakshi v. Union of India* (2004) (rules for child witness trials).

- **Child marriage**: **Prohibition of Child Marriage Act, 2006** (marriage of girls under 18 and boys under 21 is **voidable** at the option of the child; offenders face **two years' rigorous imprisonment** and fine up to **₹1 lakh**); *Society for Enlightenment and Voluntary Action v. Union of India* (2024) (guidelines for effective enforcement; personal laws cannot override the Act).

- **Other laws**: **Immoral Traffic (Prevention) Act, 1956**; **Indecent Representation of Women (Prohibition) Act, 1986**; **Juvenile Justice (Care and Protection of Children) Act, 2015**; **Child Labour (Prohibition and Regulation) Act, 1986** (amended 2016: prohibits employment of children under 14 in all occupations and adolescents in hazardous work).

### Memory Capsule: Chapter 7

- **Rape**: seven descriptions; **age of consent 18**; **marital exception** only for wife **18 or above**.

- **Punishment ladder**: rape 10 years to life; **under 16**: 20 years to life; **under 12**: 20 years to life or death; **gang rape**: 20 years to life; **gang rape of a minor under 18**: life or death.

- **s.69 BNS** (deceitful means) is **new** and is **not rape**.

- **Cases**: *Tukaram* (Mathura) -\> 1983 amendment; *Vishaka* -\> POSH; *Lillu* (two-finger test); *Pramod Pawar* (false promise of marriage); *Independent Thought* (marital rape of minors); *Arnesh Kumar* (498A arrest); *Joseph Shine* (adultery); *Satish Ghoge* (skin-to-skin); *Just Rights for Children Alliance* (child pornography, 2024).

- **POCSO**: child = under 18; **special courts**; **reporting mandatory**.

## Chapter 8: Drug Trafficking and Counterfeiting

This topic has **two parts**: (1) drug trafficking under the **Narcotic Drugs and Psychotropic Substances Act, 1985 (NDPS Act)**; (2) **counterfeiting** of coin, currency notes and Government stamps (BNS Chapter X, IPC Chapters XII), plus counterfeit goods under IP laws. Questions are mostly **factual** (quantities, punishments, sections, bail, seizure procedure) and from **Supreme Court case law**.

### 8.1 NDPS Act, 1985: background and scheme

- **Constitutional and treaty background**: Article 47 (Directive Principle to prohibit intoxicating drinks and drugs injurious to health); India is a party to the **Single Convention on Narcotic Drugs, 1961**, the **Convention on Psychotropic Substances, 1971** and the **UN Convention Against Illicit Traffic in Narcotic Drugs and Psychotropic Substances, 1988**. The Act replaced the **Opium Act 1857, Opium Act 1878 and Dangerous Drugs Act 1930**.

- **Definitions (s.2)**: **narcotic drug** (coca leaf, cannabis (hemp), opium, poppy straw and all manufactured drugs); **psychotropic substance** (substances in the Schedule); **cannabis** (**charas**, **ganja** (flowering or fruiting tops, excluding seeds and leaves when not accompanied by the tops) and any mixture); **bhang** (leaves and seeds) is excluded from the definition of cannabis in the Act and is regulated by **State laws**. **Small quantity**, **commercial quantity** and the in-between **intermediate quantity** are fixed by Central Government notification (2001 rationalisation).

- **Key sections**:

  - **s.8**: **prohibition** of cultivation, production, manufacture, possession, sale, purchase, transport, warehousing, use, consumption, inter-State import or export, import into or export from India of narcotic drugs and psychotropic substances **except for medical or scientific purposes** and in accordance with the Act (licence or permit).

  - **ss.15-23**: specific offences (poppy straw s.15; coca s.16; prepared opium s.17; opium poppy and opium s.18; cannabis s.20; **manufactured drugs and preparations s.21**; **psychotropic substances s.22**; illegal import, export and transhipment s.23).

  - **s.24**: external dealings; **s.25**: allowing premises to be used; **s.27**: **consumption**; **s.27A**: **financing illicit traffic and harbouring offenders** (**ten to twenty years**, fine ₹1 lakh to ₹2 lakh); **s.29**: **abetment and criminal conspiracy**; **s.30**: preparation; **s.31**: enhanced punishment for subsequent offences; **s.31A**: **death penalty** for certain repeat offences; **s.32A**: no suspension, remission or commutation of sentence.

  - **s.35**: **presumption of culpable mental state**; **s.54**: **presumption from possession** of illicit articles; **s.37**: offences are **cognizable and non-bailable**, with **twin conditions** for bail in cases involving commercial quantity; **s.64A**: **immunity** for addicts who volunteer for treatment.

  - **ss.41-43**: **powers of search, seizure and arrest**; **s.42**: entry, search, seizure and arrest **without warrant** (with recording of grounds and reporting to the superior within **72 hours**); **s.50**: conditions for **personal search** (the person must be told of the right to be searched before a **Gazetted Officer or Magistrate**); **s.52A**: **disposal** of seized drugs (inventory, sampling, magistrate certification); **s.67**: power to call for information and examine persons; **s.50A**: **controlled delivery**.

- **Authorities**: **Narcotics Control Bureau (NCB)**, **Central Bureau of Narcotics**, State Police, Customs, **Revenue Intelligence** (DRI). **Prevention of Illicit Traffic in Narcotic Drugs and Psychotropic Substances Act, 1988 (PIT-NDPS)** provides **preventive detention** of traffickers.

### 8.2 Punishment structure

| Quantity                                                    | Punishment (ss.15-24 type offences)                                                                                                                                                                                                                                      |
|-------------------------------------------------------------|--------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------|
| **Small quantity**                                          | **Rigorous imprisonment up to one year**, or fine up to **₹10,000**, or both (for trafficking offences; **consumption** under s.27 is **up to six months for non-cocaine type drugs**, up to **one year** for specified hard drugs, with the fine of ₹10,000 or ₹20,000) |
| **More than small but less than commercial (intermediate)** | **Rigorous imprisonment up to ten years** and fine up to **₹1 lakh**                                                                                                                                                                                                     |
| **Commercial quantity**                                     | **Rigorous imprisonment of ten to twenty years** and fine of **₹1 lakh to ₹2 lakh** (the court may impose a higher fine for reasons recorded)                                                                                                                            |
| **Repeat offenders (s.31)**                                 | **One and a half times** the punishment for the first offence                                                                                                                                                                                                            |
| **s.31A**                                                   | Death for repeat offences involving specified **very large quantities** (made **discretionary** by the **2014 amendment** after *Indian Harm Reduction Network v. Union of India* (Bombay High Court, 2011) read it down)                                                |

*(Old quantity limits were revised in 2001; the Act treats small and commercial quantities separately for each drug.)*

**Illustrative quantities** (from the 2001 notification; confirm in the schedule): **heroin** small 5 g / commercial 250 g; **charas** 100 g / 1 kg; **ganja** 1 kg / 20 kg; **cocaine** 2 g / 100 g; **opium** 25 g / 2.5 kg; **morphine** 5 g / 250 g; **MDMA** 0.5 g / 10 g; **LSD** 0.002 g / 0.1 g.

### 8.3 Bail, presumptions and search procedure (the heart of NDPS case law)

- **Twin conditions for bail (s.37)**: for offences involving **commercial quantity** (and ss.19, 24, 27A), the accused shall **not be released on bail** unless (i) the **Public Prosecutor** has had an opportunity to oppose; and (ii) the court is satisfied that there are **reasonable grounds for believing that he is not guilty** of the offence and that he is **not likely to commit any offence while on bail**. These conditions override the ordinary bail provisions.

- **Presumption of culpable mental state (s.35)**: the court presumes **knowledge and intention** and the accused must prove **absence of such mental state** beyond reasonable doubt (a **reverse onus** provision). **s.54**: presumption from possession of illicit articles; **Noor Aga v. State of Punjab** (2008) upheld the **validity** of ss.35 and 54 but held that the prosecution must first **establish the foundational facts** beyond reasonable doubt before the presumption applies.

- **Search and seizure compliance**:

  - *State of Punjab v. Balbir Singh* (1994): **compliance with ss.42 and 50 is mandatory**; non-compliance can vitiate the conviction.

  - *State of Punjab v. Baldev Singh* (1999) (Constitution Bench): s.50 is **mandatory**; the accused must be informed of his **right** to be searched before a **Gazetted Officer or Magistrate**.

  - *Vijaysinh Chandubha Jadeja v. State of Gujarat* (2011) (Constitution Bench): the **informing of the right** under s.50 is **mandatory** and should be **substantial** (proper compliance), with an **oral** explanation being enough.

  - *Union of India v. Mohanlal* (2016) and *Mohanlal v. State of Punjab* (2018): importance of **s.52A**; **sampling** must be done **before a Magistrate**; non-compliance and **delay** can undermine the prosecution.

- **Admissibility of statements**: **Tofan Singh v. State of Tamil Nadu** (2020/2021): officers vested with powers of investigation under the NDPS Act (such as **NCB officers**) are **police officers** within s.25 of the Evidence Act, so a **confessional statement recorded under s.67** is **inadmissible** as evidence of guilt. (The same principle now falls under the **BSA** corresponding provisions.)

- **Other constitutional rulings**: **Dadu @ Tulsidas v. State of Maharashtra** (2000) struck down the bar on **suspension of sentence** in s.32A as unconstitutional; **Mithu v. State of Punjab** (1983) invalidated **mandatory death** under IPC s.303 and is cited for the policy against mandatory death sentences; the Supreme Court has repeatedly stressed **proportionality** in **bail** for small-quantity or addict cases.

- **Treatment and rehabilitation**: s.64A and the **National Policy on Narcotic Drugs and Psychotropic Substances**; **de-addiction** under the *Mental Healthcare Act, 2017* approach; the **Narcotics** policy balances **harm reduction** and deterrence.

- **Interface with the BNS**: **organised crime** (s.111) expressly includes trafficking in **drugs, weapons or illicit goods** by a syndicate, so a trafficking ring can be proceeded against under both the NDPS Act and BNS s.111.

### 8.4 Counterfeiting: coin, currency notes and Government stamps

The offences sit in **BNS Chapter X (ss.178-188)**, replacing **IPC ss.230-263A** (coin and stamps) and **IPC ss.489A-489E** (currency notes and bank notes).

| Offence                                                                                                 | IPC      | Punishment (IPC; BNS mirrors it)                         |
|---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------|----------|----------------------------------------------------------|
| **Counterfeiting Indian coin**                                                                          | 232      | **Life** or up to **ten years** and fine                 |
| Counterfeiting other coin                                                                               | 231      | Up to **seven years** and fine                           |
| **Making, buying or selling instruments** for counterfeiting coin                                       | 233-234  | Up to **three years** (Indian coin: up to **ten years**) |
| Possession of counterfeit coin, delivery of counterfeit coin                                            | 239-243  | Varies (up to **seven** years)                           |
| **Counterfeiting currency notes or bank notes**                                                         | 489A     | **Life** or up to **ten years** and fine                 |
| **Using as genuine** forged or counterfeit currency notes or bank notes                                 | 489B     | **Life** or up to **ten years** and fine                 |
| **Possession** of forged or counterfeit notes **knowing or having reason to believe** them to be forged | 489C     | Up to **seven years**, or fine, or both                  |
| Making or possessing **instruments or materials** for forging or counterfeiting notes                   | 489D     | **Life** or up to **ten years** and fine                 |
| Making or using documents **resembling currency notes**                                                 | 489E     | Fine up to **₹100**                                      |
| **Counterfeiting Government stamps**, using or possessing                                               | 255-263A | Life or up to ten years in the gravest forms             |

- **Key ingredients**: the **offender must know or have reason to believe** that the note is counterfeit (for possession); the counterfeit must be **capable of being passed as genuine** (resemblance), but **perfect imitation is not required**. **Case**: *Umashankar v. State of Chhattisgarh* (2001): **mere possession** of forged notes without **knowledge or reason to believe** that they are counterfeit is not an offence under s.489C; **mens rea** must be established. ***R***emember the **mens rea rule**.

- **Terrorism link**: the **UAPA** (s.15(1)(a)(iiia)) and **BNS s.113** treat **production, smuggling or circulation of high-quality counterfeit Indian currency** that damages the monetary stability of India as a **terrorist act**. **NIA** investigates **FICN** (fake Indian currency notes) as a scheduled offence.

- **Related laws**: **Reserve Bank of India Act, 1934, s.31** (only the RBI may issue bank notes and currency notes); **Coinage Act, 2011**; **Prevention of Money Laundering Act, 2002** (proceeds of NDPS and counterfeiting offences are **scheduled offences**); **Indian Stamp Act, 1899**.

### 8.5 Counterfeit goods (IP-related offences)

- **Trade Marks Act, 1999**: **falsifying and falsely applying trade marks** (s.103: imprisonment of **six months to three years** and fine of **₹50,000 to ₹2 lakh**), **selling goods** bearing a false trade mark (s.104), **enhanced penalty for repeat offences** (s.105); **Copyright Act, 1957**: **infringement** as an offence (s.63: **six months to three years** and fine **₹50,000 to ₹2 lakh**), **possession of infringing copies** (s.63B); **Designs Act, 2000**; **Geographical Indications of Goods Act, 1999**.

- **Drugs and Cosmetics Act, 1940**: **spurious and adulterated drugs** (s.17B, s.13; punishment from **three years to life**); **Food Safety and Standards Act, 2006**: **unsafe food**.

- **BNS** cheating by personation, forgery and **making false trademarks and property marks** (BNS ss.345-350; IPC 478-489) also address counterfeit goods.

### Memory Capsule: Chapter 8

- **NDPS** = **1985**; **s.37** = **twin bail conditions**; **s.35** = **culpable mental state presumption**; **s.50** = **personal search**; **s.42** = **warrantless search**; **s.52A** = **disposal**; **s.27A** = **financing** (10 to 20 years); **s.31A** = **death** for repeat large-quantity offenders (discretionary after 2014).

- **Quantity bands**: **small** (up to 1 year), **intermediate** (up to 10 years), **commercial** (10 to 20 years, fine ₹1 to 2 lakh).

- **Cases**: *Balbir Singh* and *Baldev Singh* (mandatory compliance, s.42 and s.50), *Noor Aga* (presumptions valid), *Tofan Singh* (NCB confessions inadmissible), *Vijaysinh Jadeja* (s.50), *Dadu* (s.32A), *Mohanlal* (s.52A).

- **Counterfeiting**: **489A-489E** (IPC) now in **BNS Chapter X**; **life or ten years** for counterfeiting; **possession needs knowledge** (*Umashankar*); **high-quality counterfeit currency** is a **terrorist act** (BNS s.113).

## Chapter 9: Offences Against Public Tranquillity

BNS **Chapter XI (ss.189-197; IPC ss.141-160 and 153A, 153B)** deals with **unlawful assembly, rioting, affray and promoting enmity**. Related topics: **public nuisance** (s.270; IPC 268), **offences relating to religion** (ss.298-302; IPC 295-298), **statements conducing to public mischief** (s.353; IPC 505) and **disobedience of public servants' orders** (s.223; IPC 188). The distinctions among unlawful assembly, riot and affray are an **evergreen question**.

### 9.1 Unlawful assembly (s.189; IPC s.141)

An assembly of **five or more persons** is designated an **unlawful assembly** if the **common object** of the persons composing that assembly is:

1.  to **overawe by criminal force, or show of criminal force**, the Central or any State Government or Parliament or the Legislature of any State, or any **public servant** in the exercise of his lawful power;

2.  to **resist the execution of any law**, or of any legal process;

3.  to commit any **mischief or criminal trespass**, or other offence;

4.  by means of **criminal force or show of criminal force**, to **take or obtain possession of property**, or deprive any person of the **enjoyment of a right of way, or of the use of water or other incorporeal right**, or to **enforce any right or supposed right**;

5.  by means of **criminal force or show of criminal force**, to **compel any person to do what he is not legally bound to do**, or to **omit to do what he is legally entitled to do**.

- **Explanation**: an assembly which was **not unlawful when it assembled** may subsequently become an unlawful assembly.

- **Essentials**: (1) **five or more persons**; (2) a **common object** of the listed kind; (3) the common object must be **unlawful**, shared by **all** (or by a number sufficient to make five); (4) it need not be **formed in advance**; it can form **on the spot** (*Lalji v. State of U.P.*, 1989).

- **Mere presence** at the scene is **not enough** (*Masalti v. State of U.P.*, 1965); **active participation or sharing the common object** is required, but **overt act** is not necessary for the liability under s.190.

- **Punishments**: **being a member** of an unlawful assembly (IPC 143): up to **six months** or fine or both; **joining armed with deadly weapon** (IPC 144): up to **two years**; **joining after being commanded to disperse** (IPC 145): up to **two years**; **hiring or engaging persons** for the assembly (s.192; IPC 150): same as a member of the assembly; **knowingly joining or continuing in an assembly of five or more persons after it has been lawfully commanded to disperse** (s.193; IPC 151): up to **six months** or fine or both; **assaulting or obstructing a public servant** when suppressing a riot (s.195; IPC 152): up to **three years** or fine or both.

- **Dispersal**: the **BNSS** (successor to **CrPC ss.129-132**) lays down the procedure for **dispersal of unlawful assemblies by civil force, by armed forces**, and protection for officers who act in good faith. Magistrate and police officers can order dispersal; the **use of force must be the minimum necessary**.

### 9.2 Common object: s.190 (IPC s.149)

- **Liability of every member** of an unlawful assembly for **any offence committed by any member in prosecution of the common object**, or such as the members **knew to be likely** to be committed in prosecution of that object. See **topic 1** for the **comparison with common intention** and the cases (*Masalti*, *Lalji*, *Charan Singh*, *Yeshwant*).

### 9.3 Rioting (s.191; IPC ss.146-148)

- **Definition (s.191(1); IPC s.146)**: whenever **force or violence** is used by an **unlawful assembly**, or by any member thereof, **in prosecution of the common object** of such assembly, every member of such assembly is guilty of the offence of **rioting**.

- **Punishment**: **rioting** (s.191(2); IPC 147): up to **two years** or fine or both; **rioting armed with a deadly weapon** (s.191(3); IPC 148): up to **five years** or fine or both; **rioting by a person armed with a deadly weapon** or **members aggravated** (s.191(4)-(5)).

- **Riot is an unlawful assembly that has used force.** An unlawful assembly may exist **without any violence**; a riot needs **force or violence** (*even slight force*, such as pushing, may suffice).

- **Liability for offences during the riot**: members are liable under s.190 for offences in prosecution of the common object (e.g., murder, mischief by fire).

### 9.4 Affray (s.194; IPC ss.159-160)

- **Definition**: when **two or more persons**, by **fighting in a public place**, **disturb the public peace**, they are said to **commit an affray**.

- **Punishment**: up to **one month**, or fine up to **₹1,000** or both (the BNS raised the fine).

- **Elements**: (1) **two or more persons**; (2) **fighting**; (3) **in a public place**; (4) **disturbance of the public peace** (the **public** nature distinguishes affray from an ordinary assault).

### 9.5 Comparison: unlawful assembly, riot and affray

| Point             | Unlawful assembly (s.189) | Rioting (s.191)                      | Affray (s.194)   |
|-------------------|---------------------------|--------------------------------------|------------------|
| Persons           | **Five or more**          | **Five or more**                     | **Two or more**  |
| Common object     | **Needed** (unlawful)     | **Needed** (unlawful)                | **Not needed**   |
| Force or violence | **Not needed**            | **Must be used**                     | **Fighting**     |
| Place             | Any                       | Any                                  | **Public place** |
| Punishment        | Up to 6 months            | Up to 2 years (5 with deadly weapon) | Up to 1 month    |

### 9.6 Promoting enmity and national integration (ss.196-197; IPC 153A, 153B)

- **Promoting enmity between different groups** (s.196; IPC 153A): **words (spoken or written), signs, visible representations or electronic communication** that **promote or attempt to promote disharmony or feelings of enmity, hatred or ill-will between different religious, racial, language or regional groups or castes or communities**, on grounds of **religion, race, place of birth, residence, language, caste or community**; also **acts prejudicial to the maintenance of harmony** and **organising drills or exercises** that involve criminal force. Punishment: up to **three years**, or fine, or both; **in a place of worship** or during **religious ceremonies**: up to **five years** and fine.

- **Imputations and assertions prejudicial to national integration** (s.197; IPC 153B): e.g., claims that any class of persons cannot bear **true faith and allegiance to the Constitution** or **denying citizens their rights** on grounds of religion, etc. Punishment: up to **three years** or fine or both; **in a place of worship**: up to **five years**.

- **Cases**: *Bilal Ahmed Kaloo v. State of A.P.* (1997) (s.153A requires that **at least two groups** are involved and the **intention** to promote enmity must be shown; the offence cannot be based on a statement against one community alone); *Manzar Sayeed Khan v. State of Maharashtra* (2007) (**mens rea** and **intention to promote enmity** are essential; mere publication of a statement is not enough); *Ramesh v. Union of India* (1988) (Tamil film on Rajiv Gandhi's assassination; test of the **reasonable, strong-minded person**); *Pravasi Bhalai Sangathan v. Union of India* (2014) (hate speech; asked the Law Commission to study); *Amish Devgan v. Union of India* (2020) (hate speech, effect of speech on the **targeted group**; the Court held speech must be judged by its **effect on a reasonable person**); *Patricia Mukhim v. State of Meghalaya* (2021) (**no FIR** under 153A and 505 where the Facebook post sought **justice** and did not promote enmity).

### 9.7 Offences relating to religion (ss.298-302; IPC ss.295-298)

| Offence                                                                                                             | BNS | IPC  | Punishment                            |
|---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------|-----|------|---------------------------------------|
| **Injuring or defiling a place of worship** with intent to insult the religion of any class                         | 298 | 295  | Up to **two years** or fine or both   |
| **Deliberate and malicious acts intended to outrage religious feelings** by insulting religion or religious beliefs | 299 | 295A | Up to **three years** or fine or both |
| **Disturbing a religious assembly**                                                                                 | 300 | 296  | Up to **one year** or fine or both    |
| **Trespassing on burial places**                                                                                    | 301 | 297  | Up to **one year** or fine or both    |
| **Uttering words with deliberate intent to wound religious feelings**                                               | 302 | 298  | Up to **one year** or fine or both    |

- **Ramji Lal Modi v. State of U.P.** (1957): the Supreme Court upheld **s.295A** as a **reasonable restriction** on freedom of speech in the interest of **public order**, because it punishes only **deliberate and malicious** acts aimed at outraging religious feelings, not mere insult. **Mere criticism** or **bona fide** discussion is outside the section. **Shreya Singhal v. Union of India** (2015) is the leading authority on the distinction between **advocacy** and **incitement**.

### 9.8 Statements conducing to public mischief (s.353; IPC s.505)

- Making, publishing or circulating any **statement, rumour or report**: (a) with intent to cause, or likely to cause, any **officer, soldier, sailor or airman** to **mutiny** or otherwise disregard or fail in his duty; (b) with intent to cause, or likely to cause, **fear or alarm to the public**, whereby any person may be induced to commit an **offence against the State or against the public tranquillity**; (c) with intent to **incite, or likely to incite, any class or community** to commit any offence against any other class or community. **Punishment**: up to **three years** or fine or both; **in a place of worship or at a religious assembly**: up to **five years** and fine. The BNS includes **electronic means** and **false or misleading information** that endangers the sovereignty or security of India under s.353 (and related s.152).

### 9.9 Public nuisance and orders to maintain peace

- **Public nuisance (s.270; IPC s.268)**: an act or **illegal omission** which causes any **common injury, danger or annoyance** to the public, or to the people in general who dwell or occupy property in the vicinity, or which **must necessarily cause injury, obstruction, danger or annoyance** to persons who may have occasion to use any **public right**. A common nuisance is **not excused** on the ground that it causes **some convenience or advantage**. **Punishment** (s.292; IPC 290): fine up to **₹1,000** (older: ₹200).

- **BNSS conditional order for removal of nuisance** (CrPC s.133; **BNSS s.152**): the **Magistrate** can issue a conditional order to remove a public nuisance; **Municipal Council, Ratlam v. Vardhichand** (1980) (Justice Krishna Iyer; the **local authority** must perform its statutory duty to remove nuisance; **lack of funds** is no excuse; s.133 is **mandatory** in effect).

- **Orders under BNSS s.163 (CrPC s.144)**: urgent orders in cases of **nuisance or apprehended danger**; **Madhu Limaye v. S.D.M. Monghyr** (1970) (constitutional; reasonable restriction on rights); **Babulal Parate v. State of Maharashtra** (1961); **Anuradha Bhasin v. Union of India** (2020) (**internet shutdown** and s.144 orders must be **necessary and proportionate**, **published** and subject to **judicial review**; indefinite orders are impermissible).

- **Assembly and protest**: **Article 19(1)(b)** (peaceful assembly without arms); **Mazdoor Kisan Shakti Sangathan v. Union of India** (2018) (Jantar Mantar; the Court said protests must be **regulated**, not banned); **Amit Sahni v. Commissioner of Police** (2020) (**Shaheen Bagh**; public spaces cannot be occupied **indefinitely**); **Ramlila Maidan Incident, In Re** (2012) (**right to sleep** and **right to assemble** and police action at night); **Destruction of Public and Private Properties v. State of A.P.** (2009) (guidelines on **compensation** for property damage during protests and the **Prevention of Damage to Public Property Act, 1984**).

- **Disobedience to order duly promulgated by a public servant** (s.223; IPC 188): if it causes **obstruction, annoyance or injury**: up to **six months**; if it causes **danger to human life, health or safety, or a riot or affray**: up to **one year** and fine.

### Memory Capsule: Chapter 9

- **Unlawful assembly**: **five or more**, **common object** (five heads), no violence required.

- **Riot** = unlawful assembly + **force or violence**. **Affray** = **two or more**, **public place**, **fighting**.

- **s.190 BNS (149)** = **vicarious liability** of every member.

- **Promoting enmity** (s.196; 153A): **at least two groups**; **mens rea** needed (*Bilal Ahmed Kaloo*, *Manzar Sayeed Khan*).

- **s.299 BNS (295A)** upheld in *Ramji Lal Modi*.

- **Public nuisance**: **s.270**; **no justification of convenience**; *Ratlam* (duty of local bodies).

- **BNSS s.163** (old s.144): *Madhu Limaye* (valid), *Anuradha Bhasin* (proportionality).

## Chapter 10: Theories and Kinds of Punishment; Compensation to Victims of Crime

Punishment is the **legal consequence of crime** imposed by the State after trial. Questions test (1) the **theories** of punishment and their proponents, (2) the **kinds of punishment in BNS s.4**, (3) the **death penalty** and **life imprisonment** case law, (4) **sentencing, probation and fines**, and (5) **victim compensation** (BNSS ss.395-397, the successor to CrPC ss.357-357C).

### 10.1 Theories of punishment

| Theory                             | Core idea                                                                                                          | Thinkers                                                                             | Key points                                                                                                                                                                            |
|------------------------------------|--------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------|--------------------------------------------------------------------------------------|---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------|
| **Retributive**                    | Punishment is a **just desert** for the wrong done; the offender **deserves** suffering in proportion to the wrong | **Kant, Hegel**; the biblical "eye for an eye" (*lex talionis*)                      | Looks **backward**; **proportionality**; supports the death penalty for the gravest crimes; criticised as **vengeance**                                                               |
| **Deterrent**                      | Punishment must **frighten** the offender (**individual deterrence**) and others (**general deterrence**)          | **Jeremy Bentham, Beccaria, Paley**                                                  | Looks **forward**; assumes **rational choice**; **exemplary** punishment; criticised for **ignoring offenders who are not rational** and for the **disproportionate** punishment risk |
| **Preventive (incapacitative)**    | **Remove** the offender from society so that he **cannot repeat** the crime                                        | **Salmond** (partly)                                                                 | Imprisonment, **death**, **externment**, **preventive detention**; looks to **protection of society**                                                                                 |
| **Reformative (rehabilitative)**   | Treat the offender as a **patient**; aim is **reform** through education and training                              | **Gandhi** ("**hate the sin and not the sinner**"); **Justice Krishna Iyer**         | **Probation, parole, borstal, open prisons, juvenile justice**; **individualised** sentences; criticised as **soft**                                                                  |
| **Expiatory**                      | The offender **atones** for the sin by suffering                                                                   | Religious thought                                                                    | Ancient Hindu law and canonical law                                                                                                                                                   |
| **Restorative**                    | Repair the **harm** to the **victim and community** through dialogue and **compensation**                          | **Howard Zehr**                                                                      | **Victim-offender mediation, plea bargaining, compensation**                                                                                                                          |
| **Utilitarian / Mixed (eclectic)** | Combine **deterrence, prevention and reformation**                                                                 | **Salmond: punishment is the instrument of deterrence, retribution and reformation** | Closest to Indian sentencing practice                                                                                                                                                 |

- **Indian judicial approach**: courts apply a **blend** (*Mohd. Giasuddin v. State of A.P.*, 1977; Justice Krishna Iyer stressed the **reformative** approach and the "**hate the sin, not the sinner**" principle); *Dhananjoy Chatterjee v. State of W.B.* (1994) (**deterrence** and the **society's cry for justice** justified death); *Bachan Singh v. State of Punjab* (1980) (**reformative** and **retributive** aims are balanced; death only in the **rarest of rare** cases); *Soman v. State of Kerala* (2013) (sentencing principles: **proportionality**; the court must consider **aggravating and mitigating** factors).

- **Committee reports**: the **Malimath Committee** (2003) recommended a **sentencing guideline**; **Madhava Menon Committee** (2007) proposed a **National Policy on Criminal Justice**. **India has no statutory sentencing guideline**; discretion is judicial.

### 10.2 Kinds of punishment (BNS s.4; IPC s.53)

1.  **Death**.

2.  **Imprisonment for life**.

3.  **Imprisonment**, of two descriptions: **(i) rigorous** (with **hard labour**) and **(ii) simple**.

4.  **Forfeiture of property**.

5.  **Fine**.

6.  **Community service** (**new**).

- **Other provisions of Chapter II (ss.4-13)**: s.5 **commutation** of sentences (the appropriate Government may commute **death to life imprisonment**, etc., **without the offender's consent**); s.6 **fractions of terms** (**life imprisonment is reckoned as twenty years** for calculating fractions, unless otherwise provided); s.7 the sentence may be **wholly or partly rigorous or simple**; s.8 **amount of fine** and **default imprisonment**; s.9 **limit of punishment** for an offence made up of several offences; s.10 punishment where it is **doubtful** which of several offences was committed; s.11 **solitary confinement**; s.12 **limit of solitary confinement**; s.13 **enhanced punishment** after previous conviction.

- **Life imprisonment**: means **imprisonment for the whole of the remaining natural life** unless **remitted or commuted** by the appropriate Government (*Gopal Vinayak Godse v. State of Maharashtra*, 1961; the **twenty-year** reckoning in s.6 is only for **calculation of fractions**). **Maru Ram v. Union of India** (1981) upheld **CrPC s.433A** (a life convict must serve **fourteen years** before remission; **the BNSS has an equivalent provision**); **Swamy Shraddananda (2) v. State of Karnataka** (2008) (the court may impose a **fixed-term life sentence** (no remission) as an alternative to death); **Union of India v. V. Sriharan @ Murugan** (2016) (Constitution Bench: the **Constitutional Courts** may impose a **fixed term** of life imprisonment beyond **fourteen years**, even **for the whole natural life**; but **no Government remission** in that case). The **BNS** expressly uses "**imprisonment for life, which shall mean the remainder of that person's natural life**" for certain aggravated sexual offences and for **murder by a life convict**.

- **Rigorous imprisonment** includes hard labour (grinding corn, digging, drawing water, wood-cutting); **simple imprisonment** has no hard labour. **Prisoners' rights**: *Sunil Batra v. Delhi Administration* (1978, 1980) (**solitary confinement** and **bar fetters** in jail violate Article 21 unless justified; prisoners retain **fundamental rights**); *Charles Sobraj v. Superintendent, Central Jail, Tihar* (1978); *Prem Shankar Shukla v. Delhi Administration* (1980) (**handcuffing** is **inhuman** unless justified); *Hussainara Khatoon v. State of Bihar* (1979) (**speedy trial** and **legal aid** for undertrials); *Sheela Barse v. State of Maharashtra* (1983) (**women and child** prisoners).

- **Solitary confinement** (s.11-12; IPC 73-74): **not more than three months** in the whole; **not more than fourteen days at a time**, with **intervals** of equal length.

- **Fine**: if **no sum** is expressed, the fine is **unlimited but not excessive** (s.8); **default imprisonment** must not exceed **one-fourth** of the maximum imprisonment for the offence where the offence is punishable with both; fine is **realisable within six years**, and the offender's **death** does not discharge his **property** from liability.

- **Community service (s.4(f))**: defined in BNS s.2 as work the court orders as punishment that **benefits the community**, for which the convict is **not entitled to remuneration**. **Offences with community service** (six): **public servant unlawfully engaging in trade** (s.202), **non-attendance in obedience to an order from a public servant** (s.209), **attempt to commit suicide to compel or restrain a public servant** (s.226), **theft of property valued under ₹5,000 by a first-time convict who returns the property** (s.303(2)), **misconduct in public by a drunken person** (s.355), and **defamation** (s.356(2)).

- **Mandatory minimum punishments**: introduced for several offences (e.g., rape, dowry death, organised crime). **Forfeiture and attachment** of proceeds of crime: **BNSS s.107** (new, attachment, forfeiture or restoration of property).

### 10.3 Death penalty

- **Offences punishable with death in the BNS** include **murder (s.103)**, **murder by a life convict (s.104)**, **mob lynching (s.103(2))**, **waging war (s.147)**, **abetment of mutiny** and certain **military** offences, **rape of a girl under 12 (s.65(2))**, **rape causing death or persistent vegetative state (s.66)**, **gang rape of a woman under 18 (s.70(2))**, **repeat rape offenders (s.71)**, **kidnapping for ransom** (s.140(2): death or life), **dacoity with murder (s.310(3))**, **terrorist act causing death (s.113)**, **organised crime causing death (s.111)**, and **false evidence leading to the conviction of an innocent person for a capital offence**. Special laws include **POCSO** (aggravated penetrative sexual assault), **NDPS s.31A** and **UAPA s.16**.

- **Landmark case law**:

  - **Jagmohan Singh v. State of U.P.** (1973): upheld the death penalty; sentencing discretion is **guided** by the procedure of a trial.

  - **Rajendra Prasad v. State of U.P.** (1979): Justice Krishna Iyer limited death to cases of **extraordinary** nature (**extreme social danger**); **overruled** in *Bachan Singh*.

  - **Bachan Singh v. State of Punjab** (1980): **Constitution Bench (4:1; Justice Bhagwati dissenting)** upheld the **constitutional validity** of the death penalty (Article 21 and s.302 IPC); death can be imposed only in the **"rarest of rare" cases** when the **alternative of life imprisonment is unquestionably foreclosed**; the court must weigh **aggravating and mitigating circumstances**; **life imprisonment is the rule and death the exception**; **CrPC s.354(3)** requires **special reasons** for death.

  - **Machhi Singh v. State of Punjab** (1983): five categories to apply the rarest-of-rare test: **manner of commission**, **motive**, **anti-social or socially abhorrent nature**, **magnitude**, **personality of the victim**; and a **balance sheet** of aggravating and mitigating factors.

  - **Mithu v. State of Punjab** (1983): **mandatory death** under IPC s.303 is **unconstitutional**.

  - **Santosh Kumar Satishbhushan Bariyar v. State of Maharashtra** (2009): **crime test** and **criminal test**; the court must look at the **offender** as well as the **offence**; **not mechanical** application of rarest-of-rare.

  - **Deena v. Union of India** (1983): **hanging** is **not** a cruel, inhuman or degrading method (Article 21 upheld); **T.V. Vatheeswaran v. State of T.N.** (1983) and **Triveniben v. State of Gujarat** (1989): **inordinate delay** in execution; delay **alone** does not automatically entitle commutation unless **unexplained and excessive**.

  - **Shatrughan Chauhan v. Union of India** (2014): **undue and inordinate delay in deciding mercy petitions** is a ground for **commutation**; guidelines for the **protection of death row convicts** (e.g., **mental illness**, **solitary confinement** barred, **legal aid**). **Mohd. Arif @ Ashfaq v. Registrar, Supreme Court** (2014): **review petitions** in death sentences must be heard **in open court** by a bench of at least **three judges**. **Shabnam v. Union of India** (2015): **no execution** without **sufficient notice** of 14 days after the mercy decision to allow remedies.

  - **Mukesh v. State (NCT of Delhi)** (2017): **Nirbhaya**; death confirmed. **Yakub Memon v. State of Maharashtra** (2015): **curative** petition heard at night before execution.

  - **Manoj v. State of M.P.** (2022): the court must examine **mitigating circumstances** (psychological evaluation, jail conduct, probation report) **before** imposing death; a **Constitution Bench** reference was made in **2022** to **frame guidelines** on mitigating factors.

- **Procedure**: **pre-sentence hearing** (CrPC s.235(2); BNSS s.392(2)); **confirmation** of death sentence by the **High Court** (the BNSS chapter on confirmation, corresponding to CrPC ss.366-371); **appeal** to the Supreme Court (**Article 134**); **mercy petition** to the **Governor** (**Article 161**) and **President** (**Article 72**; **Epuru Sudhakar v. Government of A.P.**, 2006: judicial review of mercy decisions is available on **limited grounds**).

- **Abolition debate**: **Law Commission 262nd Report (2015)** recommended **abolition for all crimes except terrorism-related offences**; **Mithu** and **Bachan Singh** remain the core precedents.

### 10.4 Sentencing, probation and alternatives

- **Probation of Offenders Act, 1958**: courts may release **first-time offenders** on **probation of good conduct** (s.3: **release after admonition for certain property offences and offences punishable with up to two years; s.4: release on probation of good conduct for offences not punishable with death or life**; s.6: **restrictions on imprisonment of offenders under 21**). **Cases**: *Ratan Lal v. State of Punjab* (1964) (probation to a **young offender**; the Act is **beneficial** and **retrospective**); *Jugal Kishore Prasad v. State of Bihar* (1972); *Chhanni v. State of U.P.* (2006) (probation to **older offenders**; reasons must be recorded if not applied).

- **Sentencing principles**: **proportionality**, **individualisation**, **consideration of both the crime and the criminal**. **Aggravating** factors: **premeditation**, **cruelty**, **vulnerable victim**, **abuse of position of trust**, **repeat offence**. **Mitigating** factors: **young age**, **no criminal history**, **remorse**, **provocation**, **mental condition**, **probability of reform**.

- **Plea bargaining** (BNSS Chapter XXIII; CrPC Chapter XXIA introduced in **2005**): available for offences with punishment **up to seven years**; **not for** offences against **women or children below 14**, **socio-economic** offences, or offences **affecting the socio-economic condition of the country**; the **court** reduces the sentence (up to **one-fourth** of the minimum or one-half in some cases, depending on the stage).

- **Compounding** of offences (BNSS s.359; CrPC s.320): **with or without court permission** for the listed offences; **non-compoundable** serious offences.

- **Juvenile justice**: **Juvenile Justice (Care and Protection of Children) Act, 2015**; **no death or life imprisonment** for **children in conflict with law**; the **maximum stay** in a special home is **three years**; **heinous offences** by **16-18 year-olds** may be treated as **adults**.

- **Parole and furlough**: **parole** (temporary release for a specific purpose; **not a right**), **furlough** (a **reward** for good conduct); **State prison rules**; **Maru Ram** and **Sunil Batra** govern **remission**.

### 10.5 Compensation to victims of crime

- **Victimology**: the **UN Declaration of Basic Principles of Justice for Victims of Crime and Abuse of Power (1985)**; Indian reforms followed the **Malimath Committee (2003)** and the **Law Commission 154th Report (1996)**.

- **Statutory provisions** (BNSS; old CrPC in brackets):

  - **s.395 (CrPC 357)**: **order to pay compensation** out of the **fine** or in addition to the fine; **compensation** to the victim for the **loss or injury** and **costs**; courts may order compensation **even when fine is not part of the sentence** (357(3)). *Hari Singh v. Sukhbir Singh* (1988) (courts should **liberally use** the power); *Ankush Shivaji Gaikwad v. State of Maharashtra* (2013) (the court has a **duty** to consider compensation in **every case**; the power is **mandatory** to consider); *Suresh v. State of Haryana* (2015) (the power is **not ancillary** to a sentence).

  - **s.396 (CrPC 357A)**: **Victim Compensation Scheme** by every State, in coordination with the **Central Government**; the **District Legal Services Authority (DLSA)** or State Legal Services Authority decides the **quantum** where the **court recommends** compensation, **even when the offender is not traced or identified** or the **trial ends in acquittal or discharge**.

  - **ss.397 (CrPC 357B, 357C)**: compensation for **acid attack and sexual offences** is **in addition** to fines (e.g., under s.124 (acid), s.65 (rape under 16), s.70 (gang rape)); **free medical treatment** (first-aid, diagnosis, hospitalisation and psychological counselling) in **all hospitals**, public or private, for **victims of acid attack and rape**.

  - **Right of appeal by the victim** (proviso to CrPC s.372; **BNSS s.413**): *Satya Pal Singh v. State of M.P.* (2015).

  - **Victim participation** and **victim impact statements** in sentencing and **victim's right to be heard** (BNSS ss.360, 230 etc.).

- **Constitutional compensation (public law remedy) under Articles 32 and 226**: *Rudul Sah v. State of Bihar* (1983) (compensation for **illegal detention** after acquittal); *Bhim Singh v. State of J&K* (1985); *Nilabati Behera v. State of Orissa* (1993) (**custodial death**; compensation is **public law** and separate from private-law tort remedies); *D.K. Basu v. State of W.B.* (1997) (**arrest and detention guidelines**); *Delhi Domestic Working Women's Forum v. Union of India* (1995) (**Criminal Injuries Compensation Board** for **rape victims**; **legal representation**); *Bodhisattwa Gautam v. Subhra Chakraborty* (1996) (**interim compensation** to rape victims); *Chairman, Railway Board v. Chandrima Das* (2000) (compensation to a **foreign national** raped by railway employees); *Laxmi v. Union of India* (2014) and *Parivartan Kendra v. Union of India* (2015) (**acid attack**: minimum **₹3 lakh**; free treatment); *Nipun Saxena v. Union of India* (2018) (a **Model Victim Compensation Scheme** for **sexual offences** and **acid attack**; **NALSA's 2018 scheme** for women victims of sexual assault).

- **Special statutes with compensation**: **Scheduled Castes and Scheduled Tribes (Prevention of Atrocities) Act, 1989**; **Protection of Women from Domestic Violence Act, 2005** (monetary relief); **Motor Vehicles Act, 1988** (compensation, **no-fault liability**); **POCSO Rules, 2020** (interim compensation).

### Memory Capsule: Chapter 10

- **Theories**: retributive (Kant, Hegel), deterrent (Bentham, Beccaria), preventive, **reformative (Gandhi; Krishna Iyer)**, restorative, **mixed (Salmond)**.

- **BNS s.4** has **six punishments**: death, life, imprisonment (rigorous/simple), forfeiture, fine, **community service** (new).

- **Life imprisonment** = **natural life** (*Godse*); **fractions** reckoned at **twenty years** (s.6); **14 years** minimum before remission (*Maru Ram*; s.433A CrPC).

- **Death**: *Jagmohan* (valid), *Rajendra Prasad*, **Bachan Singh (rarest of rare; 1980)**, **Machhi Singh (five categories)**, **Mithu (mandatory death invalid)**, **Shatrughan Chauhan (delay)**, **Manoj (mitigation)**.

- **Victim compensation**: **BNSS ss.395-397** (CrPC 357, 357A, 357B, 357C); **DLSA** decides; cases: *Rudul Sah*, *Nilabati Behera*, *Delhi Domestic Working Women's Forum*, *Ankush Gaikwad*, *Laxmi*.

### 11.2 Commonly confused pairs

- **Common intention** (2+, s.3(5)) vs **common object** (5+, s.190).

- **Culpable homicide** ("likely") vs **murder** ("must in all probability"; "sufficient in the ordinary course of nature").

- **Abetment** (instigation, conspiracy with act, aid) vs **criminal conspiracy** (agreement only).

- **Preparation** (not punishable) vs **attempt** (punishable).

- **Kidnapping** (minor, unsound mind, or conveying out of India) vs **abduction** (force or deceit; any person).

- **Wrongful restraint** (one direction) vs **wrongful confinement** (all directions).

- **Theft** (taking) vs **extortion** (delivery by fear) vs **robbery** (instant fear or hurt) vs **dacoity** (five or more).

- **Criminal misappropriation** (innocent possession) vs **criminal breach of trust** (entrustment).

- **Cheating** (deception from start) vs **breach of contract**.

- **Unlawful assembly** (no force) vs **riot** (force) vs **affray** (two or more, public place, fighting).

- **Private defence of body** (death for six grave categories) vs **of property** (death for robbery, night house-breaking, arson of dwelling, etc.).

- **Insanity** (cognitive test) vs **irresistible impulse** (not recognised in India).

- **Voluntary intoxication** (no defence, intent presumed) vs **involuntary intoxication** (defence).

### 11.3 What is new or changed in the BNS (frequent MCQ source)

- **New offences**: organised crime (s.111), petty organised crime (s.112), terrorist act (s.113), mob lynching (s.103(2)), snatching (s.304), hit and run (s.106(2)), deceitful sexual intercourse (s.69), acts endangering sovereignty, unity and integrity (s.152), hiring a child to commit an offence (s.95), causing grievous hurt resulting in a persistent vegetative state (s.117(3)).

- **Dropped**: sedition (IPC 124A), adultery (IPC 497), IPC 377, attempt to commit suicide (IPC 309, replaced by narrow s.226).

- **New punishment**: **community service** (six offences).

- **Structural change**: 511 sections to **358**; **common intention** moved into s.3(5); **general exceptions** now ss.14-44; **terrorism and organised crime** inside ordinary penal law.

- **Higher fines and minimum punishments**; **natural-life imprisonment** wording; **gang rape of a woman under 18** now death or natural-life imprisonment.

- **Procedure**: BNSS (CrPC), BSA (Evidence Act), in force from **1 July 2024**.

### 11.4 Number facts to memorise

- **7 and 12**: age thresholds for immunity (s.20-21).

- **18**: age of valid consent in s.25 and for rape; age for kidnapping: **16 (male), 18 (female)**.

- **5 or more**: unlawful assembly, riot, dacoity; **2 or more**: common intention, conspiracy, affray.

- **7 years**: dowry death minimum and period after marriage; **20 days**: grievous hurt test.

- **10 years**: abetment of suicide maximum; rape minimum; attempt to murder maximum; **20 years**: minimum for rape of a girl under 16 and for gang rape.

- **3 years**: common maximum for theft; **5,000 rupees**: community service threshold for theft; **1 to 7 years**: petty organised crime.

- **10 to 20 years**: NDPS commercial quantity; **fine 1 to 2 lakh**.

- **14 years**: remission rule for life convicts (CrPC 433A).

### 11.5 Answering technique for UGC NET statement MCQs

- **Read qualifiers**: words like "only", "always", "must", "not necessarily" often make a statement wrong.

- **Anchor on the section number first** (BNS vs IPC) and check whether the option mixes them.

- **For case-law questions**, link each case to its **single** key principle and year; examiners often swap similar cases.

- **For punishment questions**, recall **ladders** (e.g., rape 10-life; under 16: 20-life; under 12: 20-life or death).

- **When two statements look correct, check the exceptions** (e.g., private defence cannot extend to death in property cases unless the listed offences exist).

- **Update check**: the BNS, BNSS and BSA are new; when an option cites an IPC section, the examiner may still expect the IPC answer, so **study both numbers**.
