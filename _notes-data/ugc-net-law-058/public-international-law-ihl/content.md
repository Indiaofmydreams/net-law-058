## Chapter 1: Sources of International Law

### 1.1 Nature and Basics

Public International Law (PIL) is the body of rules governing relations between States and other international legal persons (international organisations, and to a limited extent individuals). Its rules are created mainly by consent of States, there is no world legislature, and enforcement rests largely on reciprocity, countermeasures and institutions such as the UN Security Council.

**Schools of thought:**

- **Naturalists** (Grotius, Vattel, Pufendorf): law derives from reason and natural law.

- **Positivists** (Bynkershoek, Oppenheim, Anzilotti): law derives from State consent (treaties and custom).

- **Austin:** international law is only ‘positive international morality’ since there is no sovereign command – the classic sceptic view. **Hart** answered that it is a primitive legal system lacking secondary rules.

- **Grotius** is the ‘Father of International Law’ (*De Jure Belli ac Pacis*, 1625).

**Subjects of international law:** States (primary), international organisations (*Reparation for Injuries* AO, 1949 – the UN has objective international personality), individuals (limited – human rights, ICC liability), and sui generis entities such as the ICRC, insurgents and national liberation movements.

**States – Montevideo Convention 1933, Art 1:** (a) permanent population, (b) defined territory, (c) government, (d) capacity to enter into relations with other States.

### 1.2 Article 38(1) of the Statute of the International Court of Justice (ICJ)

Article 38 is the most tested provision of the unit. It directs the Court to apply:

| Clause   | Source                                                                                                                    | Nature                           |
|----------|---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------|----------------------------------|
| 38(1)(a) | International conventions (treaties), general or particular, establishing rules expressly recognised by contesting States | Primary                          |
| 38(1)(b) | International custom, as evidence of a general practice accepted as law                                                   | Primary                          |
| 38(1)(c) | General principles of law recognised by civilised nations                                                                 | Primary                          |
| 38(1)(d) | Judicial decisions and teachings of the most highly qualified publicists – ‘subsidiary means’, subject to Article 59      | Subsidiary                       |
| 38(2)    | *Ex aequo et bono* – only if parties agree                                                                                | Not a source; decision on equity |

**Points to remember:**

- Article 38 does not create a formal hierarchy among (a), (b), (c), but in practice a treaty between the parties prevails as *lex specialis*.

- **Article 59:** the Court’s decision has no binding force except between the parties and in respect of that particular case – no formal *stare decisis*.

- The list is not exhaustive: unilateral acts, resolutions of international organisations and soft law are additional material.

- The Statute of the ICJ is annexed to the UN Charter; Article 92 of the Charter makes the ICJ the principal judicial organ.

### 1.3 Treaties

**Vienna Convention on the Law of Treaties (VCLT) 1969:** adopted 23 May 1969, in force 27 January 1980. Applies to written treaties between States; not retroactive (Art 4). The 1986 Vienna Convention covers treaties involving international organisations. India is **not** a party to VCLT 1969 but accepts most of it as customary law.

**Key provisions:**

- **Art 2(1)(a):** ‘treaty’ = international agreement concluded between States in written form and governed by international law, whatever its particular designation.

- **Art 11:** consent to be bound may be expressed by signature, exchange of instruments, ratification, acceptance, approval or accession.

- **Art 18:** a State that has signed must refrain from acts that defeat the object and purpose of the treaty before entry into force.

- **Arts 19–23: Reservations.** Unilateral statement excluding or modifying legal effect of provisions. Not allowed if prohibited by the treaty or incompatible with its object and purpose. *Reservations to the Genocide Convention* AO (1951) introduced the **‘compatibility test’** replacing the old unanimity rule.

- **Art 26: *Pacta sunt servanda*** – every treaty in force is binding and must be performed in good faith.

- **Art 27:** internal law cannot be invoked as justification for failure to perform a treaty.

- **Arts 31–33: Interpretation** – good faith, ordinary meaning, context, object and purpose; supplementary means (travaux préparatoires) under Art 32.

- **Art 46:** a State cannot invoke violation of internal law on competence to conclude treaties unless the violation was manifest and concerned a rule of fundamental importance.

- **Arts 46–53: Invalidity** – error, fraud, corruption, coercion of a representative, coercion of a State by threat or use of force (Art 52), conflict with a peremptory norm (**Art 53, jus cogens**).

- **Art 60:** termination or suspension on material breach.

- **Art 61:** supervening impossibility of performance.

- **Art 62:** fundamental change of circumstances (*rebus sic stantibus*) – cannot be invoked for boundary treaties; narrowly applied in *Gabčíkovo-Nagymaros* (Hungary/Slovakia, 1997).

- **Art 64:** emergence of a new peremptory norm terminates conflicting treaties.

**Other treaty concepts:** bilateral/multilateral, law-making (*traités-lois*) vs contract treaties (*traités-contrats*), accession, protocol, optional protocol, depositary. **Treaty-making in India:** executive power under Arts 73 and 253; Parliament’s power to legislate to implement treaties (Art 253; Entry 14, List I); see Article 51(c) DPSP.

### 1.4 Custom

**Elements (two-element test):**

1.  **State practice (objective element)** – extensive, representative, consistent and virtually uniform, including that of specially affected States.

2.  ***Opinio juris sive necessitatis* (subjective element)** – belief that the practice is required by law.

**Cases:**

- ***Lotus* (France v Turkey, PCIJ 1927):** restrictions on sovereignty are not presumed; States have residual freedom unless a prohibitive rule exists; custom requires proof of *opinio juris*, not mere abstention.

- ***Asylum* (Colombia v Peru, ICJ 1950):** regional/local custom must be proved as constant and uniform usage accepted as law; Colombia failed to prove it.

- ***Right of Passage over Indian Territory* (Portugal v India, ICJ 1960):** **bilateral local custom** between two States is possible – Portugal’s right of passage for private persons and goods upheld, not for armed forces. (India-linked – high probability.)

- ***Anglo-Norwegian Fisheries* (UK v Norway, 1951):** **persistent objector** rule – a State that consistently objects while the custom is emerging is not bound; also straight baselines.

- ***North Sea Continental Shelf* (Germany v Denmark/Netherlands, 1969):** a treaty provision can generate custom if it is of fundamentally norm-creating character, but equidistance (Art 6, 1958 Convention) did not become custom; **no minimum duration** required, provided practice is extensive and virtually uniform.

- ***Nicaragua v USA* (1986):** non-use of force and non-intervention are customary even where treaty norms exist; treaty and custom co-exist; UNGA resolutions can evidence *opinio juris*.

- ***Jurisdictional Immunities of the State* (Germany v Italy, 2012):** State immunity as customary norm; Italian courts could not deny immunity for acts *jure imperii* even for war crimes.

- ***Nuclear Weapons* AO (1996):** UNGA resolutions may provide evidence of *opinio juris* but cannot by themselves create custom.

**Instant/modern custom:** Bin Cheng’s concept (outer space law). **Persistent objector** does not apply to *jus cogens*.

### 1.5 General Principles of Law

Principles common to major legal systems, applied to fill gaps (*non liquet*): good faith, *res judicata*, estoppel, equity, abuse of rights, no one may profit from own wrong, obligation to make reparation (*Chorzów Factory*, PCIJ 1928), limitation, due process.

### 1.6 Subsidiary Means and Other Sources

- **Judicial decisions:** ICJ, PCIJ, arbitral awards, ITLOS, domestic courts (*Pinochet*, *Filártiga*).

- **Teachings of publicists:** ILC reports, Institut de Droit International, scholars.

- **Unilateral acts:** *Nuclear Tests* (Australia/NZ v France, 1974) – public unilateral declarations made with intent to be bound create legal obligations. *Eastern Greenland* (1933): Ihlen Declaration binding on Norway.

- **Resolutions of UNGA:** generally recommendatory (Arts 10–14 Charter); declarations like the UDHR (1948) have become evidence of custom. UNSC decisions under Chapter VII are binding (Art 25, Art 48).

- **Soft law:** guidelines, codes (e.g., Rio Declaration, Guiding Principles on IDPs).

- **Jus cogens (peremptory norms):** Art 53 VCLT – accepted by the international community of States as a whole as norms from which no derogation is permitted. Examples: prohibition of aggression, genocide, slavery, torture, racial discrimination, self-determination, core IHL. **Erga omnes** obligations – *Barcelona Traction* (Belgium v Spain, 1970, *obiter*); *East Timor* (Portugal v Australia, 1995).

### 1.7 International Law and Municipal Law

- **Dualism** (Triepel, Anzilotti): two separate legal systems; treaties need transformation by legislation. **Monism** (Kelsen, Lauterpacht): one legal order; international law supreme. **UK:** custom is part of common law (incorporation); treaties need legislation (transformation). **USA:** treaties can be self-executing (Supremacy Clause).

- **India:** Art 51 (promotion of international peace and security, respect for international law and treaty obligations); Art 253; Art 73; Entry 14 List I. India follows a **dualist** approach for treaties – Parliament must legislate (*Maganbhai Ishwarbhai Patel v UOI*, 1969).

- ***Gramophone Company of India v Birendra Bahadur Pandey* (1984):** rules of customary international law not contrary to municipal law are deemed part of Indian law; courts should construe statutes to avoid conflict with international law.

- ***Vishaka v State of Rajasthan* (1997):** international conventions (CEDAW) read into Articles 14, 15, 19, 21 to fill a legislative vacuum, provided they do not contradict domestic law.

- ***Jolly Verghese v Bank of Cochin* (1980), *PUCL v UOI* (1997), *NALSA v UOI* (2014), *Puttaswamy* (2017)** – courts use international instruments as interpretive aids.

- ***Berubari Union* (1960):** cession of territory requires constitutional amendment under Art 368, not Art 3.

## Chapter 2: Recognition of States and Governments

### 2.1 Concept

Recognition is the formal or implied acknowledgement by one State of the existence of another State, a government, or a situation (belligerency, insurgency, territorial change), with willingness to treat it as having the legal consequences of that status. It is **political in motive but legal in effect**.

### 2.2 Theories

| Theory                        | Core idea                                                                                         | Proponents                                                                                 |
|-------------------------------|---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------|--------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------|
| **Constitutive**              | Recognition creates statehood; an entity becomes an international person only through recognition | Hegel, Oppenheim, Anzilotti; Lauterpacht (qualified – duty to recognise once criteria met) |
| **Declaratory (evidentiary)** | Statehood exists once Montevideo criteria are fulfilled; recognition merely acknowledges it       | Hall, Brierly, Montevideo Convention Art 3, OAS Charter                                    |

- **Montevideo Art 3:** ‘The political existence of the State is independent of recognition by the other States.’

- Modern practice favours the declaratory theory with elements of collective legitimisation (e.g., UN admission as strong evidence).

- **Problem with the constitutive theory:** a State recognised by some but not others – is it a State or not?

### 2.3 Types and Forms

- **De facto vs de jure:** de facto = provisional, based on effective control but doubts about permanence; de jure = full, final recognition. *Luther v Sagor* (1921, UK) – Soviet government.

- **Express vs implied** (e.g., exchange of ambassadors, bilateral treaties; **mere participation in a multilateral conference or UN voting does not imply recognition**).

- **Individual vs collective.**

- **Conditional recognition** – e.g., European Community Guidelines 1991 and Badinter Commission on Yugoslavia.

- **Recognition of belligerency** – civil war with control of territory; **insurgency** is a lesser status.

- **Retroactive effect:** de jure recognition relates back to the date of de facto recognition.

- **Withdrawal of recognition** is mainly possible for de facto governments.

### 2.4 Doctrines

- **Tobar Doctrine (1907):** refuse recognition to governments that come to power by unconstitutional means.

- **Estrada Doctrine (1930, Mexico):** no declarations of recognition of governments; merely continue or withdraw diplomatic agents.

- **Stimson Doctrine (1932):** non-recognition of territorial changes obtained by force (Japan in Manchuria). Reflected in the Friendly Relations Declaration (1970) and the *Wall* AO (2004).

- **Hallstein Doctrine (West Germany, 1955):** no relations with States recognising East Germany.

- **Wilson Doctrine:** recognition on basis of constitutional legitimacy.

- **Collective non-recognition:** UNSC Res 276 (1970) on Namibia; Res 541 (1983) on Northern Cyprus (recognised only by Turkey); Southern Rhodesia (Res 216, 217 of 1965).

### 2.5 Recognition of Governments

Tests: effective control, popular acquiescence, willingness to observe international obligations. *Tinoco Arbitration* (Great Britain v Costa Rica, 1923) – non-recognition by Britain did not defeat the *de facto* effectiveness of the Tinoco government. The UK stopped formally recognising governments in 1980. **Legal effects** include sovereign immunity, standing in domestic courts, title to State property and diplomatic privileges.

### 2.6 Contemporary Issues

- **Kosovo:** declared independence 17 Feb 2008; ICJ AO (22 July 2010) – declaration of independence did not violate general international law or UNSC Res 1244.

- **Palestine:** UNGA Resolution 67/19 (29 Nov 2012) granted ‘non-member observer State’ status; enhanced rights in May 2024 (UNGA ES-10/23) while full UN membership was blocked by a US veto in UNSC (April 2024). Spain, Ireland and Norway recognised Palestine in 2024; in September 2025 a further group (including UK, France, Canada, Australia, Portugal) recognised it. India recognised Palestine in 1988.

- **Taiwan, Western Sahara, Northern Cyprus, Abkhazia/South Ossetia, Crimea:** classic examples of limited recognition.

- **Taliban government (Afghanistan):** de facto control since Aug 2021; Russia formally recognised it in July 2025 – a recent ‘recognition of government’ example.

- **Self-determination and secession:** *Western Sahara* AO (1975); *Quebec Secession Reference* (Canada, 1998); *Chagos Archipelago* AO (2019) – UK’s continued administration unlawful; UK–Mauritius treaty (2025) on sovereignty with Diego Garcia base arrangements.

### 2.7 State Succession (linked to recognition)

- Types: universal vs partial; merger, dissolution, secession, cession, decolonisation (‘newly independent State’ – clean slate).

- **Vienna Convention on Succession of States in respect of Treaties 1978** (in force 1996) and **Vienna Convention on Succession of States in respect of State Property, Archives and Debts 1983** (not in force).

- **Continuity vs succession:** India was treated as the continuing State of British India (retained UN membership); Pakistan was a new State. Russia continued the USSR’s UN seat in 1991.

- **Clean slate** doctrine (Nyerere’s ‘Tanganyika formula’); devolution agreements.

## Chapter 3: Nationality and Refugees

### 3.1 Nationality: Concept

Nationality is the legal bond between an individual and a State, entailing reciprocal rights and duties and enabling the State to exercise diplomatic protection. Nationality is **within domestic jurisdiction** (*Nationality Decrees in Tunis and Morocco*, PCIJ 1923), but international law limits its effects. **Hague Convention 1930, Art 1:** each State determines under its own law who are its nationals; other States recognise this only if consistent with international conventions, custom and generally recognised principles.

**Nottebohm Case (Liechtenstein v Guatemala, ICJ 1955):** Nottebohm, a German national, naturalised in Liechtenstein shortly before WWII. ICJ held nationality must rest on a **‘genuine link’** (a real connection of existence, interests and sentiments); Guatemala was not obliged to recognise Liechtenstein’s claim. The test is applied mainly to diplomatic protection, not nationality per se.

### 3.2 Modes of Acquisition and Loss

1.  **Birth:** *jus soli* (place of birth – USA, Canada); *jus sanguinis* (descent – Germany; India for children of Indian citizens abroad).

2.  **Naturalisation** (residence, marriage etc.).

3.  **Registration and option.**

4.  **Subjugation and cession (collective naturalisation),** annexation, restoration.

5.  **Legitimation, adoption, marriage.**

**Loss:** renunciation, deprivation, long residence abroad, acquiring another nationality (India – Citizenship Act 1955, s. 9), release, substitution.

### 3.3 Dual Nationality and Statelessness

- **Dual nationality:** *Canevaro Case* (Italy v Peru, PCA 1912); *Mergé Case* (1955) – effective nationality principle. Hague Convention 1930, Arts 3–5. **India does not permit dual citizenship** (OCI is not citizenship).

- **Statelessness:** 1954 Convention relating to the Status of Stateless Persons; 1961 Convention on the Reduction of Statelessness; UDHR Art 15 (‘Everyone has the right to a nationality’); ICCPR Art 24(3). India is not party to either statelessness convention.

- **Women’s nationality:** 1957 Convention on Nationality of Married Women; CEDAW Art 9.

### 3.4 Corporate Nationality and Diplomatic Protection

- **Barcelona Traction (Belgium v Spain, 1970):** the State of incorporation (not of shareholders) has standing to protect a company; exceptions limited. *ELSI* (USA v Italy, 1989); *Ahmadou Sadio Diallo* (Guinea v DRC, 2010) – protection of shareholders’ direct rights.

- **Exhaustion of local remedies** (*Interhandel*) is a precondition; **continuous nationality** rule. ILC Draft Articles on Diplomatic Protection 2006.

- *Mavrommatis Palestine Concessions* (1924): the State taking up a national’s case asserts its own right.

- **Consular access:** *LaGrand* (2001), *Avena* (2004), ***Kulbhushan Jadhav* (India v Pakistan, ICJ 2019)** – Pakistan breached Art 36 VCCR; ordered effective review and reconsideration.

### 3.5 Indian Perspective

- Constitution Arts 5–11; Citizenship Act 1955 (birth, descent, registration, naturalisation, incorporation of territory); Citizenship (Amendment) Act 2019 and Rules 2024; Section 6A (Assam Accord) upheld by the Supreme Court in October 2024 (4:1).

### 3.6 Refugees: Definition and Convention Framework

**1951 Convention relating to the Status of Refugees (in force 22 April 1954) and 1967 Protocol:**

- **Art 1A(2):** a person who, owing to well-founded fear of being persecuted for reasons of **race, religion, nationality, membership of a particular social group or political opinion**, is outside his country of nationality and unable or unwilling to avail himself of its protection.

- The 1951 Convention was limited to events before 1 January 1951 (with an optional geographical limitation to Europe); **the 1967 Protocol removed both limits.**

- **Exclusion (Art 1F):** crimes against peace, war crimes, crimes against humanity; serious non-political crime; acts contrary to UN purposes. **Cessation (Art 1C).** **Art 1D:** Palestinian refugees receiving UNRWA assistance.

- **Art 31:** no penalties for illegal entry if coming directly from a territory of threat and presenting promptly to authorities.

- **Art 33: *Non-refoulement*** – no Contracting State shall expel or return a refugee to frontiers of territories where life or freedom would be threatened. **Art 33(2):** exception for refugees who are a danger to security or convicted of a particularly serious crime. Non-refoulement is **customary international law** and by many regarded as *jus cogens*; also found in CAT Art 3 and ECHR Art 3 (*Soering*, *Chahal*).

- **Regional instruments:** **OAU Convention 1969** (broader – includes external aggression, occupation, foreign domination, events seriously disturbing public order); **Cartagena Declaration 1984** (Latin America); **Bangkok Principles 1966** (AALCO).

- **UNHCR:** established by UNGA Res 428(V) of 14 December 1950; began work on 1 January 1951; HQ Geneva; Nobel Peace Prize 1954 and 1981. **UNRWA** (UNGA Res 302(IV), 1949) serves Palestinian refugees.

- **Global Compact on Refugees (2018)** – non-binding; **Global Compact for Safe, Orderly and Regular Migration (2018)**; **New York Declaration (2016)**; Global Refugee Forum (2019, December 2023).

- **Internally Displaced Persons (IDPs):** not refugees; Guiding Principles on Internal Displacement (1998); **Kampala Convention 2009** (AU – first binding IDP treaty).

- **Historic:** Nansen Passport (1922), League High Commissioner Fridtjof Nansen, IRO (1946–52).

### 3.7 India and Refugees

- India is **not a party** to the 1951 Convention or 1967 Protocol and has no national refugee law; it relies on the Foreigners Act 1946, Registration of Foreigners Act 1939, Passport (Entry into India) Act 1920 and executive discretion. India is a member of UNHCR’s **Executive Committee** and has hosted Tibetans (1959), Bangladeshi refugees (1971), Sri Lankan Tamils, Afghans, Chakmas and Rohingya.

- ***NHRC v State of Arunachal Pradesh* (1996):** Chakma refugees – Article 21 protects all persons, including foreigners.

- ***Ktaer Abbas Habib Al Qutaifi v UOI* (Gujarat HC, 1999)** – non-refoulement implicit in Article 21; ***Zothansangpuii v State of Manipur* (Gauhati HC, 2012)**.

- ***Mohd Salimullah v UOI* (2021)** – Supreme Court declined to stop deportation of Rohingya; ***Hans Muller of Nuremberg v Superintendent, Presidency Jail* (1955)** – the Government’s power to expel foreigners is absolute under the Foreigners Act.

- Recent: Citizenship Amendment Act 2019 (religion-based criteria; challenged under Art 14); the **Immigration and Foreigners Act 2025** replaced the 1946 Act and related laws.

- **Refugee vs asylum seeker vs migrant:** a refugee has been recognised; an asylum seeker awaits determination; economic migrants fall outside the 1951 definition.

## Chapter 4: Extradition and Asylum

### 4.1 Extradition: Meaning and Basis

Extradition is the surrender of an accused or convicted person by the State where he is found to the State that seeks to prosecute or punish him. It is **not an obligation under general international law**; it depends on **treaty, reciprocity or domestic law**. *Aut dedere aut judicare* (extradite or prosecute) is a treaty-based obligation (Hague Convention for the Suppression of Unlawful Seizure of Aircraft 1970; Torture Convention Art 7). In *Belgium v Senegal* (Habré case, ICJ 2012) the Court held that Senegal violated Art 7 CAT by failing to submit the case for prosecution.

### 4.2 Principles of Extradition

1.  **Dual criminality:** the act must be an offence in both requesting and requested States.

2.  **Speciality:** the surrendered person can be tried only for the offence for which extradition was granted, unless the requested State consents.

3.  **Political offence exception:** no extradition for political offences (pure vs relative political offences). **Attentat clause (Belgian clause, 1856):** an attack on a head of State is not a political offence. Terrorism, genocide, war crimes and hijacking are increasingly excluded from the political offence exception.

4.  **Non bis in idem (double jeopardy).**

5.  **Prima facie case** (common law States); **non-extradition of nationals** (civil law States such as Germany and France; common law States generally extradite nationals).

6.  **Death penalty and torture risk:** *Soering v UK* (ECtHR, 1989) – extradition to the USA where he faced the death-row phenomenon would violate Art 3 ECHR; *Chahal v UK* (1996); *Othman (Abu Qatada) v UK* (2012) on fair trial risk.

7.  **Reciprocity and lapse of time.**

8.  **Extradition vs deportation/expulsion:** extradition is on request of another State, treaty-based and with judicial scrutiny, for criminal justice; deportation is a unilateral act removing an alien in the State’s own interest. **Disguised extradition/rendition** is controversial (*Eichmann*; *Re Bennett*).

9.  **Interpol Red Notice** is a request to locate and provisionally arrest, **not** an international arrest warrant.

### 4.3 Indian Law on Extradition

- **Extradition Act 1962** applies to both **treaty States** and **non-treaty States** (by notification and arrangement – s. 3). The Central Government processes requests; a **Magistrate’s inquiry** is held (s. 7); **s. 2(c)** defines ‘extradition offence’ (punishable with imprisonment of at least **one year** under the laws of both States).

- **Section 31:** the Central Government may discharge or refuse surrender, e.g., where the offence is of a political character or the request is not in good faith/in the interests of justice. Speciality is also recognised under the statutory scheme.

- India has extradition treaties with 40+ States and arrangements with several more (India–UK 1992; India–USA 1997; India–Bangladesh 2013, amended 2016).

- ***Abu Salem v State of Maharashtra* (2011):** the Supreme Court held that the Government of India was bound by its assurances to Portugal (no death penalty, no more than 25 years) – extradition assurances bind courts and the executive alike (speciality).

- **Recent examples:** extradition of **Tahawwur Rana** (26/11 accused) from the USA in April 2025; Mehul Choksi arrested in Belgium in April 2025 (extradition proceedings); Vijay Mallya and Nirav Modi (UK courts, prison-conditions assurances); Bangladesh’s request concerning Sheikh Hasina (treaty political-offence exception debate).

- **Fugitive Economic Offenders Act 2018** supports India’s efforts against economic offenders abroad.

### 4.4 Asylum

**Asylum** is protection granted by a State to a person (usually a political refugee) on its territory or in its premises, with non-surrender to the State of origin. **Asylum is a right of the State, not of the individual** – UDHR Art 14 recognises only the right to **seek and enjoy** asylum; there is no corresponding duty to grant it (except through non-refoulement).

**Types:**

1.  **Territorial asylum** – within State territory; flows from territorial sovereignty. **UN Declaration on Territorial Asylum 1967** (UNGA Res 2312(XXII)) – non-binding; Art 3: no rejection at the frontier or expulsion where persecution may follow.

2.  **Extra-territorial (diplomatic/neutral) asylum** – in embassies, consulates, warships, aircraft; exceptional and mainly Latin American practice.

**Cases and instruments:**

- ***Asylum Case* (Colombia v Peru, ICJ 1950):** Haya de la Torre, a Peruvian rebel leader, took refuge in the Colombian embassy in Lima. The Court held Colombia was **not entitled to unilaterally and definitively qualify the offence** (political or common) so as to bind Peru; diplomatic asylum derogates from territorial sovereignty and needs a special legal basis; no regional custom was proved.

- ***Haya de la Torre Case* (Colombia v Peru, 1951):** the Court declined to order surrender but held that asylum could not continue indefinitely; parties were to negotiate a solution.

- **Latin American Conventions:** Havana 1928, Montevideo 1933, **Caracas Conventions 1954** (Diplomatic Asylum; Territorial Asylum).

- **VCDR 1961:** Art 22 (inviolability of mission premises) and Art 41 (premises not to be used in a manner incompatible with mission functions) – no general right of diplomatic asylum.

- **Examples:** Cardinal Mindszenty in the US legation in Budapest (1956–71); **Julian Assange** in the Ecuadorian embassy in London (2012–2019); the Dalai Lama in India (1959).

- **Regional:** African Charter Art 12(3); American Convention Art 22(7); EU Charter Art 18. India is party to no such regime and acts through executive discretion and Art 21 jurisprudence.

**Quick comparison:**

| Feature       | Extradition                             | Asylum                                                    |
|---------------|-----------------------------------------|-----------------------------------------------------------|
| Direction     | Surrender to requesting State           | Protection from requesting State                          |
| Basis         | Treaty/Act, reciprocity                 | Sovereignty; humanitarian considerations; no general duty |
| Target        | Accused/convicted criminals             | Political offenders/persecuted persons                    |
| Judicial role | Court inquiry (India: Magistrate, s. 7) | Mostly executive discretion                               |
| Key rule      | Dual criminality, speciality            | Non-refoulement; qualification of offence (*Asylum Case*) |

## Chapter 5: United Nations Organs

### 5.1 UN Charter – Basics

- Signed **26 June 1945** at San Francisco; in force **24 October 1945** (UN Day). Original members: **51**; present membership: **193** (South Sudan, 2011, the latest). Palestine and the Holy See are non-member observer States. Headquarters: New York.

- **Official languages (6):** Arabic, Chinese, English, French, Russian, Spanish. **Working languages:** English and French (Secretariat).

- **Art 1 – Purposes:** maintain international peace and security; develop friendly relations based on equal rights and self-determination; international cooperation in economic, social, cultural and humanitarian problems; be a centre for harmonising actions.

- **Art 2 – Principles:** sovereign equality (2(1)); good faith (2(2)); peaceful settlement (2(3)); **prohibition of threat or use of force (2(4))**; assistance to UN (2(5)); non-Members to act in accordance with principles (2(6)); **non-intervention in domestic jurisdiction (2(7))** – *not applicable to Chapter VII enforcement measures*.

- **Membership (Arts 3–6):** Art 4 – open to peace-loving States accepting the obligations; admission by GA on **SC recommendation** (*Competence of the General Assembly for Admission* AO, 1950). Art 5 – suspension; Art 6 – expulsion (never used).

- **Art 7 – Six principal organs:** General Assembly, Security Council, ECOSOC, Trusteeship Council, ICJ, Secretariat.

- **Art 103:** obligations under the Charter prevail over obligations under any other international agreement (cf. *Lockerbie*, 1992). **Amendment:** Art 108 (two-thirds of GA and ratification by two-thirds of Members including all P5); Art 109 (review conference).

### 5.2 General Assembly (Chapter IV, Arts 9–22)

- All Members, one vote each; the ‘town meeting of the world’. Regular session begins on the Tuesday of the third week of September. Six Main Committees (First – disarmament/international security; Second – economic and financial; Third – social, humanitarian and cultural; Fourth – special political and decolonisation; Fifth – administrative and budgetary; Sixth – legal).

- **Art 10:** may discuss any matter within the Charter and make recommendations. **Art 11:** peace and security – general principles, may call attention of SC. **Art 12:** GA shall not make recommendations on a dispute while the SC is exercising its functions (unless the SC so requests). **Art 13:** study and recommendations to promote international law development and codification (**ILC**, established 1947; 34 members, five-year terms; Geneva). **Art 14:** peaceful adjustment of situations. **Art 17:** budget; apportionment of expenses (*Certain Expenses* AO, 1962 – peacekeeping costs are ‘expenses of the Organization’). **Art 19:** loss of vote if arrears equal or exceed contributions due for the preceding **two full years**. **Art 22:** subsidiary organs.

- **Voting (Art 18):** **important questions** (peace and security recommendations, election of members of organs, admission, suspension/expulsion, budget) need **two-thirds of members present and voting**; other questions need a **simple majority**. Abstention is not counted as a vote.

- **Uniting for Peace Resolution** (377(V), 1950): if the SC fails due to veto, the GA can meet in emergency special session within 24 hours and recommend collective measures. Used for Korea (1950), Suez (1956), Afghanistan (1980), Ukraine (2022). **Veto initiative:** GA Res 76/262 (April 2022) – the GA meets within 10 working days after a veto.

- GA resolutions are generally non-binding, except on internal matters (budget, admission, etc.). **Chair/President:** elected for each session (80th session: Annalena Baerbock, 2025–26).

### 5.3 Security Council (Chapter V, Arts 23–32)

- **Composition (Art 23):** **15 members** – **5 permanent (P5: China, France, Russia, UK, USA)** and **10 non-permanent** elected by the GA for **two-year terms** (two-thirds majority; not eligible for immediate re-election). Increased from 11 to 15 by the 1963 amendment (in force 1965). **Regional distribution of 10:** Africa 3, Asia-Pacific 2, Latin America and Caribbean 2, Western Europe and Others 2, Eastern Europe 1. **Members for 2026:** Denmark, Greece, Pakistan, Panama, Somalia (terms ending 2026) and Bahrain, Colombia, DR Congo, Latvia, Liberia (elected for 2026–27). Presidency rotates monthly in English alphabetical order.

- **Functions:** Art 24 – primary responsibility for maintenance of peace and security; Art 25 – Members agree to accept and carry out SC decisions (*Namibia* AO, 1971 – Art 25 applies to all binding decisions, not only Chapter VII).

- **Voting (Art 27):** each member has one vote. **Procedural matters** – affirmative vote of **nine** members. **All other matters** – nine votes **including the concurring votes of the permanent members** (veto). **Abstention is not treated as a veto** (practice endorsed in *Namibia* AO). Art 27(3): a party to a dispute shall abstain in decisions under Chapter VI and Art 52(3).

- **Chapter VI (Arts 33–38) – Pacific Settlement:** Art 33 – negotiation, enquiry, mediation, conciliation, arbitration, judicial settlement, resort to regional agencies, or other peaceful means; Art 34 – investigation; Art 36 – recommending procedures (legal disputes should generally be referred to the ICJ – Art 36(3)); Art 37 – referral; Art 38 – recommendation at request of all parties.

- **Chapter VII (Arts 39–51) – Enforcement:** Art 39 – determines existence of **threat to the peace, breach of the peace or act of aggression**; Art 40 – provisional measures; **Art 41 – non-military measures** (sanctions, severance of relations, interruption of communications); **Art 42 – military action**; Art 43 – special agreements for troops (never concluded); Art 47 – Military Staff Committee; **Art 51 – inherent right of individual or collective self-defence** if an armed attack occurs, until the SC takes necessary measures; must be reported to the SC; requirements of necessity and proportionality (*Caroline* test; *Nicaragua*, *Oil Platforms*, *DRC v Uganda*).

- **Chapter VIII (Arts 52–54):** regional arrangements – enforcement action by regional agencies requires SC authorisation (Art 53).

- **Practice:** ad hoc tribunals ICTY (Res 827, 1993) and ICTR (Res 955, 1994) under Chapter VII; peacekeeping (not expressly in the Charter – ‘Chapter VI½’; first observer mission UNTSO 1948; first armed force UNEF I 1956); Libya intervention (Res 1973, 2011) – Responsibility to Protect (World Summit 2005); counter-terrorism Res 1373 (2001).

- **Reform debate:** G4 (Brazil, Germany, India, Japan) vs Uniting for Consensus (‘Coffee Club’); African Common Position (Ezulwini Consensus) demands two permanent seats with veto; IGN (Intergovernmental Negotiations). India has served as a non-permanent member eight times (latest 2021–22).

### 5.4 Economic and Social Council (ECOSOC) – Chapters IX–X, Arts 55–72

- **54 members** elected by the GA for **three-year terms** (one-third retire annually); originally 18, enlarged to 27 (1965) and 54 (1973). Art 62 – studies, reports, recommendations; Art 63 – coordinates specialised agencies through agreements; **Art 71 – consultation with NGOs** (consultative status).

- Functional commissions (Statistical, Population, Social Development, Status of Women, Narcotic Drugs, Crime Prevention, etc.) and regional commissions. The **Commission on Human Rights** was replaced in 2006 by the **Human Rights Council** (GA Res 60/251) – 47 members, subsidiary body of the GA, Universal Periodic Review (UPR).

- **Specialised agencies (Art 57):** ILO (1919, Geneva), FAO (Rome), UNESCO (Paris), WHO (Geneva, 1948), World Bank/IBRD, IMF, ICAO (Montreal), IMO (London), ITU, UPU, WMO, WIPO, UNIDO, IFAD, UNWTO. **WTO** and **IAEA** are related organisations, not specialised agencies.

### 5.5 Trusteeship Council (Chapters XII–XIII, Arts 86–91)

Supervised the International Trusteeship System. Suspended operations on **1 November 1994** after the independence of Palau (the last Trust Territory). Chapter XI (Art 73) deals with **Non-Self-Governing Territories** (declaration regarding NSGTs; Special Committee on Decolonisation – ‘Committee of 24’). **GA Res 1514 (XV), 1960** – Declaration on the Granting of Independence to Colonial Countries and Peoples.

### 5.6 International Court of Justice (Chapter XIV, Arts 92–96; ICJ Statute)

- **Seat:** Peace Palace, The Hague. Successor to the PCIJ (1922–1946). The only principal organ not located in New York.

- **Composition:** **15 judges**, no two of the same nationality (Art 3); elected for **9-year terms** by the **GA and SC concurrently** (absolute majority in each – no veto in SC); one-third retire every three years (triennial elections); President and Vice-President for 3 years; **quorum 9** (Art 25). **Judges *ad hoc*** (Art 31) – a party without a national judge may appoint one. **President:** Iwasawa Yuji (since March 2025); the next regular election (five seats) falls in autumn 2026 – terms of five judges, including Dalveer Bhandari of India, expire in February 2027 (verify result).

- **Contentious jurisdiction (Art 34, 36):** only **States** may be parties. Bases: **special agreement** (compromis); **compromissory clauses** in treaties (Art 36(1)); **Optional Clause** (Art 36(2) – compulsory jurisdiction among declarants, subject to **reciprocity**); **forum prorogatum**. Art 36(6): the Court decides on its own jurisdiction (*Kompetenz-Kompetenz*). India has an Optional Clause declaration (current 2019) with several reservations (Commonwealth disputes, etc.).

- **Interim measures (Art 41):** binding – *LaGrand* (2001). **Art 59:** decision binding only on parties; **Art 60:** final and without appeal; **Art 61:** revision on discovery of a new fact (within 10 years); **Art 62–63:** intervention.

- **Enforcement (Art 94):** each Member undertakes to comply; the SC may make recommendations or decide on measures (e.g., *Nicaragua v USA* – USA blocked by veto).

- **Advisory jurisdiction (Art 96):** GA and SC, and other organs/agencies authorised by GA. Advisory opinions are **not binding**. Key AOs: *Reparation for Injuries* (1949), *Genocide Reservations* (1951), *Certain Expenses* (1962), *Namibia* (1971), *Western Sahara* (1975), *Nuclear Weapons* (1996), *Wall* (2004), *Kosovo* (2010), *Chagos* (2019), **Obligations of States in respect of Climate Change** (23 July 2025), and AO relating to Israel’s obligations in respect of the UN and other organisations (October 2025).

- **Important contentious cases:** *Corfu Channel* (UK v Albania, 1949 – first case), *Nicaragua* (1986), *Gabčíkovo-Nagymaros* (1997), *Bosnia Genocide* (2007), *Croatia v Serbia* (2015), *Jadhav* (2019), *The Gambia v Myanmar* (genocide, provisional measures 2020), **South Africa v Israel** (Genocide Convention, provisional measures from January 2024), **Ukraine v Russia** (Genocide Convention).

### 5.7 Secretariat (Chapter XV, Arts 97–101)

- Headed by the **Secretary-General** (Art 97): appointed by the GA on the **recommendation of the SC** (veto applies); five-year renewable term. **Art 99:** SG may bring to the attention of the SC any matter threatening international peace and security (political role). **Art 100:** independence of staff. **Art 101:** staff appointed by GA regulations; paramount consideration is efficiency, competence and integrity.

- **Secretaries-General:** Trygve Lie (Norway, 1946–52), Dag Hammarskjöld (Sweden, 1953–61), U Thant (Burma, 1961–71), Kurt Waldheim (Austria, 1972–81), Javier Pérez de Cuéllar (Peru, 1982–91), Boutros Boutros-Ghali (Egypt, 1992–96), Kofi Annan (Ghana, 1997–2006), Ban Ki-moon (South Korea, 2007–16), **António Guterres (Portugal, from 1 January 2017; second term ends 31 December 2026)**. The selection of the next SG is happening in 2026 – verify the appointee.

### 5.8 Other UN Bodies (frequently asked)

- **UNCITRAL (1966)**, **UNCTAD (1964)**, **UNDP**, **UNEP (1972)**, **UNICEF (1946)**, **UN Women**, **UNHCR**, **Peacebuilding Commission (2005)**, **ITLOS** (Hamburg, 21 judges, UNCLOS 1982, in force 1994), **International Seabed Authority** (Kingston, Jamaica), **ICC** (Rome Statute 1998, in force 1 July 2002; The Hague; independent – not a UN organ; India not a party).

## Chapter 6: Dispute Settlement Mechanisms

### 6.1 Legal Framework

- **UN Charter Art 2(3):** all Members shall settle disputes by peaceful means; **Art 33(1):** parties to a dispute likely to endanger peace shall seek a solution by negotiation, enquiry, mediation, conciliation, arbitration, judicial settlement, resort to regional agencies or arrangements, or other peaceful means of their choice. **Principle of free choice of means.**

- **Earlier instruments:** Hague Conventions for the Pacific Settlement of International Disputes **1899** and **1907** (created the **Permanent Court of Arbitration, PCA**); Covenant of the League of Nations; General Act for the Pacific Settlement of International Disputes 1928; **Kellogg–Briand Pact 1928** (renunciation of war); **Manila Declaration 1982** (UNGA Res 37/10) on peaceful settlement; **Friendly Relations Declaration 1970** (UNGA Res 2625(XXV)).

- **Classification:** (A) diplomatic/non-judicial means; (B) legal means; (C) institutional/political means through international organisations; (D) coercive means short of war.

### 6.2 Diplomatic (Non-Judicial) Means

1.  **Negotiation:** direct talks; the most common method; obligation to negotiate in good faith, not necessarily to reach agreement (*North Sea Continental Shelf*; *Lake Lanoux*).

2.  **Good offices:** a third party brings the parties together but does not participate in the negotiations (e.g., US good offices in the Russo-Japanese war, Treaty of Portsmouth 1905).

3.  **Mediation:** the third party participates actively and may propose terms, which are non-binding (e.g., Soviet mediation at **Tashkent 1966** between India and Pakistan; US mediation in the Camp David Accords 1978).

4.  **Inquiry (fact-finding):** impartial investigation of facts (*Dogger Bank* incident 1904 – Anglo-Russian Commission of Inquiry; Hague Conventions).

5.  **Conciliation:** a commission examines facts and law and proposes terms of settlement; non-binding (VCLT Annex – conciliation under Art 66(b)).

### 6.3 Arbitration

Settlement of disputes between States by judges of their own choice on the basis of respect for law, producing a **binding award** (Hague Convention 1907, Art 37). Modern arbitration began with the **Jay Treaty 1794** and the ***Alabama Claims* arbitration (1872)**.

- Requires a ***compromis*** (special agreement) or compromissory clause; **ad hoc** tribunals or institutional (PCA); awards binding and final; limited grounds for nullity (excess of power, corruption, failure to state reasons). **ILC Model Rules on Arbitral Procedure 1958.**

- **Permanent Court of Arbitration (PCA):** established by the 1899 Hague Convention; seat: Peace Palace, The Hague; an administrative framework (panel of arbitrators, International Bureau) rather than a standing court; India is a member.

- **Leading arbitrations:** *Alabama Claims* (1872); *Island of Palmas* (1928 – territorial sovereignty and effective occupation); ***Trail Smelter* (USA v Canada, 1938/1941 – no State may use its territory to cause injury to another; precursor to the polluter-pays and prevention principle)**; *Lake Lanoux* (France/Spain, 1957 – shared rivers, duty to negotiate); *Rainbow Warrior* (France/New Zealand, 1990); ***Rann of Kutch* (India v Pakistan, 1968)**; **Bay of Bengal Maritime Boundary** (Bangladesh v India, PCA 2014); ***Enrica Lexie* (Italy v India)** – ITLOS provisional measures (2015), Annex VII arbitral award (2020: Italian marines enjoy immunity, India entitled to compensation); **South China Sea Arbitration** (Philippines v China, PCA 2016 – ‘nine-dash line’ has no legal basis; China rejected the award); **Chagos MPA Arbitration** (2015).

- **Indus Waters Treaty (1960):** Court of Arbitration and Neutral Expert proceedings (*Kishenganga*, 2013 award). India placed the treaty ‘in abeyance’ in April 2025; the Court of Arbitration in its Supplemental Award of June 2025 held that it retains competence – an important recent India-centric item.

- **Investment arbitration:** ICSID (Washington Convention 1965; India is not a party), UNCITRAL rules; India’s Model BIT 2016; *White Industries v India* (2011), *Cairn Energy v India* (2020 – retrospective tax), *Devas v Antrix*.

### 6.4 Judicial Settlement

- **ICJ** (see 5.6). **PCIJ (1922–1946)** was the predecessor under the League.

- **ITLOS (Hamburg):** established by UNCLOS 1982 (Annex VI), 21 judges (9-year terms), jurisdiction in law of the sea disputes and **prompt release (Art 292)**; **Seabed Disputes Chamber**; advisory opinion on climate change and the law of the sea (21 May 2024). **UNCLOS Part XV:** Art 287 – States may choose among ITLOS, ICJ, Annex VII arbitration (default), Annex VIII special arbitration; Art 290 – provisional measures.

- **UNCLOS in brief:** adopted 10 December 1982 (Montego Bay), in force 16 November 1994; internal waters; territorial sea 12 nm; contiguous zone 24 nm; **EEZ 200 nm**; continental shelf up to 200 nm (or up to 350 nm); high seas freedoms; innocent passage; transit passage; archipelagic sea lanes; hot pursuit (Art 111). India ratified in 1995.

- **Regional/human rights courts:** ECtHR (Strasbourg), Inter-American Court (San José), African Court (Arusha). The Inter-American Court delivered a climate advisory opinion (AO-32) in July 2025.

- **International criminal courts:** ICTY, ICTR (now IRMCT), Special Court for Sierra Leone, **ICC** (see Section 8).

### 6.5 Political/Institutional Means

SC (Chapters VI and VII), GA (Arts 10–14), Secretary-General (Art 99, good offices), regional organisations (OAS, AU, EU, ASEAN, OSCE – Chapter VIII).

### 6.6 Coercive Means (Short of War)

- **Retorsion:** unfriendly but lawful act (recalling ambassadors, trade restrictions).

- **Reprisals/countermeasures:** otherwise unlawful acts in response to an internationally wrongful act; must be proportionate and temporary (*Naulilaa*, 1928; *Air Services Agreement*, 1978; *Gabčíkovo-Nagymaros*, 1997). Armed reprisals are prohibited by the Charter.

- **Embargo, blockade (pacific blockade), intervention, boycott.**

### 6.7 State Responsibility (ILC Articles 2001)

- **Elements:** (a) conduct attributable to the State; (b) breach of an international obligation (Art 2). **Art 12** – breach regardless of origin.

- **Circumstances precluding wrongfulness (Arts 20–25):** consent, self-defence, countermeasures, *force majeure*, distress, necessity. None can preclude wrongfulness for breach of a peremptory norm (Art 26).

- **Reparation (Art 31, 34–37):** restitution, compensation, satisfaction (*Chorzów Factory*). **Arts 40–41:** serious breaches of peremptory norms; **Art 48:** invocation by non-injured States (*erga omnes* obligations).

- **Cases:** *Corfu Channel*, *Nicaragua*, *Genocide* (Bosnia v Serbia 2007 – ‘effective control’ test; Tadić ‘overall control’ test rejected for State responsibility), *Tehran Hostages* (1980).

## Chapter 7: World Trade Organization (WTO): Basics

### 7.1 History

- **Bretton Woods (1944):** IMF and IBRD; the proposed **International Trade Organization (ITO)** under the **Havana Charter (1948)** failed (US did not ratify). **GATT 1947** was signed in Geneva by 23 countries on 30 October 1947 and applied provisionally from **1 January 1948**.

- **GATT rounds:** Geneva (1947), Annecy (1949), Torquay (1951), Geneva (1956), Dillon (1960–61), **Kennedy (1964–67)** (anti-dumping), **Tokyo (1973–79)** (non-tariff barriers), **Uruguay (1986–94)** (launched at Punta del Este; concluded at **Marrakesh on 15 April 1994**). **Doha Development Agenda** (2001) – stalled.

- **WTO:** created by the **Marrakesh Agreement Establishing the WTO**; came into force on **1 January 1995**; HQ Geneva; **166 members** (Comoros and Timor-Leste joined in 2024). WTO has international legal personality (Art VIII). **Director-General:** Ngozi Okonjo-Iweala (Nigeria) – first woman and first African; since March 2021; second term from September 2025. First WTO DG: Renato Ruggiero (Peter Sutherland was last GATT DG). India is an original member of the GATT and WTO.

### 7.2 Institutional Structure (Marrakesh Agreement, Arts IV–IX)

- **Ministerial Conference** (highest body, meets at least every two years); **General Council** (also acts as the Dispute Settlement Body and Trade Policy Review Body); Councils for Trade in Goods, Trade in Services and TRIPS; committees; Secretariat headed by the DG.

- **Decision-making (Art IX):** by **consensus**; if not possible, by voting – each member one vote (majority of votes cast); **three-fourths** majority for authoritative interpretations and waivers; amendments depend on type of provision.

- **Ministerial Conferences:** MC1 Singapore (1996); MC3 Seattle (1999 – collapsed); MC4 Doha (2001); MC5 Cancún (2003); MC6 Hong Kong (2005); MC9 **Bali (2013) – Trade Facilitation Agreement and the ‘Peace Clause’ on public stockholding**; MC10 Nairobi (2015 – abolition of agricultural export subsidies); MC11 Buenos Aires (2017); **MC12 Geneva (June 2022) – Fisheries Subsidies Agreement, TRIPS waiver decision on COVID-19 vaccines**; **MC13 Abu Dhabi (Feb–Mar 2024)** – e-commerce customs duties moratorium extended; **MC14 Yaoundé, Cameroon (March 2026)** – verify outcomes.

### 7.3 Agreements Under the WTO (Annexes)

**Later agreements:** Trade Facilitation Agreement (in force 22 February 2017); **Fisheries Subsidies Agreement** (adopted June 2022; entered into force **15 September 2025**); Information Technology Agreement.

### 7.4 Core Principles

- **Most-Favoured-Nation (MFN):** GATT **Art I**; GATS Art II; TRIPS Art 4 – no discrimination among trading partners. Exceptions: customs unions/FTAs (**Art XXIV**), Enabling Clause (1979 – preferences for developing countries, GSP), GATS Art V.

- **National Treatment:** **Art III** – imported goods must be treated no less favourably than domestic goods once they have entered the market (*Japan – Alcoholic Beverages*).

- **Tariff bindings:** **Art II** – schedules of concessions; **Art XI** – general elimination of quantitative restrictions; **Art X** – transparency.

- **Trade remedies:** **Anti-dumping (Art VI)** – export price below normal value; **countervailing duties** (SCM); **Safeguards (Art XIX)** – emergency measures against import surges, temporary and non-discriminatory.

- **General exceptions (Art XX):** (a) public morals, (b) human, animal or plant life or health, (g) conservation of exhaustible natural resources (e.g., *US – Gasoline*, *US – Shrimp/Turtle*, *EC – Asbestos* (Art XX(b)), *Brazil – Retreaded Tyres*). **Art XXI:** security exceptions (*Russia – Traffic in Transit*, 2019). **Art XII/XVIII:** balance-of-payments and infant-industry measures for developing countries.

- **Special and Differential Treatment (SDT)** for developing and least-developed countries.

### 7.5 Other Key Agreements

- **Agreement on Agriculture (AoA):** three pillars – market access, domestic support, export competition. **Domestic support boxes:** *Amber* (trade-distorting, subject to reduction; de minimis 5% for developed and 10% for developing countries), *Blue* (production-limiting programmes), *Green* (minimal distortion). **India’s issues:** public stockholding for food security (MSP), Special Safeguard Mechanism (SSM), the ‘Peace Clause’ (Bali 2013), cotton subsidies.

- **TRIPS:** minimum standards (Berne Convention copyright; patents 20 years – Art 33; trademarks; geographical indications; industrial designs; undisclosed information); compulsory licensing (Art 31); **Doha Declaration on TRIPS and Public Health (2001)**; **Art 31bis** (in force January 2017); India – *Novartis v UOI* (2013, s. 3(d) Patents Act), Natco–Bayer compulsory licence (2012).

- **GATS – four modes of supply:** Mode 1 cross-border; Mode 2 consumption abroad; Mode 3 commercial presence; Mode 4 movement of natural persons.

- **SPS and TBT:** health/technical standards; the SPS Agreement recognises Codex, OIE and IPPC standards (*EC – Hormones*).

- **TRIMs:** prohibits local-content and trade-balancing requirements.

### 7.6 Dispute Settlement Understanding (DSU)

- **Dispute Settlement Body (DSB)** – General Council members; administers the DSU.

- **Stages:** (1) **Consultations** (60 days) → (2) **Panel** (establishment is virtually automatic by **negative/reverse consensus**; 3 members; report ordinarily within six to nine months) → (3) **Adoption of report** by the DSB by negative consensus → (4) **Appellate Body** review (7 members, four-year terms; 60–90 days; only issues of law) → (5) **Implementation** within a reasonable period of time (Art 21.3(c) arbitration) → (6) **Compensation or suspension of concessions/retaliation** (Art 22; cross-retaliation permitted). **Art 21.5:** compliance panels. **Art 25:** arbitration as an alternative.

- **Appellate Body crisis:** the AB has been **non-functional since 11 December 2019** because the USA has blocked appointments. Members use **‘appeal into the void’**; the **MPIA** (Multi-Party Interim Appeal Arbitration Arrangement, April 2020 – an Art 25 arrangement among the EU, China, Canada, Japan and others) provides an alternative; reform talks continue.

- **India-related disputes:** *India – Quantitative Restrictions* (US, 1999), *India – Solar Cells* (DS456), *India – Agricultural Products* (avian influenza, DS430), *EC – Tariff Preferences* (India v EC, 2004 – Enabling Clause), *India – ICT tariffs* (EU/Japan/Chinese Taipei, 2023 panels; appeal into the void), US steel and aluminium measures (India’s retaliation notifications).

- **Recent trend:** unilateral tariff measures since 2025 (especially US tariffs), trade-and-security (Art XXI) controversies, WTO reform (dispute settlement, level playing field, e-commerce moratorium).

## Chapter 8: International Humanitarian Law (IHL): Conventions and Protocols

### 8.1 Concept, Sources and Evolution

- **IHL (law of armed conflict/*jus in bello*)** regulates the conduct of hostilities and protects persons not or no longer taking part in them. It applies **equally to all parties**, irrespective of the legality of the war (*jus ad bellum* – Art 2(4), Art 51 UN Charter is a separate branch).

- **Two streams:** **Hague law** (means and methods of warfare – Hague Conventions 1899/1907, Hague Regulations 1907) and **Geneva law** (protection of victims – Geneva Conventions 1949 and Additional Protocols 1977). Additional Protocol I (1977) merges both.

- **Milestones:** *Lieber Code* (1863, USA); **ICRC founded in 1863** (Henry Dunant, after Solferino 1859; *A Memory of Solferino*, 1862); **First Geneva Convention 1864**; **St Petersburg Declaration 1868** (prohibition of explosive projectiles under 400 g); Hague Peace Conferences **1899 and 1907**; **Geneva Gas Protocol 1925**; Geneva Conventions of 1929; **Four Geneva Conventions of 12 August 1949** (in force 21 October 1950); **Additional Protocols 8 June 1977** (in force 7 December 1978); **Protocol III 2005** (in force 2007).

- **Martens Clause** (Hague 1899 preamble; AP I Art 1(2)): in cases not covered by treaties, civilians and combatants remain under the protection of the principles of the law of nations, from established custom, **principles of humanity and dictates of public conscience**.

- **Customary IHL:** ICRC Study (Henckaerts and Doswald-Beck, 2005) – 161 rules. *Nuclear Weapons* AO (1996) called the fundamental rules of humanitarian law ‘intransgressible principles of international customary law’; the Court could not conclude definitively on the lawfulness of nuclear weapons in an extreme circumstance of self-defence where the very survival of a State is at stake.

### 8.2 Fundamental Principles

1.  **Military necessity** – only measures required to achieve a legitimate military aim.

2.  **Humanity** – no unnecessary suffering or superfluous injury (AP I Art 35(2); Hague Reg Art 23(e)).

3.  **Distinction** – between combatants and civilians, military objectives and civilian objects (AP I Arts 48, 51, 52).

4.  **Proportionality** – no attack expected to cause incidental civilian harm excessive in relation to the concrete and direct military advantage anticipated (AP I Art 51(5)(b)).

5.  **Precaution** – feasible precautions in attack (Art 57) and against effects of attack (Art 58).

6.  **Non-discrimination (adverse distinction), Independence of IHL from *jus ad bellum*, Neutrality.**

- **Prohibited methods:** perfidy (AP I Art 37 – feigning protected status), denial of quarter, **starvation of civilians as a method of warfare (AP I Art 54)**, indiscriminate attacks (Art 51(4)), reprisals against protected persons, **attacks on the natural environment (Arts 35(3), 55 – widespread, long-term and severe damage)**, attacks on works and installations containing dangerous forces (Art 56 – dams, dykes, nuclear plants). *Ruses of war* are permitted.

### 8.3 The Four Geneva Conventions of 1949

| Convention | Subject                                                      | Origin         | Notes                                                                                                              |
|------------|--------------------------------------------------------------|----------------|--------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------|
| **GC I**   | Wounded and sick in armed forces in the field                | 1864           | Protects medical personnel, units, transports; Art 38 emblems (Red Cross, Red Crescent)                            |
| **GC II**  | Wounded, sick and shipwrecked members of armed forces at sea | Hague X (1907) | Hospital ships                                                                                                     |
| **GC III** | Prisoners of war                                             | 1929           | Art 4 POW status; Art 17 name, rank, date of birth, number; Art 118 release without delay after active hostilities |
| **GC IV**  | Protection of civilian persons in time of war                | New (1949)     | Occupied territories; internment; Art 33 prohibits collective punishment; Art 49 deportation/transfer              |

- **Universal ratification:** all States are parties (Palestine acceded in 2014). **India** ratified the Conventions in **1950** and enacted the **Geneva Conventions Act, 1960**; India is **not** a party to AP I or AP II.

- **Common Articles (1–4 in each Convention):** **Art 1** – undertake to respect and **ensure respect**; **Art 2** – applies to **international armed conflicts** (declared war or any armed conflict between two or more States, occupation even without resistance); **Art 3** – **non-international armed conflicts** (‘mini-convention’): humane treatment without adverse distinction; prohibits violence to life and person, hostage-taking, outrages upon personal dignity, sentences without regular judicial guarantees; wounded and sick shall be collected and cared for; ICRC may offer its services. *Nicaragua* (1986) – Art 3 reflects elementary considerations of humanity applicable in any conflict. *Hamdan v Rumsfeld* (US Supreme Court, 2006) – Common Art 3 applies to Guantanamo detainees.

- **Penal enforcement (Arts 49/50/129/146):** parties must enact legislation, search for and **try or extradite** persons alleged to have committed or ordered **grave breaches** (universal jurisdiction – *aut dedere aut judicare*).

- **Grave breaches (common to all Conventions):** wilful killing; torture or inhuman treatment including biological experiments; wilfully causing great suffering or serious injury to body or health; **extensive destruction and appropriation of property not justified by military necessity**. **GC III:** compelling a POW to serve in hostile forces; depriving of fair trial. **GC IV:** unlawful deportation or transfer, unlawful confinement, taking of hostages. **AP I Art 85** adds attacks on civilians, perfidy, apartheid, unjustifiable delay in repatriation, attacks on cultural property.

- **Combatant and POW status (GC III Art 4):** members of armed forces; militia/volunteer corps/organised resistance movements satisfying **four conditions** – (1) responsible command, (2) fixed distinctive sign recognisable at a distance, (3) carrying arms openly, (4) conducting operations in accordance with laws and customs of war; ***levée en masse*** (Art 4(A)(6)); **Art 5** – doubtful cases decided by a competent tribunal. **Mercenaries** are not entitled to combatant/POW status (AP I Art 47); **spies** (Art 46) lose status. **Child soldiers:** Optional Protocol to CRC (2000) – no compulsory recruitment under 18; recruitment under 15 is a war crime.

- **Emblems:** Red Cross (reversed Swiss flag), Red Crescent, **Red Crystal** (Protocol III, 2005). The **Red Lion and Sun** is no longer used (Iran dropped it in 1980). **Misuse of emblem** = perfidy/war crime.

- **Occupation:** Hague Regulations Arts 42–56; GC IV Arts 27–34, 47–78. **Occupant has administration, not sovereignty;** must respect laws in force unless absolutely prevented (Hague Reg Art 43); cannot transfer its own civilian population into occupied territory (GC IV **Art 49(6)**); collective penalties prohibited (Art 33); duty to ensure food and medical supplies (Art 55). **Wall AO (2004):** GC IV applies in the Occupied Palestinian Territory; settlements and the Wall violate IHL; *Armed Activities* (DRC v Uganda, 2005): Uganda as occupying power in Ituri. ICJ AO of **19 July 2024** declared Israel’s continued presence in the OPT unlawful.

### 8.4 Additional Protocols

- **Protocol I (1977) – International armed conflicts:** includes **wars of national liberation** against colonial domination, alien occupation and racist regimes (**Art 1(4)**). Key: Art 35 (basic rules), Art 37 (perfidy), Art 43–44 (armed forces, combatants – Art 44(3) relaxed distinction in exceptional situations), Art 48 (distinction), Art 51 (civilian population), Art 52 (civilian objects; definition of military objective in 52(2)), Art 53 (cultural property), Art 54 (starvation), Art 56, Art 57–58 (precautions), Art 75 (fundamental guarantees), Art 85 (grave breaches), **Art 90 – International Humanitarian Fact-Finding Commission (IHFFC)**.

- **Protocol II (1977) – Non-international armed conflicts:** higher threshold than Common Art 3 – requires armed forces/dissident groups under responsible command exercising **control over part of the territory** to carry out sustained and concerted military operations and implement the Protocol; excludes riots, isolated and sporadic acts of violence (Art 1(2)). Key: Art 4 (fundamental guarantees), Art 13 (civilians), Art 14 (starvation), Art 17 (forced movement). No POW status in NIACs.

- **Protocol III (2005):** additional distinctive emblem (**Red Crystal**).

- **India:** not party to AP I and II; says that it observes their rules to the extent they reflect custom.

### 8.6 International Red Cross and Red Crescent Movement

- **Components:** ICRC (1863, Geneva – private Swiss association with international mandate under the Geneva Conventions, i.e., Common Art 3 and GC III Art 126), IFRC (1919), National Societies (Indian Red Cross Society – Act of 1920).

- **Seven Fundamental Principles (proclaimed at Vienna 1965):** humanity, impartiality, neutrality, independence, voluntary service, unity, universality. Henry Dunant won the first Nobel Peace Prize (1901); the ICRC won the Nobel Peace Prize in 1917, 1944 and 1963.

### 8.7 Accountability: War Crimes, Genocide, Crimes Against Humanity

- **Nuremberg Tribunal (IMT, London Charter 1945):** crimes against peace, war crimes, crimes against humanity; **Nuremberg Principles** affirmed by GA Res 95(I) (1946) and formulated by ILC (1950); **Tokyo Tribunal (IMTFE, 1946)**. Principles: individual criminal responsibility; official position no defence; superior orders no defence (but may mitigate); right to fair trial.

- ***Ex post* tribunals:** **ICTY** (SC Res 827, 1993; Hague; closed 2017), **ICTR** (SC Res 955, 1994; Arusha; closed 2015) – residual functions with the **IRMCT**; Special Court for Sierra Leone (Charles Taylor, 2012); ECCC (Cambodia); Special Tribunal for Lebanon. **Cases:** *Tadić* (ICTY 1995 – jurisdiction; the test for armed conflict; war crimes can occur in NIACs; 1999 – ‘overall control’ test); ***Akayesu* (ICTR 1998 – first conviction for genocide by an international tribunal; rape as an act of genocide)**; *Furundžija*, *Kunarac* (sexual violence, enslavement), *Krstić* (Srebrenica genocide).

- **Command responsibility:** AP I Arts 86–87; Rome Statute Art 28; *Yamashita* (US Military Commission, 1945).

- **Universal jurisdiction:** *Eichmann* (Israel, 1961); *Pinochet* (UK House of Lords, 1999 – no immunity for torture); *Arrest Warrant* (DRC v Belgium, ICJ 2002 – sitting foreign ministers enjoy personal immunity before foreign national courts, but not before certain international courts).

- **Genocide Convention 1948:** adopted 9 December 1948; in force 12 January 1951; term coined by **Raphael Lemkin** (1944). **Art II:** acts committed with **intent to destroy, in whole or in part, a national, ethnical, racial or religious group** – (a) killing; (b) serious bodily or mental harm; (c) deliberately inflicting conditions of life calculated to bring about physical destruction; (d) imposing measures to prevent births; (e) forcibly transferring children. **Art III:** punishable acts – genocide, conspiracy, direct and public incitement, attempt, complicity. Political groups are not covered. **Art IX:** ICJ jurisdiction. *Bosnia v Serbia* (2007): Srebrenica was genocide; Serbia failed to prevent and punish but was not responsible for committing it; *Croatia v Serbia* (2015): requires *dolus specialis*; *Gambia v Myanmar*; *South Africa v Israel* (2024 provisional measures). India ratified the Genocide Convention in 1959.

### 8.8 International Criminal Court (ICC)

- **Rome Statute:** adopted **17 July 1998**; in force **1 July 2002**; seat: **The Hague**; permanent treaty-based court, **not part of the UN** (relationship agreement 2004). Roughly 125 States Parties after Ukraine joined (1 January 2025); several withdrawal notices since (Hungary, and announcements by Burkina Faso, Mali and Niger – verify current status). **India, USA, China, Russia, Israel are not parties.**

- **Crimes (Art 5):** genocide (Art 6), crimes against humanity (Art 7 – widespread or systematic attack against any civilian population), war crimes (Art 8, including NIACs), **crime of aggression** (Arts 8*bis*, 15*bis*, 15*ter* – Kampala amendments 2010, activated on 17 July 2018).

- **Jurisdiction:** only over **natural persons** (Art 25), aged 18 or above (Art 26), **after 1 July 2002** (Art 11); **no immunity for official capacity** (Art 27); **command responsibility** (Art 28); no limitation (Art 29); superior orders (Art 33). **Art 12:** preconditions – territory of a State Party or nationality of the accused (or *ad hoc* acceptance). **Art 13:** triggers – State Party referral, **UNSC referral** (Chapter VII – Darfur 2005, Libya 2011), or *proprio motu* investigation by the Prosecutor (with Pre-Trial Chamber authorisation). **Art 16:** SC may defer for 12 months (renewable). **Art 17:** **complementarity** – the ICC is a court of last resort. **Art 77:** maximum penalty 30 years or life; **no death penalty**.

- **Organs (Art 34):** Presidency, Chambers (18 judges, nine-year non-renewable terms), Office of the Prosecutor, Registry; **Assembly of States Parties**; Trust Fund for Victims.

- **Cases/trend:** *Lubanga* (2012 – first conviction, child soldiers), *Ntaganda*, *Ongwen*, *Al Hassan*; **arrest warrants against Vladimir Putin (March 2023), Benjamin Netanyahu and Yoav Gallant (November 2024)**; **warrants for Taliban leaders (2025)**; **Rodrigo Duterte** arrested and transferred to The Hague (March 2025). Compliance disputes (Mongolia 2024; Hungary).

- **India’s position:** participated in Rome (1998) but did not sign; objections – SC referral and deferral powers, treatment of nuclear weapons, *proprio motu* powers and jurisdiction over non-parties, absence of death penalty.

### 8.9 Human Rights Law and IHL; Emerging Issues

- **Relationship:** both apply in armed conflict; IHL is *lex specialis* (*Nuclear Weapons* AO; *Wall* AO; *DRC v Uganda*). **ICCPR Art 4:** non-derogable rights – Arts 6, 7, 8(1)–(2), 11, 15, 16, 18.

- **Emerging issues:** **lethal autonomous weapons systems** (CCW GGE; UNGA resolution in 2024); **cyber operations** (Tallinn Manual 2.0, 2017); starvation and humanitarian access; attacks on health care (SC Res 2286, 2016); explosive weapons in populated areas (Dublin Declaration, 2022); protection of cultural property; **Special Tribunal for the Crime of Aggression against Ukraine** (Council of Europe–Ukraine agreement, June 2025); **75th anniversary of the Geneva Conventions** (August 2024).

## Chapter 10: Examiner's Traps and Recent-Trend Radar

### 10.1 Frequently Confused Pairs

- **Good offices vs mediation:** good offices – third party does not take part in negotiations; mediation – third party proposes terms.

- **Arbitration vs conciliation:** award binding vs recommendation non-binding.

- **Extradition vs deportation; asylum vs refugee status; refugee vs IDP.**

- **Constitutive vs declaratory theory;** Estrada (no recognition of governments) vs Stimson (non-recognition of territory taken by force) vs Tobar (unconstitutional governments).

- **Hague law vs Geneva law;** Common Art 2 (international) vs Common Art 3 (non-international); AP I vs AP II.

- **ICC vs ICJ vs ITLOS vs PCA vs ICTY.** ICC – individuals; ICJ – States; ITLOS – law of the sea; PCA – arbitration administrator; ICTY – ad hoc.

- **Veto:** affects substantive SC matters only; abstention and absence are not vetoes; procedural matters need nine votes without veto; ICJ judge election has no veto.

- **Advisory opinions** are not binding; **interim measures** are binding; **judgments** are binding only between parties (Art 59).

- **GA vs SC:** GA recommendations are non-binding; SC Chapter VII decisions are binding (Art 25, Art 48).

- **Art 51** is an exception to Art 2(4); self-defence is available only against an ‘armed attack’ (*Nicaragua*), and Art 51 also demands reporting to the SC.

- **WTO:** MFN (Art I) vs national treatment (Art III); safeguards (Art XIX) vs anti-dumping (Art VI) vs countervailing duties (SCM); WTO ≠ UN specialised agency.

### 10.2 Mnemonics

- **Art 38 sources – ‘T-C-G-J’:** Treaty, Custom, General principles, Judicial decisions/publicists.

- **Refugee grounds – ‘RRN-PS-PO’:** Race, Religion, Nationality, Particular Social group, Political Opinion (five).

- **P5 – ‘CFRUU’:** China, France, Russia, UK, USA.

- **GC I–IV:** **L**and-wounded, **S**ea-wounded, **P**OWs, **C**ivilians – ‘LSPC’ (increasing order of coverage: 1 land, 2 sea, 3 POW, 4 civilians).

- **WTO principles:** MFN, NT, Bindings, Transparency, Exceptions, S&DT.

- **Dispute settlement (Art 33):** N-E-M-C-A-J-R-O (Negotiation, Enquiry, Mediation, Conciliation, Arbitration, Judicial settlement, Regional agencies, Other).

### 10.4 How to Attempt MCQs from This Unit

1.  **Eliminate by articles:** many options are wrong because they swap Art 51 (self-defence) with Art 2(4) (use of force), Art 33 with Art 36 etc. Memorise article numbers with their headings.

2.  **‘Binding vs non-binding’ trap:** GA resolutions, advisory opinions, Red Notices, soft law – non-binding; SC Chapter VII decisions, ICJ judgments between parties, interim measures – binding.

3.  **India-specific hooks:** India is *not* a party to the Refugee Convention, Rome Statute, AP I/II, Ottawa, CCM, TPNW, NPT, VCLT 1969; India *is* a party to Geneva Conventions 1949, Genocide Convention, CWC, BWC, CCW, UNCLOS, WTO agreements.

4.  **Assertion–Reason:** check if the reason actually explains the assertion (e.g., asylum is a State right, hence no individual right to be granted asylum – correct explanation).

5.  **Match-the-case:** link each case to a single keyword (see 9.3); *Asylum* ≠ *Haya de la Torre*; *Nottebohm* ≠ *Barcelona Traction*.

6.  **Time-box:** this unit’s questions are recall-based; spend under 40 seconds per question and use the saved time for analytical units.
