## Chapter 0: How the Exam Tests This Unit

### 0.1 Exam pattern

**Exam pattern (verify against the latest NTA notification):** UGC NET Law Paper 2 has 100 objective questions of 2 marks each (200 marks). The syllabus has 10 units, so each unit carries roughly 10 questions. Commercial Law (Unit VI) is a high-scoring, factual unit, and a well-prepared candidate can expect 8–12 questions from it.

### 0.2 Typical MCQ formats

- **Statement-based:** Statements I and II, choose the correct option. This is the most common format.

- **Match the following:** sections with topics (for example s.73 – compensation, s.74 – liquidated damages), or cases with principles.

- **Assertion–Reason:** common for doctrines such as frustration, privity, and the Turquand rule.

- **Sequence/chronology:** order of company incorporation steps, or the order of discharge of a surety.

- **Numeric/fact recall:** time limits, thresholds, and minimum numbers (for example the minimum members of a public company, or the CSR thresholds).

- **Which-is-NOT-correct:** negative questions that test exceptions.

### 0.3 Recent trends

1.  Questions increasingly test *section numbers and statutory definitions*, not only case law.

2.  Amendments are favourites: Companies (Amendment) Acts 2015, 2017, 2019, 2020; the Jan Vishwas (Amendment of Provisions) Act 2023; the LLP (Amendment) Act 2021 and LLP Rules; NI Act amendments of 2015 and 2018; the Specific Relief (Amendment) Act 2018.

3.  E-contract questions combine the IT Act 2000 with the Contract Act and the Bharatiya Sakshya Adhiniyam 2023 (the replacement of the Evidence Act).

4.  CSR questions test the exact rules: Section 135, Schedule VII, and the CSR Rules 2014 as amended in 2021.

5.  Landmark Supreme Court cases are repeated across years, so learn the *principle* attached to each case.

### 0.4 How to study each topic

## Chapter 1: Essential Elements of Contract and E-Contract

### 1.1 Background of the Indian Contract Act, 1872

The Act came into force on **1 September 1872**. It is based largely on English common law principles. Originally it also covered sale of goods and partnership, but these were carved out:

- **Sale of Goods:** formerly ss.76–123, now the **Sale of Goods Act, 1930**.

- **Partnership:** formerly ss.239–266, now the **Indian Partnership Act, 1932**.

- **Remaining structure:** ss.1–75 (general principles), ss.124–147 (indemnity and guarantee), ss.148–181 (bailment and pledge), and ss.182–238 (agency).

The Act is **not exhaustive**. Section 1 saves usages and customs of trade, and other statutes apply alongside it.

### 1.2 Key Definitions under Section 2

- **s.2(a) Proposal (offer):** a person signifies to another his willingness to do or abstain from doing anything, with a view to obtaining the other’s assent.

- **s.2(b) Promise:** a proposal when accepted.

- **s.2(c) Promisor and promisee:** the person making the proposal is the promisor, and the person accepting is the promisee.

- **s.2(d) Consideration:** when, at the desire of the promisor, the promisee or any other person has done or abstained from doing, or does or abstains, or promises to do or abstain, something. That something is a consideration for the promise.

- **s.2(e) Agreement:** every promise and every set of promises forming the consideration for each other.

- **s.2(f) Reciprocal promises:** promises which form the consideration or part of the consideration for each other.

- **s.2(g) Void agreement:** an agreement not enforceable by law.

- **s.2(h) Contract:** an agreement enforceable by law.

- **s.2(i) Voidable contract:** an agreement enforceable by law at the option of one or more of the parties, but not at the option of the other or others.

- **s.2(j) Void contract:** a contract which ceases to be enforceable by law.

**Formula to remember:** Agreement + Enforceability = Contract. All contracts are agreements, but not all agreements are contracts.

### 1.3 Section 10: Essentials of a Valid Contract

Section 10 says all agreements are contracts if they are made by the **free consent** of parties **competent to contract**, for a **lawful consideration** and with a **lawful object**, and are **not expressly declared void**. Writing and registration are required only where a statute demands them.

The full checklist is:

1.  Two or more parties (offer and acceptance).

2.  Intention to create legal relations.

3.  Free consent.

4.  Capacity of parties.

5.  Lawful consideration.

6.  Lawful object.

7.  Certainty and possibility of performance.

8.  Not declared void.

9.  Legal formalities, where required.

### 1.4 Offer (Proposal)

**Kinds of offer:** express or implied; general (to the world, as in *Carlill v Carbolic Smoke Ball Co.*), specific, cross-offers, counter-offers, and standing (continuing) offers (for example tenders: *Great Northern Railway v Witham*).

**Rules:**

- The offer must be capable of creating legal relations and be clear and definite.

- It must be **communicated** to the offeree. In *Lalman Shukla v Gauri Dutt* (1913), the servant who found the missing nephew could not claim the reward, because he did not know of the offer when he acted. (Contrast Carlill, where performance after knowledge sufficed.)

- An offer is different from an **invitation to treat**. Examples: price lists, advertisements, goods displayed in shops (*Pharmaceutical Society v Boots*), catalogues, auction bids, and the statement in *Harvey v Facey* (“lowest price for Bumper Hall Pen”), which was only information. In an auction, the bid is the offer and the fall of the hammer is acceptance (s.64 Sale of Goods Act).

- A counter-offer destroys the original offer (*Hyde v Wrench*). A mere request for information is not a counter-offer (*Stevenson v McLean*).

**Revocation of an offer (ss.5 and 6):** may be revoked at any time before acceptance is complete as against the proposer. Modes (s.6): by communication of notice of revocation; by lapse of time (stipulated or reasonable); by failure to fulfil a condition precedent; by death or insanity of the proposer (if the acceptor comes to know of it before acceptance); by counter-offer; by rejection.

### 1.5 Acceptance (s.7, s.8)

- Acceptance must be **absolute and unqualified**, and expressed in the **manner prescribed**; if no manner is prescribed, in a usual and reasonable manner.

- **Silence is not acceptance** (*Felthouse v Bindley*). The offeror cannot impose acceptance by silence.

- Only the person to whom the offer is made can accept it, except in a general offer.

- **Acceptance by conduct** (s.8): performing the conditions of a proposal, or accepting consideration for a reciprocal promise, is acceptance.

- Acceptance must be given **before the offer lapses or is revoked**, and within a reasonable time.

**Communication (s.4):** the communication of a proposal is complete when it comes to the knowledge of the person to whom it is made. Acceptance is complete **as against the proposer** when put in a course of transmission to him, so as to be out of the power of the acceptor to withdraw it. As against the acceptor it is complete when it comes to the knowledge of the proposer. The same logic applies to revocation.

**Postal rule:** *Adams v Lindsell* and *Household Fire Insurance v Grant*: acceptance by post is complete on posting. **Instantaneous communication** (telephone, telex, e-mail): the contract is made where and when the acceptance is *received*, per *Entores Ltd v Miles Far East Corp.* (English Court of Appeal) and followed by the Supreme Court in *Bhagwandas Goverdhandas Kedia v Girdharilal Parshottamdas* (1966).

### 1.6 Intention to Create Legal Relations

The statute does not mention it expressly, but courts apply the English presumptions. **Social and domestic agreements** are presumed not to be intended as legal (*Balfour v Balfour*), though the presumption can be rebutted (*Merritt v Merritt*). **Commercial agreements** are presumed legal, unless there is an “honour clause” (*Rose and Frank Co. v Crompton Bros.*). Advertisements with mere puffery are not contracts, but those with a serious promise are (*Carlill*).

### 1.7 Capacity to Contract (ss.11, 12)

Section 11: every person is competent to contract who (a) is of the **age of majority** under the law to which he is subject, (b) is of **sound mind**, and (c) is **not disqualified** by any law.

**Minor** (Indian Majority Act 1875): below 18 years, or below 21 where a guardian of the person or property has been appointed by a court or the minor’s property is under the Court of Wards.

- **Mohori Bibee v Dharmodas Ghose** (Privy Council, 1903): a minor’s agreement is **void ab initio**. Hence it cannot be ratified after majority.

- There is **no estoppel** against a minor, and a minor cannot be made liable even if he misrepresented his age. Restitution may be ordered only where the minor himself seeks relief and has obtained property by fraud (Specific Relief Act 1963, s.33, and equitable restitution).

- A minor may be a **promisee or beneficiary** (*Raghava Chariar v Srinivasa Raghava*).

- A minor’s estate is liable for **necessaries** supplied (s.68). The minor is not personally liable.

- **Guardian’s contracts** for the minor’s benefit are enforceable (*Mir Sarwarjan v Fakhruddin*).

- A minor cannot be a **partner**, but may be admitted to the benefits of partnership (s.30, Partnership Act). A minor may be an agent, and the principal is liable (s.184), but the minor is not personally liable.

**Sound mind (s.12):** capable of understanding the contract and forming a rational judgment of its effect on his interests at the time of making it. A person usually of unsound mind may contract during lucid intervals. A usually sound person cannot contract when delirious, drunk, or so ill that he cannot understand.

**Disqualified persons:** alien enemies, foreign sovereigns and ambassadors (partly), convicts (during sentence), insolvents, and corporations beyond their objects (ultra vires).

### 1.8 Free Consent (ss.13–22)

**s.13:** two or more persons consent when they agree upon the same thing in the same sense (*consensus ad idem*). **s.14:** consent is free when it is not caused by coercion, undue influence, fraud, misrepresentation, or mistake. **s.19:** an agreement caused by coercion, fraud or misrepresentation is **voidable** at the option of the party whose consent was so caused. **s.19A:** same for undue influence (with power of court to set aside on terms). Mistake, by contrast, renders an agreement void (bilateral) or has no effect (unilateral).

**Coercion (s.15):** committing or threatening to commit any act forbidden by the Indian Penal Code (now the Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita, 2023), or unlawfully detaining or threatening to detain any property, with the intention of causing any person to enter into an agreement.

- It is irrelevant whether the IPC/BNS is in force where coercion is employed.

- Coercion may come from a stranger to the contract and be directed at any person (*Ranganayakamma v Alwar Setti*: a widow was forced to adopt a boy by threatening to prevent the husband’s cremation).

- A threat to commit suicide is coercion (*Chikham Amiraju v Chikham Seshama*).

- A threat to file a civil suit is *not* coercion.

**Undue influence (s.16):** a contract is induced by undue influence where the relations between the parties are such that one is in a position to **dominate the will** of the other and uses that position to obtain an **unfair advantage**.

- Presumed relationships: parent and child, guru and disciple, doctor and patient, solicitor and client, trustee and beneficiary, fiancé and fiancée (not husband and wife by default).

- Where the dominant party holds a real or apparent authority, or the other is weak by age, illness or mental distress, the burden is on the dominant party to prove the contract was not induced by undue influence (s.16(3)). In an **unconscionable bargain**, the burden is on the stronger party.

- Cases: *Lloyds Bank v Bundy*; *Raghunath Prasad v Sarju Prasad* (moneylender, unconscionable interest); *Subhas Chandra Das Mushib v Ganga Prasad Das* (1967); *Ladli Prasad Jaiswal v Karnal Distillery* (1963).

- **Pardanashin women:** the contract must be proved to have been explained to and understood by them.

**Fraud (s.17):** includes any of the following acts committed by a party, or with his connivance, or by his agent, with intent to deceive another party or to induce him to enter into the contract: (1) suggestion as a fact of that which is not true by one who does not believe it true, (2) active concealment of a fact by one having knowledge or belief of it, (3) a promise made without any intention of performing it, (4) any other act fitted to deceive, and (5) any such act or omission as the law specially declares to be fraudulent.

- **Mere silence is not fraud** unless there is a duty to speak or the silence is equivalent to speech (Explanation to s.17).

- Contracts *uberrimae fidei* (insurance, family settlements, partnership, company prospectus, guarantee) require full disclosure.

- *Derry v Peek*: fraud is a false statement made knowingly, without belief in its truth, or recklessly.

- The defrauded party may rescind and claim damages. Under s.19, a party induced by misrepresentation cannot avoid the contract if he had the means of discovering the truth with ordinary diligence, but this exception does not protect a fraudster.

**Misrepresentation (s.18):** (1) a positive assertion in a manner not warranted by the information of the person making it, though he believes it true; (2) any breach of duty which gains an advantage to the person committing it without intent to deceive; (3) causing, however innocently, a party to make a mistake as to the substance of the thing which is the subject of the agreement.

**Mistake (ss.20–22):**

- **s.20 (bilateral mistake of fact essential to the agreement):** the agreement is **void**. Examples: *Raffles v Wichelhaus* (the *Peerless* case, two ships of the same name), *Couturier v Hastie* (non-existent goods), *Cooper v Phibbs* (mistaken ownership), *Cundy v Lindsay* (mistake as to identity).

- **s.21:** mistake as to a **law not in force in India** is treated as mistake of fact. A mistake as to a **law in force in India** has the same effect as ignorance of law and does **not** make the contract voidable.

- **s.22:** a contract is not voidable merely because it was caused by one party’s **unilateral** mistake as to a matter of fact.

- *Tarsem Singh v Sukhminder Singh* (1998): the mistake must be as to a fact essential to the agreement.

### 1.9 Lawful Consideration and Object

**Features of Indian consideration (s.2(d)):**

- Moves **at the desire of the promisor**.

- May move from the promisee **or any other person**. Thus a **stranger to consideration** may sue (*Chinnaya v Ramayya*, Madras HC, 1882; *Durga Prasad v Baldeo*), but a **stranger to the contract** cannot (privity).

- **Past consideration** is good consideration in India (“has done or abstained”), unlike England. Cases: *Sindha v Abraham*; *Kedarnath v Gorie Mohamed*.

- Consideration must be **real**, not illusory, but need not be **adequate** (Explanation 2 to s.25). Inadequacy may be relevant to free consent.

- Consideration must not be an **already existing legal duty** (pre-existing duty rule, partly relaxed in *Williams v Roffey Bros*).

**s.25 (agreement without consideration is void), with three exceptions:**

1.  A **written and registered** agreement made on account of **natural love and affection** between parties standing in a near relation to each other.

2.  A promise to **compensate for something done voluntarily** (past voluntary service) for the promisor.

3.  A **written, signed promise to pay a time-barred debt** (s.25(3)).

Other cases needing no consideration: completed **gift** (Transfer of Property Act), gratuitous **bailment**, **agency** (s.185), charitable subscriptions (when acted upon), and **remission/accord** (s.63).

**Privity of contract:** only parties to a contract can sue or be sued (*Tweddle v Guest*; *Dunlop Pneumatic Tyre v Selfridge*). In India: *Jamna Das v Ram Autar* (Privy Council). **Exceptions:** trust, marriage settlements, family arrangements and partition, acknowledgment/estoppel, assignment, covenants running with land, agency, statutory rights (insurance beneficiary), and *M.C. Chacko v State Bank of Travancore* (1969), where a beneficiary was allowed to enforce a contract because of a trust-like arrangement.

**Unlawful consideration/object (s.23):** forbidden by law; of such a nature that, if permitted, it would defeat the provisions of any law; fraudulent; involving or implying injury to the person or property of another; or the court regards it as **immoral or opposed to public policy**. Such agreements are **void**.

- *Gherulal Parakh v Mahadeodas Maiya* (1959): public policy should be applied narrowly, and wagering agreements are void but not illegal.

- *Central Inland Water Transport Corp. v Brojo Nath Ganguly* (1986): unconscionable and unreasonable contract terms are opposed to public policy.

- *ONGC v Saw Pipes Ltd.* (2003): “patent illegality” as a ground to set aside an award under public policy; later narrowed in *Associate Builders v DDA* (2015), and now governed by s.34(2A) of the Arbitration and Conciliation Act, 1996.

- Examples of immoral: marriage brokerage contracts, sexual immorality. Public policy: stifling prosecution, trafficking in public offices, champerty and maintenance (limited).

**Void by express provision (ss.24–30):**

- **s.24:** consideration or object partly unlawful. If the lawful part is separable (**severability**), it is enforceable; otherwise the whole is void.

- **s.26:** agreements in restraint of marriage (except of minors).

- **s.27:** agreements in **restraint of trade** are void, with the sole statutory exception of **sale of goodwill** within specified local limits. Cases: *Madhub Chunder v Raj Coomar Dass*; *Nordenfelt v Maxim Nordenfelt Guns* (English); *Niranjan Shankar Golikari v Century Spinning* (1967: a negative covenant during the term of employment is valid); *Percept D’Mark v Zaheer Khan* (2006: post-term restraint void); *Superintendence Co. of India v Krishan Murgai* (1981).

- **s.28:** agreements in restraint of legal proceedings (absolutely restricting remedies, or curtailing limitation periods). Exceptions: arbitration agreements and agreements to refer existing disputes. Courts uphold **exclusive jurisdiction clauses** where more than one court has jurisdiction (*Hakam Singh v Gammon (India) Ltd.*, 1971; *ABC Laminart v A.P. Agencies*, 1989). Section 28 was amended in 1997.

- **s.29:** agreements whose meaning is uncertain.

- **s.30:** **wagering agreements** are void; collateral transactions are not void (*Gherulal Parakh*). Exception: prizes for horse races above Rs. 500 (s.294A IPC, now covered under BNS). Some states (e.g., Maharashtra, Gujarat) have stricter amendments making wagers illegal.

### 1.10 Contingent Contracts (ss.31–36)

A **contingent contract** depends on an event collateral to the contract (s.31). Enforcement: on the happening of the event (s.32), on its non-happening (s.35), and a contract contingent on an impossible event is void (s.36). s.33 deals with events happening in a certain way, and s.34 with the conduct of a living person. *Certainty* (s.29) and *possibility* (s.56) are separate requirements. Insurance and indemnity are classic contingent contracts, while a wager is *not* contingent because the event is not collateral.

### 1.11 Formalities

No general requirement of writing or registration. Specific statutory requirements: s.25(1) registration; arbitration agreements in writing (Arbitration Act s.7); Registration Act 1908, s.17 (immovable property above Rs. 100); Transfer of Property Act (sale, mortgage, lease, gift); Negotiable Instruments Act (written and signed).

### 1.12 E-Contracts

An **e-contract** is a contract formed and executed through electronic means (the internet, e-mail, apps, EDI) rather than on paper. The general law of contract applies equally to it.

**Types:** **click-wrap** (the user clicks ‘I agree’), **browse-wrap** (terms linked on the site, weakest enforceability), **shrink-wrap** (terms inside software packaging), **e-mail contracts**, **EDI** (electronic data interchange), and smart contracts on a blockchain.

**Information Technology Act, 2000 (amended 2008)** is based on the **UNCITRAL Model Law on Electronic Commerce, 1996**. Key provisions:

- **s.2(1)(t):** definition of ‘electronic record’.

- **s.3 and s.3A:** authentication by digital signature and electronic signature.

- **s.4:** legal recognition of electronic records.

- **s.5:** legal recognition of electronic signatures.

- **s.10A (inserted in 2008):** contracts formed through electronic means are not unenforceable *solely* because electronic form or means were used for the proposal, acceptance or revocation.

- **s.11–13:** attribution, acknowledgment and the **time and place of dispatch and receipt**. Dispatch occurs when the record enters a computer resource outside the originator’s control. Receipt occurs when it enters the designated computer resource of the addressee, or if none is designated, when the addressee retrieves it. Place is the place of business of the originator (dispatch) and the addressee (receipt).

- **First Schedule (s.1(4)):** the Act does not apply to negotiable instruments (other than cheques), powers of attorney, trusts, wills, and contracts for the sale or conveyance of immovable property.

- **s.75:** extra-territorial application.

**Evidentiary position:** earlier s.65B of the Evidence Act governed electronic evidence (*Anvar P.V. v P.K. Basheer*, 2014; *Arjun Panditrao Khotkar v Kailash Kushanrao Gorantyal*, 2020, which held that the s.65B certificate is mandatory). Under the **Bharatiya Sakshya Adhiniyam, 2023** (in force from 1 July 2024) the corresponding provision is **s.63**.

**Key cases:**

- **Trimex International FZE Ltd. v Vedanta Aluminium Ltd.** (2010): a contract may be concluded through e-mail exchange even without a formal signed document.

- **Shakti Bhog Foods v Kola Shipping** (2009): an arbitration agreement can be concluded by electronic communication.

- **P.R. Transport Agency v Union of India** (Allahabad HC, 2006): an e-mail acceptance concludes the contract at the place where it is received.

- **Bhagwandas Kedia** (1966): the rule for instantaneous communication.

**Related legislation:** Consumer Protection (E-Commerce) Rules, 2020; Digital Personal Data Protection Act, 2023; UN Convention on the Use of Electronic Communications in International Contracts (2005); UNCITRAL Model Law on Electronic Signatures (2001).

## Chapter 2: Breach, Frustration, Void and Voidable Agreements

### 2.1 Void Agreement, Void Contract, Voidable Contract and Illegal Agreement

| Feature                 | Void agreement                                | Void contract                                  | Voidable contract                                              | Illegal agreement                         |
|-------------------------|-----------------------------------------------|------------------------------------------------|----------------------------------------------------------------|-------------------------------------------|
| Definition              | s.2(g): not enforceable by law from the start | s.2(j): contract that ceases to be enforceable | s.2(i): enforceable at the option of one party                 | Forbidden by law or against public policy |
| Legal effect            | No legal effect from inception                | Valid at first, later void                     | Valid until avoided                                            | Void, with penal consequences             |
| Examples                | Minor’s agreement, wager, restraint of trade  | Frustration under s.56, contingent event fails | Consent by coercion, undue influence, fraud, misrepresentation | Contract to commit a crime                |
| Collateral transactions | Enforceable, with some exceptions             | n/a                                            | n/a                                                            | Collateral transactions generally tainted |

**Agreements declared void** by the Act: s.11 (incompetent party), s.20 (bilateral mistake), s.23 (unlawful object/consideration), s.24 (partly unlawful and inseparable), s.25 (no consideration), s.26 (restraint of marriage), s.27 (restraint of trade), s.28 (restraint of legal proceedings), s.29 (uncertainty), s.30 (wager), s.36 (contingent on impossible event), s.56 para 1 (agreement to do an impossible act).

**Voidable by statute:** s.19 and 19A (consent not free), s.39 (repudiation by a party), s.53 (prevention of performance by one party), s.55 (failure to perform at a time fixed when time is of the essence).

**Consequences:**

- **s.64:** a party rescinding a voidable contract must **restore any benefit received** to the other party.

- **s.65:** when an agreement is discovered to be void, or a contract becomes void, any person who has received any advantage must **restore it or make compensation**. See *Kuju Collieries Ltd. v Jharkhand Mines Ltd.* (1974). Note that in *Mohori Bibee* restitution under s.65 was refused, because s.65 applies to persons competent to contract.

- **Rights of third parties:** a bona fide purchaser for value without notice acquires good title before rescission.

- **Rescission** may be lost by affirmation, lapse of time, intervention of third-party rights, or impossibility of restitutio in integrum.

### 2.2 Discharge of Contract

1.  **By performance** (ss.37–67): both parties must perform or offer to perform.

    - **Tender (s.38):** an offer of performance that is unconditional, made at the proper time and place, with a reasonable opportunity to inspect the goods. A valid tender of performance, if refused, relieves the promisor of liability for non-performance but not of the promise itself. A valid tender of money does not discharge the debt.

    - **Joint promisors (ss.42–45).**

    - **Appropriation of payments (ss.59–61):** the debtor can direct the appropriation; if not, the creditor; if neither, in the order of time.

    - **Reciprocal promises (ss.51–54):** performance in the order specified; where one is dependent on the other, the party at fault must compensate.

    - **s.55 (time as the essence):** if time is of the essence and the promisor fails, the contract is voidable at the promisee’s option. If not of the essence, the promisee can claim compensation only. In contracts for sale of immovable property, time is presumed not to be of the essence (*Hind Construction Contractors v State of Maharashtra*, 1979; *Gomathinayagam Pillai v Palaniswami Nadar*, 1967).

2.  **By mutual agreement** (ss.62–63): **novation** (substitution of a new contract), **rescission** (cancellation), **alteration**, **remission** (acceptance of lesser performance or satisfaction), **waiver**, **accord and satisfaction**.

3.  **By impossibility** (s.56), discussed below.

4.  **By lapse of time**, under the Limitation Act, 1963.

5.  **By operation of law:** death (in personal contracts), insolvency, merger, and material alteration of a written document.

6.  **By breach** (s.39 and following).

### 2.3 Frustration of Contract: Section 56

**Text of s.56:**

- **Para 1:** An agreement to do an act impossible in itself is **void**.

- **Para 2:** A contract to do an act which, after the contract is made, becomes **impossible**, or, by reason of some event which the promisor could not prevent, **unlawful**, becomes **void** when the act becomes impossible or unlawful.

- **Para 3:** Where one person has promised to do something which he knew, or with reasonable diligence might have known, and which the promisee did not know, to be impossible or unlawful, such promisor must **compensate** the promisee for any loss through the non-performance.

**Indian position:** the doctrine is a **rule of positive law** under s.56, not a rule based on an implied term. Unlike English law (where frustration was developed by the courts and is regulated by the **Law Reform (Frustrated Contracts) Act, 1943**), the Indian statute governs, and s.65 provides restitution.

**Leading cases:**

- **Taylor v Caldwell** (1863): music hall destroyed by fire before the concerts. Origin of the doctrine.

- **Krell v Henry** (1903): rooms hired for viewing the coronation procession, which was cancelled. The foundation of the contract was frustrated.

- **Herne Bay Steam Boat Co. v Hutton** (1903): hire of a boat to see the naval review. Not frustrated as the boat could still cruise.

- **Satyabrata Ghose v Mugneeram Bangur & Co.** (1954): Supreme Court: s.56 is wider than the English rule; ‘impossible’ includes **impracticable and useless** from the point of view of the object and purpose of the parties. The test is the **destruction of the foundation** of the contract.

- **Naihati Jute Mills v Khyaliram Jagannath** (1968): mere commercial hardship or difficulty is not frustration.

- **Alopi Parshad & Sons v Union of India** (1960): increase in cost does not frustrate a contract.

- **Sushila Devi v Hari Singh** (1971): migration after Partition made performance impossible.

- **Energy Watchdog v CERC** (2017): a rise in coal prices and a change in Indonesian regulation did not frustrate the power purchase agreement; **force majeure** clauses are dealt with under s.32 and are distinct from s.56.

- **Halliburton Offshore Services v Vedanta Ltd.** (Delhi HC, 2020): Covid-19 force majeure claims are examined on the contract wording and cannot excuse non-performance that occurred before the event.

**Grounds for frustration:** destruction of the subject-matter; non-occurrence of a contemplated event; death or incapacity of a party in personal-service contracts; change of law or governmental intervention; outbreak of war.

**Where the doctrine does NOT apply:** self-induced frustration; mere hardship, delay or increased cost; a contract expressly providing for the risk (force majeure clauses); **leases** (a transfer of property, per *Raja Dhruv Dev Chand v Raja Harmohinder Singh*, 1968); and where the event was foreseeable.

**Initial vs supervening impossibility:** initial impossibility (para 1) renders the agreement void from the start; supervening impossibility (para 2) makes a valid contract void *later*.

### 2.4 Breach of Contract

**Meaning:** failure or refusal to perform contractual obligations without lawful excuse.

**Kinds:**

- **Actual breach:** on the due date of performance, or during performance.

- **Anticipatory breach (s.39):** a party, before the due date, refuses wholly to perform or disables himself. The promisee may **treat the contract as repudiated** and sue at once, or **wait until the due date** (affirm) and keep the contract alive (*Hochster v De La Tour*, 1853; *Frost v Knight*, 1872). By electing to wait, the promisee risks supervening events and still has the duty to mitigate.

**Remedies for breach:**

1.  **Rescission** (s.39, s.75): the injured party may rescind and claim compensation (s.75).

2.  **Damages (s.73):** compensation for any loss or damage caused which naturally arose in the usual course of things or which the parties knew, when they made the contract, to be likely to result. Compensation is not given for remote and indirect loss. The **rule in *Hadley v Baxendale* (1854)** underlies this: damages are limited to (a) those arising naturally and (b) those within the reasonable contemplation of both parties at the time of contract. The injured party has a **duty to mitigate** (Explanation).

3.  **Liquidated damages and penalty (s.74):** where a sum is named as payable on breach, or a penalty is stipulated, the party complaining is entitled to **reasonable compensation not exceeding the amount named**, whether or not actual damage is proved. The English distinction between penalty and liquidated damages is not followed.

    - *Fateh Chand v Balkishan Dass* (1963): s.74 applies to both; the court awards reasonable compensation; **forfeiture of earnest money** beyond a reasonable amount is a penalty.

    - *Maula Bux v Union of India* (1969): forfeiture of earnest money/security deposit.

    - *ONGC v Saw Pipes* (2003): the party claiming must prove the loss unless the loss is impossible to prove.

    - *Kailash Nath Associates v DDA* (2015): the Supreme Court summarised the s.74 principles.

4.  **Specific performance:** governed by the **Specific Relief Act, 1963**, as amended by the **Specific Relief (Amendment) Act, 2018** (in force 1 October 2018). Specific performance is now a **general rule rather than a discretionary remedy**. New s.20 provides **substituted performance**; s.20A sets out special provisions for infrastructure projects (courts cannot grant injunctions that obstruct them); s.20B designates special courts.

5.  **Injunction:** preventing a breach of a negative covenant (Specific Relief Act, Chapter VII).

6.  **Quantum meruit** (‘as much as he has earned’): a claim for reasonable remuneration for work done where the contract is discharged by breach or is incomplete (*Alopi Parshad*; s.70 as a quasi-contract).

7.  **Restitution** (ss.64, 65, 72).

**Types of damages:** *nominal* (breach with no loss), *ordinary or general*, *special* (foreseeable by the parties), *exemplary/vindictive* (generally **not** awarded in contract except for breach of promise to marry and wrongful dishonour of a cheque by a bank), and *damages for mental agony* (rare).

**Remoteness:** *Hadley v Baxendale* (mill-shaft case), *Victoria Laundry v Newman*, *Czarnikow v Koufos (The Heron II)*. In India: *Murlidhar Chiranjilal v Harishchandra Dwarkadas* (1962).

## Chapter 3: Standard Form of Contract and Quasi-Contract

### 3.1 Standard Form Contracts (Contracts of Adhesion)

**Meaning:** a contract whose terms are drafted in advance by one party (usually the stronger, such as a corporation, bank, insurer, airline, telecom operator or website) and offered on a **‘take it or leave it’** basis. The other party can only adhere and has no real chance to negotiate. Examples: railway tickets, insurance policies, bank loan forms, bills of lading, airline tickets, software licences, app terms and conditions.

**Features:** pre-printed or digital; uniform; non-negotiable; often contain exemption/exclusion clauses; unequal bargaining power; efficient for mass transactions.

**Advantages:** saves time and cost, uniformity, certainty, ease of administration, standard risk allocation.

**Disadvantages:** unfair and one-sided terms, hidden clauses, limitation of liability, unequal bargaining power, no meaningful consent.

**Judicial control:**

- **Notice rule:** the terms must be brought to the notice of the other party *before or at* the time of contract (*Parker v South Eastern Railway*; *Thornton v Shoe Lane Parking*; *Olley v Marlborough Court*).

- **Signature rule:** a person who signs is bound, even if he has not read (*L’Estrange v Graucob*), unless there is fraud or misrepresentation (*Curtis v Chemical Cleaning*).

- **Contra proferentem:** ambiguities are construed against the party who drafted them.

- **Doctrine of fundamental breach:** attempted by Lord Denning, but the House of Lords held in *Suisse Atlantique* (1967) and *Photo Production v Securicor* (1980) that it is a rule of construction, not of law.

- **Unreasonable and unconscionable terms:** *Central Inland Water Transport Corp. v Brojo Nath Ganguly* (1986) applied Article 14 to a standard-form service rule and declared it unconscionable and void under s.23 as against public policy. Also *Delhi Transport Corporation v DTC Mazdoor Congress* (1991) and *LIC of India v Consumer Education and Research Centre* (1995).

- **Bharathi Knitting Co. v DHL Worldwide Express Courier** (1996): a party who signs a standard form is bound by its terms.

- **Lily White v Munuswami** (Madras HC, 1966): a laundry’s limitation of liability on a ticket was held reasonable.

**Statutory control in India:**

- **Indian Contract Act:** ss.16, 23, 28 and 74.

- **Consumer Protection Act, 2019** (in force 20 July 2020): **s.2(46)** defines ‘**unfair contract**’ (terms causing significant change in the rights of consumers, such as excessive security deposits, disproportionate penalty, refusing to accept early repayment, unilateral termination without reasonable cause, or assignment to a third party to the consumer’s detriment). The **State Commission and National Commission** may declare unfair terms **null and void** (ss.49(2) and 59(2)).

- **Unfair trade practice** (s.2(47)) and **Consumer Protection (E-Commerce) Rules, 2020**.

- Law Commission of India, **199th Report (2006)** on ‘Unfair (Procedural and Substantive) Terms in Contract’ recommended a separate statute (not enacted).

### 3.2 Quasi-Contract (Sections 68–72): ‘Certain Relations Resembling Those Created by Contract’

**Concept:** a quasi-contract is not a real contract. There is no offer, acceptance or agreement; the **law imposes an obligation** to prevent **unjust enrichment** (no person should be enriched at the expense of another). The Act places it in Chapter V.

| Section  | Situation                                                                                                                                    | Rule                                                                                                       |
|----------|----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------|------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------|
| **s.68** | Supply of **necessaries** to a person incapable of contracting (minor, person of unsound mind) or to one whom he is legally bound to support | Supplier is entitled to be reimbursed from the **property** of the incapable person; no personal liability |
| **s.69** | Payment by a person **interested** in the payment of money which another is **bound by law to pay**                                          | Entitled to be reimbursed by the other                                                                     |
| **s.70** | **Non-gratuitous act** done lawfully for another, who enjoys the benefit                                                                     | The enjoyer must **compensate** or restore                                                                 |
| **s.71** | **Finder of goods**                                                                                                                          | Subject to the same responsibility as a **bailee**                                                         |
| **s.72** | Money paid or thing delivered by **mistake or under coercion**                                                                               | Must be **repaid or returned**                                                                             |

**Section 70, conditions (very frequently tested):**

1.  A person must lawfully do something for another or deliver something to him.

2.  He must not do so **gratuitously**.

3.  The other person must **enjoy the benefit** of it.

Cases: *State of West Bengal v B.K. Mondal & Sons* (1962: the benefit must be accepted voluntarily, not forced); *Mulamchand v State of Madhya Pradesh* (1968); *Damodara Mudaliar v Secretary of State*; *Alopi Parshad*.

**Section 72 cases:** *Sales Tax Officer v Kanhaiya Lal Makund Lal Saraf* (1959: tax paid under mistake of law is recoverable, and the State can be made to refund); *Mafatlal Industries v Union of India* (1997: refund of excise duty is subject to the principle of **unjust enrichment**, and where the burden has been passed on to consumers the claim fails).

**s.71 (finder of goods):** the finder takes on a bailee’s obligations (ss.151–152, 154). He may retain the goods against the owner until lawful charges are paid, and may sue for a reward if one was publicly offered (s.168). If the owner cannot be found, the finder may sell only if the goods are perishable or the lawful charges amount to two-thirds of their value (s.169).

**Contract vs quasi-contract:**

- A contract arises from consent; a quasi-contract arises by operation of law.

- A contract requires competent parties and an agreement; a quasi-contract does not.

- In a contract, damages are measured by the loss; in a quasi-contract the claim is limited to the value of the benefit conferred.

**Related doctrines:** **unjust enrichment** (*Lipkin Gorman v Karpnale Ltd.*), **quantum meruit** (reasonable payment for work done), and **total failure of consideration** (*Fibrosa Spolka Akcyjna v Fairbairn Lawson Combe Barbour Ltd.*, 1943).

## Chapter 4: Specific Contracts: Bailment, Pledge, Indemnity, Guarantee and Agency

**Where they sit in the Indian Contract Act, 1872:** Indemnity and Guarantee = ss.124–147; Bailment = ss.148–171; Pledge = ss.172–179; Agency = ss.182–238. (Chapter VIII covers bailment and pledge; Chapter X covers agency.)

### 4.1 Bailment (ss.148–171)

**Definition (s.148):** the delivery of goods by one person to another for some purpose, upon a contract that they shall, when the purpose is accomplished, be returned or otherwise disposed of according to the directions of the person delivering them. The delivering person is the **bailor**; the person to whom goods are delivered is the **bailee**.

**Essentials:** (1) a contract (express or implied), (2) **delivery of possession** (not ownership), (3) goods only (movable property; not money meant to be spent, not immovables), (4) for a purpose, and (5) the obligation to return or dispose according to directions. **Delivery (s.149)** may be actual or constructive (doing anything with the effect of putting the goods in possession of the bailee or any person authorised to hold them on his behalf).

**Kinds:** gratuitous bailment (no reward) and bailment for reward; also bailment for the benefit of the bailor, of the bailee, or of both. Examples: loan for use, deposit, hire, pawn, carriage, repair.

**Duties of the bailor:**

- **s.150:** disclose known faults in the goods that materially interfere with their use or expose the bailee to risk. In gratuitous bailment the bailor is liable for damage if he knew of the faults and did not disclose them. In bailment for hire, the bailor is liable **whether or not he knew** of the faults.

- **s.158:** to repay necessary expenses (gratuitous bailment) and extraordinary expenses.

- **s.164:** to indemnify the bailee for loss arising from the bailor’s defective title.

**Duties of the bailee:**

- **s.151:** take as much care of the goods as a man of ordinary prudence would take of his own goods of the same bulk, quality and value. **s.152:** the bailee who takes such care is not liable for loss, destruction or deterioration, in the absence of a special contract.

- **s.153:** the bailor may terminate the bailment if the bailee does an act inconsistent with its terms.

- **s.154:** the bailee who makes unauthorised use is liable for damage arising from it.

- **ss.155–157 (mixture of goods):** with consent, the parties share in proportion; without consent but separable, the bailee bears the expense of separation and any damage; without consent and inseparable, the bailee must compensate the bailor for the loss.

- **s.160:** return or deliver according to the bailor’s directions when the time has expired or the purpose has been accomplished. **s.161:** if the bailee fails to return on time, he is responsible for loss even without negligence.

- **s.163:** deliver any **increase or profit** accruing from the goods (accretions), unless otherwise agreed.

- **s.165–166:** bailee’s delivery to one of several joint owners, and to the bailor without title.

**Termination:** s.153 (inconsistent act), s.162 (death of bailor or bailee in gratuitous bailment), and completion of purpose or expiry of time.

**Rights of the bailee:** s.168 (finder’s rights to compensation and to sue for reward offered), **s.170 particular lien** (retain goods bailed to him for work done on them till he is paid), **s.171 general lien** (bankers, factors, wharfingers, attorneys of High Court and policy-brokers may retain goods bailed to them as a security for a general balance of account, unless there is a contract to the contrary). **s.169:** the finder may sell if the owner cannot be found with reasonable diligence, if the goods are perishable, or if the lawful charges equal two-thirds of the value.

**Special bailees:** carriers (Carriers Act, 1865: a common carrier is liable as an insurer subject to statutory exceptions); railways (Railways Act, 1989); innkeepers; banks keeping lockers.

**Bailment vs sale:** in bailment ownership remains with the bailor and the same goods must be returned; in sale ownership passes to the buyer. **Bailment vs pledge:** a pledge is a bailment made as security for a debt.

### 4.2 Pledge (ss.172–179)

**Definition (s.172):** the bailment of goods as **security for payment of a debt or performance of a promise**. The bailor is the **pawnor (pledgor)** and the bailee is the **pawnee (pledgee)**.

**Essentials:** goods (movable), delivery of possession (actual or constructive), a debt or promise to be secured, and a contract.

**Rights of the pawnee:**

- **s.173:** retain the goods for payment of the debt, interest, and necessary expenses.

- **s.174:** no right to retain for any debt other than the one pledged, unless there is a contract to the contrary (but presumed for subsequent advances).

- **s.175:** claim extraordinary expenses incurred for preservation.

- **s.176:** on default, the pawnee may (a) sue on the debt and retain the goods as collateral, or (b) **sell the goods after giving the pawnor reasonable notice**. If the proceeds are less than the debt, the pawnor remains liable for the balance; any surplus must be paid to the pawnor.

**Right of the pawnor (s.177):** to **redeem** at any time before actual sale, on payment of the debt, interest and expenses incurred owing to the default. Cases: *Lallan Prasad v Rahmat Ali* (1967: the pawnee’s sale without notice is not valid); *Bank of Bihar v Damodar Prasad* (1969).

**Pledge by non-owners (exceptions to the rule *nemo dat*):**

- **s.178:** by a **mercantile agent** in possession of goods or documents of title with the owner’s consent, acting in the ordinary course of business, if the pawnee acts in good faith without notice.

- **s.178A:** by a person in possession under a **voidable contract** (if the contract has not been rescinded before the pledge and the pawnee acts in good faith without notice).

- **s.179:** by a pawnor having a **limited interest** (to the extent of that interest).

- A pledge by a **co-owner in possession** with the consent of the others, and by a **seller in possession** after sale.

**Pledge vs mortgage, hypothecation and lien:**

| Feature       | Pledge        | Mortgage                 | Hypothecation                          | Lien                                      |
|---------------|---------------|--------------------------|----------------------------------------|-------------------------------------------|
| Subject       | Movable goods | Immovable property       | Movables, possession stays with debtor | Goods in the possession of the lienholder |
| Possession    | Transferred   | Not necessarily          | Not transferred                        | Retained                                  |
| Nature        | Security      | Transfer of interest     | Charge/equitable security              | Bare right to retain                      |
| Governing law | Contract Act  | Transfer of Property Act | SARFAESI/contract                      | Contract Act, Sale of Goods Act           |

### 4.3 Contract of Indemnity (ss.124–125)

**Definition (s.124):** a contract by which one party promises to **save the other from loss caused to him by the conduct of the promisor himself, or by the conduct of any other person**. The promisor is the **indemnifier** and the promisee is the **indemnified (indemnity-holder)**.

**Nature:** two parties; a contingent contract; the liability arises on the loss. The Act’s definition is narrower than the English one because it covers only losses caused by **human conduct**; losses from fire, accident or other events not caused by human agency are not within the strict statutory definition. In *Gajanan Moreshwar v Moreshwar Madan* (Bombay HC, 1942), the court held that the Act’s provisions are not exhaustive and that equitable principles of indemnity apply, so the indemnifier must pay even before the indemnity-holder has actually paid, if the liability has become absolute. Insurance contracts (fire, marine, motor) are contracts of indemnity, but **life and personal accident insurance are not** (the loss cannot be measured).

**Rights of the indemnity-holder (s.125),** when sued: (1) all damages he is compelled to pay in a suit in respect of any matter covered by the promise; (2) all costs he is compelled to pay in bringing or defending the suit, if he acted as prudently as in his own case or under the promisor’s authority; and (3) all sums paid under the terms of a **compromise** of the suit, if not contrary to the promisor’s orders and if prudent or authorised.

### 4.4 Contract of Guarantee (ss.126–147)

**Definition (s.126):** a contract to **perform the promise, or discharge the liability, of a third person in case of his default**. The person giving the guarantee is the **surety**; the person in respect of whose default it is given is the **principal debtor**; the person to whom it is given is the **creditor**. A guarantee may be **oral or written**.

**Essentials:** three parties (principal debtor, creditor, surety); a liability which is existing or likely to arise; a promise by the surety; consideration (s.127: **anything done or any promise made for the benefit of the principal debtor** is sufficient consideration; the surety need not benefit directly); free consent, and no misrepresentation or concealment (ss.142–143). A guarantee obtained by misrepresentation or concealment of a material part of the circumstances is **invalid** (but contracts of guarantee are not contracts *uberrimae fidei* in the strict insurance sense; the creditor must disclose facts that would affect the surety’s willingness).

**Kinds:** **specific** (a single debt or transaction) and **continuing** (s.129: extends to a series of transactions, such as a bank overdraft). **Revocation of a continuing guarantee:** by notice to the creditor as to **future transactions** (s.130), and by the **death of the surety** as to future transactions in the absence of a contract to the contrary (s.131).

**Extent of liability (s.128):** the surety’s liability is **co-extensive** with that of the principal debtor, unless the contract provides otherwise. It is **immediate**, and the creditor need not first proceed against the principal debtor or realise other securities (*Bank of Bihar v Damodar Prasad*, 1969; *State Bank of India v Indexport Registered*, 1992).

**Discharge of the surety (ss.133–139):**

| Section | Mode of discharge                                                                                                                             |
|---------|-----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------|
| s.133   | **Variance** in the terms of the contract between creditor and principal debtor without the surety’s consent                                  |
| s.134   | **Release or discharge of the principal debtor** by the creditor’s contract, or by the creditor’s act or omission that legally discharges him |
| s.135   | Creditor’s **composition** with, giving time to, or promise not to sue the principal debtor, without the surety’s consent                     |
| s.139   | Creditor’s act or omission **impairing the surety’s eventual remedy** against the principal debtor                                            |
| s.141   | **Loss or parting with security** by the creditor (the surety is discharged to the extent of its value)                                       |

**Not discharging the surety:** s.136 (agreement by the creditor with a third person to give time to the principal debtor), s.137 (mere forbearance to sue), s.138 (release of one co-surety does not discharge the others, nor free him from liability to contribute), and the principal debtor’s insolvency (a discharge by operation of law).

**Rights of the surety:**

- **Against the principal debtor:** **subrogation** and indemnity on payment (s.140, s.145), the right to the benefit of the creditor’s securities (s.141).

- **Against the creditor:** to have the securities handed over on payment.

- **Against co-sureties:** **contribution** (s.146: equally, in the absence of a contract; s.147: where co-sureties are bound in different sums, equally, subject to the limit of the maximum).

**Bank guarantees and letters of credit:** a performance or bank guarantee is an **independent contract** between bank and beneficiary and generally must be honoured on demand. Courts restrain payment only for **fraud** or **irretrievable injury/special equity** (*U.P. Co-operative Federation v Singh Consultants*, 1988; *Svenska Handelsbanken v Indian Charge Chrome*, 1994; *Standard Chartered Bank v Heavy Engineering Corporation*, 2020).

**Indemnity vs guarantee:**

| Basis                    | Indemnity                                         | Guarantee                                                                |
|--------------------------|---------------------------------------------------|--------------------------------------------------------------------------|
| Parties                  | Two (indemnifier and indemnified)                 | Three (creditor, principal debtor, surety)                               |
| Contracts                | One                                               | Three (principal debt, creditor–surety, surety–debtor implied indemnity) |
| Liability                | Primary and independent                           | Secondary (arises on default)                                            |
| Right to sue third party | Indemnifier cannot ordinarily sue the third party | Surety can sue the principal debtor after payment                        |
| Nature                   | Contingent                                        | Conditional on default                                                   |

### 4.5 Contract of Agency (ss.182–238)

**Definition (s.182):** an **agent** is a person employed to do any act for another or to represent another in dealings with third persons. The person for whom the act is done is the **principal**.

**Capacity:** the principal must have attained majority and be of sound mind (s.183). Any person may be an agent, but a minor or person of unsound mind cannot be made responsible to the principal (s.184). **No consideration** is necessary (s.185).

**Kinds of agents:** general, special and universal; **mercantile agents** (factor, broker, commission agent, auctioneer, del credere agent, banker); sub-agents (s.191) and substituted agents (s.194). **Del credere agent:** for an extra commission guarantees the solvency of third parties.

**Creation of agency:** express or implied (s.187); **by necessity** (s.189, emergency, *Great Northern Railway v Swaffield*); **by ratification** (ss.196–200); **by estoppel/holding out** (s.237); by operation of law (partnership, s.18 Partnership Act); and by prior relationship (husband and wife in the matter of household necessities).

**Authority of agent:** s.188 (an agent having authority to do an act has authority to do every lawful thing necessary); s.189 (emergency); **s.190 (delegation)**: *delegatus non potest delegare*, but sub-agency is allowed where ordinary business custom, the nature of the agency requires, or purely ministerial acts.

**Ratification (ss.196–200):** where acts are done by one person on behalf of another without authority, the principal may ratify them, expressly or impliedly.

- The agent must have **purported to act for the principal**; the principal must have been in **existence** at the time of the act (*Kelner v Baxter*: a promoter cannot bind a company not yet in existence) and **competent** at ratification.

- The principal must have **full knowledge** of material facts (s.198).

- The **whole** transaction must be ratified (s.199).

- Ratification cannot be used to **injure a third person** (s.200), and it cannot be made after the time for performance has passed.

- Ratification has **retrospective effect** (relation back; *Bolton Partners v Lambert*).

**Duties of an agent:** follow the principal’s directions (s.211); act with reasonable skill and diligence (s.212); render accounts (s.213); communicate with the principal in difficulty (s.214); **no secret profit** and no dealing on his own account without disclosure (ss.215–216); pay sums received (s.218). **Rights:** remuneration (s.219), retainer (s.217), lien (s.221), indemnity for lawful acts (s.222), and indemnity for acts done in good faith (s.223).

**Termination (s.201):** by revocation by the principal, renunciation by the agent, completion of the business, death or unsoundness of mind of either, insolvency of the principal, and expiry of time. **Agency coupled with interest (s.202):** the agent has an interest in the property that is the subject matter; it cannot be terminated to the prejudice of that interest in the absence of an express contract. Revocation is not allowed after the authority has been partly exercised (s.204), and reasonable notice is needed (s.206).

**Liability to third parties:**

- **Disclosed principal:** the principal is bound, the agent is not personally liable (s.226 and s.230).

- **s.230 exceptions (agent personally liable):** contract for goods to be bought from abroad, where the agent does not disclose the principal’s name, and where the principal cannot be sued.

- **Undisclosed principal (s.231):** the third party may hold either the agent or the principal liable once the principal is discovered.

- **s.235:** a pretended agent is liable for breach of the **warranty of authority**. **s.237:** the principal is bound where he induced the third party to believe the agent’s acts were authorised (estoppel).

- **s.238:** misrepresentation or fraud by an agent affects the contract as if made by the principal. **s.229:** notice to the agent is notice to the principal.

## Chapter 5: Sale of Goods Act, 1930

**Basics:** in force from **1 July 1930**; ss.1–66; carved out of the Contract Act (old ss.76–123). The general Contract Act principles apply unless inconsistent (s.3). It does not apply to sale of immovable property.

### 5.1 Key Definitions (s.2)

- **Buyer** s.2(1); **delivery** s.2(2): voluntary transfer of possession from one person to another; **deliverable state** s.2(3); **document of title** s.2(4); **fault** s.2(5); **future goods** s.2(6): goods to be manufactured, produced or acquired by the seller after making the contract.

- **Goods** s.2(7): every kind of **movable property other than actionable claims and money**; includes **stock and shares, growing crops, grass, and things attached to or forming part of the land which are agreed to be severed before sale or under the contract of sale**.

- **Insolvent** s.2(8); **mercantile agent** s.2(9); **price** s.2(10): money consideration for a sale of goods; **property** s.2(11): the **general property** (ownership) in goods, not merely a special property; **quality of goods** s.2(12); **seller** s.2(13); **specific goods** s.2(14): goods identified and agreed upon at the time the contract of sale is made.

- **Classification of goods (s.6):** *existing* (owned or possessed by the seller) or *future*; **specific, ascertained, unascertained**; and **contingent goods** (future goods the acquisition of which depends on a contingency).

### 5.2 Contract of Sale (ss.4–10)

**s.4(1):** a contract whereby the seller **transfers or agrees to transfer the property in goods** to the buyer **for a price**. It may be absolute or conditional. **s.4(3): sale** is when property in goods is transferred at the time of the contract (an executed contract); **s.4(4): agreement to sell** is when the transfer takes place at a future time or subject to a condition later to be fulfilled (an executory contract). An agreement to sell becomes a sale when the time elapses or the conditions are fulfilled.

**Essentials:** two parties (buyer and seller, different persons); goods; a price in money; transfer of property; and the usual elements of a valid contract. **s.5:** no particular form required, and the contract may be oral or written, or implied from conduct. **s.7:** if the contract is for **specific goods which, without the knowledge of the seller, have perished** at the time of the agreement, the agreement is **void**. **s.8:** where specific goods perish after the agreement to sell but before the risk passes to the buyer, without fault of either party, the agreement is **avoided**.

**Sale vs agreement to sell:**

| Basis                     | Sale                                                   | Agreement to sell                   |
|---------------------------|--------------------------------------------------------|-------------------------------------|
| Nature                    | Executed; a conveyance                                 | Executory contract                  |
| Transfer of property      | Immediate                                              | At a future time or on a condition  |
| Remedy on breach by buyer | Seller can sue for the **price**                       | Seller can sue only for **damages** |
| Risk                      | Passes to the buyer (*res perit domino*)               | Remains with the seller             |
| Insolvency of buyer       | Seller must deliver and has only a claim for the price | Seller can refuse to deliver        |

**Sale vs other transactions:** vs **bailment** (no transfer of ownership); vs **barter/exchange** (no price in money); vs **gift** (no price); vs **hire-purchase** (the hirer has only an option to buy; see *K.L. Johar & Co. v Deputy Commercial Tax Officer*, 1965); vs **pledge** (security, not transfer of ownership); vs **contract for work and labour** (the substance is skill and labour, not sale of goods).

**Price (ss.9–10):** fixed by the contract, or left to be fixed in a manner agreed, or determined by the course of dealing; otherwise the buyer must pay a **reasonable price** (s.9(2)). **s.10:** where the price is to be fixed by a third-party valuer who fails, the agreement is void, but the buyer who has taken goods must pay a reasonable price. **s.11:** unless otherwise agreed, stipulations as to time of payment are *not* the essence of the contract.

### 5.3 Conditions and Warranties (ss.12–17)

- **Condition (s.12(2)):** a stipulation **essential to the main purpose** of the contract, the breach of which gives the buyer the right to **repudiate** the contract (and claim damages).

- **Warranty (s.12(3)):** a stipulation **collateral** to the main purpose, the breach of which gives a right to **damages only**, not to reject the goods.

- **s.12(4):** whether a stipulation is a condition or warranty depends on the construction of the contract. A stipulation may be a condition though called a warranty.

- **s.13:** a **condition may be treated as a warranty** where the buyer waives the condition, elects to treat the breach as a breach of warranty, or **accepts the goods** (in a non-severable contract, where specific goods are involved).

**Implied conditions and warranties:**

- **s.14(a): Condition as to title.** The seller has a right to sell the goods (or will have a right at the time property is to pass). Case: *Rowland v Divall* (the buyer recovers the full price although he used the car, because there was a total failure of consideration).

- **s.14(b): Warranty of quiet possession.**

- **s.14(c): Warranty that goods are free from any charge or encumbrance** in favour of a third party not declared or known to the buyer.

- **s.15: Sale by description.** The goods must correspond with the description (*Varley v Whipp*; *Beale v Taylor*). If the sale is by sample as well as description, the bulk must correspond with both (s.17).

- **s.16(1): Fitness for purpose.** Where the buyer expressly or by implication makes known to the seller the **particular purpose** and **relies on the seller’s skill or judgment**, there is an implied condition that the goods are reasonably fit for that purpose (*Priest v Last*; *Grant v Australian Knitting Mills*). The proviso: in the case of a **specified article under its patent or trade name**, there is no implied condition of fitness.

- **s.16(2): Merchantable quality.** Where goods are bought by description from a seller who **deals in goods of that description**, there is an implied condition that goods are of merchantable quality. If the buyer has **examined** the goods, there is no implied condition regarding defects which such examination ought to have revealed (*Wallis v Pratt*). The **doctrine of *caveat emptor*** (let the buyer beware) is the general rule, subject to these exceptions.

- **s.16(3):** implied conditions annexed by usage of trade. **s.16(4):** an express warranty does not negate an implied condition unless inconsistent.

- **s.17: Sale by sample:** the bulk must correspond with the sample in quality; the buyer must have a reasonable opportunity to compare; the goods must be free from defect not apparent on reasonable examination of the sample.

### 5.4 Transfer of Property and Risk (ss.18–26)

- **s.18:** where there is a contract for the sale of **unascertained goods**, no property in the goods is transferred until the goods are **ascertained**. (*Specific goods:* s.19.)

- **s.19(1)–(2):** in specific or ascertained goods, property passes at the time the parties **intend** it to pass, judged by the terms of the contract, the conduct of the parties and the circumstances.

- **Rules for ascertaining intention (ss.20–24):**

  - **s.20:** specific goods in a **deliverable state**: property passes at the time of the contract, even if payment or delivery is postponed.

  - **s.21:** specific goods to be put into a deliverable state by the seller: property passes when the seller does so and the **buyer has notice**.

  - **s.22:** specific goods in a deliverable state, but the seller has to weigh, measure or test them to ascertain the price: property passes when the act is done and the buyer has notice.

  - **s.23:** **unascertained or future goods by description:** property passes when goods of that description and in a deliverable state are **unconditionally appropriated** to the contract, with the **assent** (express or implied) of the other party.

  - **s.24: Goods sent on approval or ‘on sale or return’:** property passes (a) when the buyer signifies approval or acceptance, (b) when he does any act adopting the transaction (for example pledging or selling the goods), or (c) when he retains the goods beyond the time fixed (or a reasonable time) without giving notice of rejection.

- **s.25: Reservation of the right of disposal** (until conditions are fulfilled). Where goods are shipped and the bill of lading makes the goods deliverable to the seller or his agent, the seller is *prima facie* reserving the right of disposal.

- **s.26: Risk** prima facie follows property (*res perit domino*), whether delivery has been made or not. If delivery is delayed by the fault of either party, the goods are at the risk of the party in fault as regards any loss that might not have occurred but for such fault.

### 5.5 Transfer of Title: Sale by Non-Owners (ss.27–30)

**General rule:** *nemo dat quod non habet*: no one can give a better title than he himself has. **Exceptions:**

1.  **s.27 (Estoppel):** where the owner, by conduct or words, leads the buyer to believe that the seller is the owner or has authority to sell, the owner is estopped from denying the seller’s authority. Also **sale by a mercantile agent** in possession of goods or documents of title with the owner’s consent, in the ordinary course of business, and where the buyer acts in good faith without notice.

2.  **s.28 (Joint owners):** sale by one of several joint owners in sole possession with the consent of the others.

3.  **s.29 (Voidable title):** a person in possession under a contract voidable on grounds such as fraud sells before the contract is rescinded, and the buyer acts in good faith without notice.

4.  **s.30(1) (Seller in possession after sale):** the seller, continuing in possession, delivers or transfers the goods to a person who takes in good faith without notice.

5.  **s.30(2) (Buyer in possession):** the buyer, having obtained possession with the seller’s consent, delivers or transfers the goods to a person who takes in good faith without notice.

6.  **Other exceptions:** a sale by a **finder of lost goods** (s.169 ICA), by a **pawnee** (s.176 ICA), by an **unpaid seller** exercising his right of resale (s.54), under an **order of court** or an execution sale, and sale by an **official assignee or insolvency official**. Cases: *Eastern Distributors v Goldring*; *Lickbarrow v Mason* (documents of title).

### 5.6 Performance of the Contract (ss.31–44)

- **s.31:** the duty of the seller is to deliver the goods and of the buyer to accept and pay for them. **s.32:** delivery and payment are **concurrent conditions** unless otherwise agreed.

- **Modes of delivery (s.33):** **actual** (physical handing over), **constructive** (the person in possession acknowledges that he holds on behalf of the buyer), and **symbolic** (the means of control, such as the keys of a warehouse or a bill of lading).

- **s.34:** part delivery is a delivery of the whole if made with the intent to deliver the whole. **s.35:** the seller is not bound to deliver unless the buyer applies for delivery. **s.36:** place of delivery; the cost of putting goods in a deliverable state is borne by the seller; the buyer must pay for the cost of **taking delivery**. **s.37:** delivery of the wrong quantity (less: the buyer may reject; more: the buyer may accept the contracted quantity and reject the excess, or reject the whole). **s.38:** instalment deliveries (no obligation to accept in instalments unless agreed). **s.39:** delivery to a carrier is *prima facie* delivery to the buyer. **s.40:** deterioration in transit. **s.41:** the buyer’s right to **examine** the goods. **s.42:** acceptance occurs when the buyer intimates acceptance, does an act inconsistent with the seller’s ownership, or retains the goods beyond a reasonable time. **s.43:** a buyer who rightfully refuses is not bound to return the goods. **s.44:** liability of the buyer for neglecting or refusing delivery.

### 5.7 Rights of the Unpaid Seller (ss.45–54)

**Who is an unpaid seller (s.45):** one to whom the whole of the price has not been paid or tendered, or who has received a bill of exchange or negotiable instrument as conditional payment that is dishonoured.

**Rights (s.46):**

1.  **Rights against the goods** (even where property has passed):

    - **Lien (ss.47–49):** to retain possession until payment or tender, where the goods were sold without any stipulation as to credit, or the credit term has expired, or the buyer has become **insolvent**. **s.49:** the lien is lost by delivery to a carrier without reserving the right of disposal, by the buyer lawfully obtaining possession, or by waiver. A part delivery does not by itself lose the lien (s.48).

    - **Stoppage in transit (ss.50–52):** the right to resume possession of goods in transit when the buyer becomes **insolvent**. Transit begins when the goods are delivered to a carrier and ends when the buyer or his agent takes delivery (s.51). The stoppage is effected by taking actual possession or by giving notice to the carrier (s.52).

    - **Resale (s.54):** by an unpaid seller who has exercised a right of lien or stoppage, where the goods are perishable, or where he gives notice to the buyer of his intention to resell and the buyer does not pay within a reasonable time. If there is a loss on resale, the seller may recover it from the buyer; the surplus belongs to the seller where there was no condition about resale and the buyer was in default.

2.  **Rights against the buyer personally:** s.55 suit for the **price** (where property has passed and the buyer wrongfully neglects to pay, or where the price is payable on a certain day irrespective of delivery); s.56 **damages for non-acceptance**; s.61 **interest** and special damages.

### 5.8 Remedies of the Buyer (ss.57–61)

- **s.57:** damages for **non-delivery**.

- **s.58:** **specific performance** (for specific or ascertained goods), now to be read with the Specific Relief Act, 1963, as amended in 2018.

- **s.59:** remedy for **breach of warranty**: set off the loss against the price or sue for damages.

- **s.60:** **repudiation** of the contract before the date of delivery (anticipatory breach).

- **s.61:** interest and special damages. **s.62:** exclusion of implied terms by express agreement or custom. **s.63:** reasonable time is a question of fact.

### 5.9 Auction Sales (s.64)

- The sale is divided into lots; each lot is a separate contract unless otherwise agreed.

- The sale is **complete on the fall of the hammer** (or other customary announcement); until then, a bidder may withdraw his bid.

- The right to bid may be **reserved expressly**; if not, the seller or any person on his behalf may not bid, and any sale in contravention is **voidable at the buyer’s option**. A **reserve price** may be stipulated.

- Pretended bidding to raise the price makes the sale voidable (s.64(6)).

## Chapter 6: Partnership and Limited Liability Partnership

### 6.1 Indian Partnership Act, 1932

**Basics:** in force from **1 October 1932** (except s.69, effective 1 October 1933); ss.1–69 (original structure), replacing Chapter XI of the Contract Act (ss.239–266). Partnership is governed by the principles of **contract** (s.5: it arises from contract, not from status; members of a Hindu undivided family carrying on business are not partners merely by status).

**Definition (s.4):** the relation between persons who have **agreed to share the profits of a business carried on by all or any of them acting for all**. Persons are individually **partners** and collectively a **firm**; the name is the **firm name**.

**Essentials:**

1.  An **agreement** between two or more persons (contractual capacity required).

2.  A **business** (a continuous activity for profit).

3.  **Sharing of profits** (sharing of losses is not essential; sharing of gross receipts is not partnership).

4.  **Mutual agency**: the business is carried on by all or any of them acting for all. This is the real test (*Cox v Hickman*, 1860: mere sharing of profits is not conclusive; *Mollwo March & Co. v Court of Wards*, 1872).

**s.6 (Mode of determining existence):** regard must be had to the real relation between the parties, as shown by all relevant facts taken together. Sharing of profits or gross returns, or receipt of a share of profits, does not by itself make a person a partner.

**Maximum number of partners:** 50 (Companies Act, 2013, s.464, read with Companies (Miscellaneous) Rules, 2014); 10 for banking business. An association exceeding the limit is an illegal association. **Firm is not a legal person** (it is not distinct from partners in civil law), although for income tax and certain other purposes it is treated as a unit.

**Kinds of partners:** active (working), sleeping/dormant, nominal, **partner by estoppel/holding out** (s.28), partner in profits only, sub-partner, and a **minor admitted to the benefits** (s.30).

**Kinds of partnership:** at will (s.7: no fixed duration; any partner may dissolve by notice, s.43) and particular (s.8: for a particular venture or adventure).

### 6.2 Relations of Partners to One Another (ss.9–17)

- **s.9:** partners must carry on the business to the greatest common advantage, be just and faithful to each other, and render true accounts and full information.

- **s.10:** duty to indemnify for loss caused by fraud.

- **s.11:** the mutual rights and duties may be determined by contract, and may be varied by consent.

- **s.12:** rights of partners: every partner has the right to take part in the conduct of the business; to be consulted and heard (a decision in the ordinary course of business is by majority, but a change in the nature of the business requires consent of all); to have access to books; and the duty to act diligently. **s.13:** unless otherwise agreed: no remuneration; profits shared **equally** and losses in the profit ratio; **interest on capital: none**; **interest on advances beyond capital: 6% per annum**; right to be indemnified for expenses incurred and liabilities in an emergency or in the ordinary conduct of business; liability for wilful neglect.

- **s.14:** **property of the firm:** all property originally brought in or acquired in the course of business, including goodwill. **s.15:** application of firm property exclusively for the firm’s business.

- **s.16:** personal profits earned by a partner from firm’s business or use of its property or name; **competing business** (the partner must account for and pay over the profit).

- **s.17:** rights and duties after change in the constitution of the firm (on a continuing basis); the partner’s rights after the firm’s dissolution.

### 6.3 Relations of Partners to Third Parties (ss.18–30)

- **s.18:** a partner is the **agent** of the firm for the purposes of its business.

- **s.19: implied authority** to bind the firm in acts done in the usual way of business. **Exceptions (not allowed unless there is a contract to the contrary):** submit a dispute to arbitration; open a bank account in his own name; compromise or relinquish a claim; withdraw a suit; admit liability in a suit; acquire immovable property on behalf of the firm; transfer immovable property belonging to the firm; **enter into partnership on behalf of the firm**.

- **s.20:** express restriction of implied authority is effective against third parties with notice. **s.21:** emergency authority. **s.22:** mode of binding the firm (an act done in the firm name or in any manner expressing intention to bind). **s.23:** admissions by a partner concerning the affairs of the firm are evidence against the firm. **s.24:** notice to the acting partner is notice to the firm.

- **s.25: Liability** of every partner for all acts of the firm done while he is a partner is **joint and several**.

- **s.26–27:** the firm is liable for wrongs and misapplication of money by a partner acting in the ordinary course of business (*Hamlyn v Houston*, an English authority).

- **s.28/29: Holding out:** a person who represents himself, or knowingly lets himself be represented, as a partner is liable to those who have given credit on that faith. A **partner by estoppel**.

- **s.30: Minors:** a minor cannot be a partner but may be **admitted to the benefits** with the consent of all. He has a share of profits and property, may inspect accounts, and cannot sue the firm for his share except for dissolution. He is not personally liable. On attaining majority (or on knowing he was admitted), within **six months** he must give public notice electing whether to become or not become a partner. If he elects to become a partner, he becomes personally liable to third parties for acts of the firm since he was admitted to the benefits; if he elects not to, his share is not liable for acts after the date of the notice.

### 6.4 Incoming and Outgoing Partners (ss.31–38)

- **s.31:** a new partner can be introduced only with the **consent of all** the partners, unless otherwise agreed. An incoming partner is not liable for acts before joining (s.31(2)).

- **s.32: Retirement:** with the consent of all, by agreement, or by notice in the case of a partnership at will. A retiring partner remains liable for acts before retirement, and also for subsequent acts until **public notice** is given (s.32(3)). His liability can be discharged by an agreement with the third party and the continuing partners.

- **s.33: Expulsion:** only in good faith, by a power conferred by contract and exercised by a majority.

- **s.34:** insolvency of a partner. **s.35:** death of a partner (the estate is not liable for subsequent acts). **s.36:** rights of an outgoing partner to carry on a competing business (permitted, unless restrained by contract, but he cannot use the firm name or represent himself as carrying on the firm’s business). **s.37:** an outgoing partner (where the business continues without final settlement) has the right to a share in subsequent profits or to **6% interest** on his share at his option.

### 6.5 Dissolution (ss.39–55)

**Dissolution of the firm** differs from **dissolution of partnership**: the former ends the whole firm, while the latter means the relationship between some partners changes (for example, by retirement) and the firm continues.

**Modes:**

- **s.40:** by agreement.

- **s.41: Compulsory dissolution:** on insolvency of all partners, or all but one, or on any event that makes the business or partnership unlawful.

- **s.42: By contingencies** (subject to contract): expiry of term; completion of the adventure; death of a partner; insolvency of a partner.

- **s.43:** by notice in partnerships at will.

- **s.44: By the court on grounds:** unsound mind; permanent incapacity of a partner; misconduct likely to prejudicially affect the business; persistent breach of agreement; transfer of the whole interest of a partner to a third party; the business can only be carried on at a loss; and any other **just and equitable ground**.

**Consequences:** **s.45:** continued liability for acts after dissolution until public notice (not for the estate of a deceased, insolvent or retired partner’s apparent liability). **s.46:** right of partners to have the business wound up and property applied in payment of debts. **s.47:** authority to continue for winding up. **s.48: Mode of settlement of accounts** in the absence of agreement: (a) **losses**, including deficiencies of capital, are paid first out of **profits**, next out of **capital**, and lastly, if necessary, by the partners individually in the proportion in which they were entitled to share profits (*Garner v Murray*: a solvent partner need not make up the deficiency of an insolvent partner’s capital); (b) **assets** are applied in the following order: **debts to third parties**, then **advances (loans) by partners** (rateably), then **capital** of each partner, and the **residue** (if any) is shared in the profit ratio. **s.49:** the right to personal profits earned after dissolution. **s.51:** return of premium on premature dissolution. **s.52:** where a partnership is rescinded for fraud, the party defrauded has a lien and rights. **s.53:** restraining use of the firm name or property after dissolution. **s.54–55:** agreements in restraint of trade and goodwill (the goodwill is sold with the business, and the selling partner may be restrained from carrying on a competing business).

### 6.6 Registration of Firms (ss.56–71)

**Registration is optional**, not compulsory. Procedure: an application to the Registrar of Firms, with the prescribed fee, signed by all partners, with the details of firm name, place of business, date of joining, names and addresses, and duration (s.58). **Effects of non-registration (s.69):**

- \(1\) **No suit by a partner (or his representative) against the firm or other partners** to enforce a right arising from the contract or conferred by the Act.

- \(2\) **No suit by or on behalf of an unregistered firm against any third party** to enforce a right arising from a contract.

- \(3\) **No set-off** or other proceeding claimed by or on behalf of the firm.

- **Exceptions (s.69(3)–(4)):** suits for the dissolution of a firm or for accounts, and the realisation of the property of a dissolved firm or an insolvent partner; the right to sue for **claims under Rs. 100**; suits that do not arise out of a contract (torts, statutory rights); firms with no place of business in the territories where the Act applies; **third parties** can sue an unregistered firm.

### 6.7 Limited Liability Partnership Act, 2008

**Background:** Enacted to provide a hybrid form combining **limited liability** and flexible organisation. It was in force from **31 March 2009**. The **LLP Rules, 2009** and the **LLP (Amendment) Act, 2021** (and the LLP Rules 2022 after) added reforms.

**Key features:**

- **Body corporate** and a **separate legal entity** distinct from its partners (s.3), with **perpetual succession** (continuity despite changes in partners) and a common seal (optional).

- **Minimum two partners; no upper limit** (s.6). If the number falls below two for more than **six months**, the surviving partner who carries on business with knowledge is personally liable for obligations incurred after that period.

- **Partners:** any individual or body corporate (s.5). An individual is not capable of being a partner if he is of unsound mind (declared by a competent court), an undischarged insolvent, or has applied to be adjudicated insolvent.

- **Designated partners (s.7):** at least **two** (individuals) and at least **one resident in India** (resident means present in India for not less than **120 days** in the preceding one year). Each must obtain a **Director Identification Number (DIN)**. They are responsible for compliance.

- **LLP Agreement (s.23):** governs mutual rights and duties; in the absence of agreement, the **First Schedule** governs (for example, equal profit sharing, no remuneration; each partner has equal rights in management).

- **Partners are agents of the LLP only**, not of other partners (s.26). The LLP is **not bound** by acts of a partner done without authority where the other party knows this (s.27). The LLP is liable for wrongful acts of its partners in the course of business (s.27).

- **Limited liability:** the liability of a partner is limited to his **agreed contribution**. A partner is not personally liable for the wrongful acts of other partners (s.28), but remains liable for his own wrongful act (s.28(2)).

- **s.30: Fraud:** where an act is carried out with intent to defraud creditors or for any fraudulent purpose, the LLP and any partner knowingly party to it have **unlimited liability** for the LLP’s liabilities, and are liable to penal consequences (imprisonment 6 months to 5 years, and a fine).

- **Contributions:** may be tangible or intangible property, movable or immovable, or any other benefit (including cash, promissory notes, services).

**Incorporation (ss.11–15):** an **incorporation document** (and a statement of compliance) is filed online with the **Registrar of Companies** (through form FiLLiP); the Registrar issues a **certificate of incorporation** (s.11, 12, 13) which is conclusive evidence. The LLP’s name must end with **‘LLP’** or ‘Limited Liability Partnership’ (s.15).

**Accounts and compliance:** statement of accounts and solvency (**Form 8**) and annual return (**Form 11**) are filed every year (ss.34–35). **Audit** is required if turnover exceeds Rs. 40 lakh or contribution exceeds Rs. 25 lakh (LLP Rules, 2009, Rule 24). Maintenance of proper books for each year.

**LLP (Amendment) Act, 2021 (notified in 2022): key reforms:**

- Introduced the concept of a **‘small LLP’** (contribution up to Rs. 25 lakh, turnover up to Rs. 40 lakh, as prescribed) with **lower penalties** for default.

- **Decriminalised** and rationalised several offences; introduced **compounding** and adjudication of penalties.

- Allowed **Indian LLPs to raise funds** by way of ECB and allowed **conversion** and **foreign LLP** reforms.

- Provisions on **‘Foreign LLPs’**, appeals to the **NCLT** and **NCLAT**, **LLP Rules** for **conversion** of firms, private companies and unlisted public companies (Schedules II–IV) into LLPs, and **Authority** for **Adjudicating Officers**.

- Now **Central Government-appointed Adjudicating Officers** and **Special Courts** deal with penalties.

**Conversion:** from a partnership firm (Second Schedule), a private company (Third Schedule), or an unlisted public company (Fourth Schedule). LLPs can also convert to a company under the Companies Act, 2013 (Chapter XXI).

**Winding up (ss.63–64):** voluntary or by the **Tribunal (NCLT)**. The grounds for winding up by the Tribunal include: the LLP decides it should be wound up by the Tribunal; the LLP is unable to pay its debts; the LLP has acted against the sovereignty and integrity of India or the security of the State; the LLP has defaulted in filing the statement of accounts and annual returns for **five consecutive financial years**; and the Tribunal considers it **just and equitable**. The Companies Act provisions apply in so far as they relate to winding up.

## Chapter 7: Negotiable Instruments Act, 1881

**Basics:** in force from **1 March 1882**; based on English law (Bills of Exchange Act, 1882). The Act applies to **promissory notes, bills of exchange and cheques**; usage and custom may recognise other instruments (for example **hundis**, as saved by s.1).

### 7.1 Meaning and Kinds of Negotiable Instruments

**s.13(a):** a negotiable instrument means a **promissory note, bill of exchange or cheque payable either to order or to bearer**. The essential idea is **free transferability**, with the right to give a good title to a **holder in due course** free from the defects of earlier holders.

**Features:** (1) freely transferable by delivery (bearer) or endorsement and delivery (order); (2) the holder takes in his **own name** and may sue in his own name; (3) a holder in due course gets a **better title** than the transferor (an exception to the *nemo dat* rule); (4) **notice of transfer** is not required; (5) there is a **presumption** as to consideration, date, acceptance, transfer, endorsement and stamp (s.118).

| Instrument           | Section | Definition (short)                                                                                                                                                                                                                     | Parties                                        |
|----------------------|---------|----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------|------------------------------------------------|
| **Promissory note**  | s.4     | A **written, unconditional undertaking**, signed by the maker, to pay a certain sum of money only to, or to the order of, a certain person or to the bearer. **Not a bank note or a currency note.**                                   | Maker (promisor) and payee                     |
| **Bill of exchange** | s.5     | A written, **unconditional order**, signed by the maker, directing a certain person to pay a certain sum only to, or to the order of, a certain person or to the bearer.                                                               | Drawer, drawee (acceptor on acceptance), payee |
| **Cheque**           | s.6     | A bill of exchange **drawn on a specified banker** and **not expressed to be payable otherwise than on demand**. It includes the electronic image of a truncated cheque and a cheque in electronic form (added by the 2002 amendment). | Drawer, drawee bank, payee                     |

**Differences:** A promissory note has **two parties**, a bill has **three**. A promissory note is a promise to pay, a bill is an order. A promissory note needs no acceptance; a bill (except one payable on demand) needs **acceptance by the drawee**. A cheque is **always payable on demand and drawn on a banker**. A promissory note cannot be made payable to bearer **on demand** (s.31 RBI Act reserved the issue of such notes to the RBI).

**Kinds of bills:** inland or foreign; time or demand; trade or accommodation bills. **Maturity (s.21–25):** an instrument payable at a fixed period matures on the due date after **three days of grace** (s.22); if the last day is a public holiday, the instrument is due on the **preceding business day** (s.25). Instruments payable **on demand or at sight** carry no days of grace.

**Other key definitions:** **s.8: holder** (entitled in his own name to the possession and to recover the amount); **s.9: holder in due course** (HDC): (i) takes the instrument **for consideration**, (ii) **before maturity**, (iii) **without notice of any defect** in the title of the transferor, and (iv) it is a negotiable instrument in complete and regular form (s.9 says: becomes the possessor of a cheque payable to bearer or the payee/endorsee of an order instrument). **s.10: payment in due course**: in accordance with the apparent tenor, in good faith and without negligence, to the person in possession, and under circumstances which do not afford a reasonable ground for believing he is not entitled to receive the payment.

### 7.2 Negotiation, Endorsement and Presumptions

- **s.14: negotiation** is the transfer to any person so as to constitute him the holder. A **bearer instrument** is negotiated by **delivery** (s.47); an **order instrument** by **endorsement and delivery** (s.48).

- **Endorsement (ss.15–16):** the signature of the maker or holder on the instrument, on the back or face, for the purpose of negotiation. Kinds: **in blank** (signature only; then becomes payable to bearer), **in full** (name of the endorsee), **restrictive** (prohibits further negotiation: ‘pay D only’), **conditional**, **partial** (invalid: s.56), **sans recours**, and **facultative**.

- **s.118: presumptions** until the contrary is proved: (a) **consideration**; (b) **date**; (c) **time of acceptance**; (d) **time of transfer**; (e) **order of endorsements**; (f) **stamp**; (g) that the holder is a **holder in due course**. **s.139:** it shall be presumed, unless the contrary is proved, that the holder received the cheque for the discharge, in whole or in part, of a debt or other liability.

- **s.53:** a holder in due course gets a **good title** free from the defects of the transferor’s title, and even if the transferor obtained it by fraud, unless the holder himself was a party to the fraud. **s.58:** an instrument obtained by unlawful means or for unlawful consideration cannot be recovered by the person who obtained it unless he is a HDC.

- **Liability of parties:** **s.30** the drawer of a bill or cheque is bound to compensate the holder if the instrument is dishonoured; **s.32** the acceptor is **primarily liable**; **s.35** the endorser is liable to subsequent holders; **s.37** the maker of a promissory note is **primarily liable**. **s.38:** as between prior and subsequent parties, the prior parties are liable to the later. **s.41–42:** acceptance for honour (**s.108 onwards**).

- **Capacity (s.26):** every person capable of contracting may bind himself as a maker, drawer, acceptor, endorser etc. A **minor** may draw, endorse, deliver and negotiate so as to bind all parties **except himself**.

- **Material alteration (s.87):** a material alteration without the consent of the parties **avoids** the instrument, except against a party who made or authorised or consented to the alteration (and subsequent parties). A holder in due course can recover as it originally stood (s.88 and s.89).

### 7.3 Presentment, Dishonour and Discharge

- **Presentment for acceptance (ss.61–75):** a bill payable after sight must be presented for acceptance. **Presentment for payment** is required (s.64) to charge the parties, within a reasonable time, during business hours, at the proper place. A **cheque** must be presented within its validity period (**three months** from the date of the cheque, per RBI direction effective 1 April 2012, and **within the period of its validity** under s.138).

- **Dishonour:** by non-acceptance or non-payment. **Notice of dishonour (ss.93–98)** must be given to parties sought to be held liable. **Noting and protest (ss.99–104):** noting by a notary public, and protest for foreign bills.

- **Discharge (ss.77–82):** by **payment in due course** (s.78), by cancellation, release, material alteration, or merger. The drawer and endorsers are discharged by delay in presentment, by the holder’s default, and by novation.

### 7.4 Crossing of Cheques (ss.123–131A)

- **General crossing (s.123):** two parallel transverse lines, with or without the words ‘& Co.’, ‘not negotiable’; the cheque is payable only through a bank account, not over the counter.

- **Special crossing (s.124):** the name of a particular banker is written across the face; the drawee bank must pay only to that banker or his agent.

- **s.125:** the holder may cross an uncrossed cheque, or a generally crossed cheque specially. **s.126:** payment in due course of a crossed cheque. **s.128:** payment in due course of a crossed cheque protects the paying banker. **s.129:** a banker who pays a crossed cheque otherwise than as directed is liable to the true owner. **s.130:** the effect of the words **‘not negotiable’**: the holder cannot give a better title than he himself has. **s.131:** protection to a **collecting banker** who in good faith and without negligence receives payment for a customer. **s.131A:** extension to drafts.

### 7.5 Dishonour of Cheques: Sections 138–148 (Chapter XVII)

Chapter XVII was inserted in **1988** to ensure the credibility of cheques as payment instruments. It makes the dishonour of a cheque for **insufficiency of funds** (or because it exceeds the arrangement with the bank) a **criminal offence**.

**Ingredients of s.138:**

1.  A **cheque** is drawn by a person on an account maintained by him with a banker, in favour of another person.

2.  It is issued for the **discharge, in whole or in part, of any debt or other liability** that is **legally enforceable**.

3.  The cheque is **presented to the bank within three months** (or within its validity period, whichever is earlier).

4.  The cheque is returned unpaid because the money standing to the credit of the account is **insufficient**, or **exceeds the arrangement** with the bank.

5.  The payee or holder in due course gives a **written demand notice within 30 days** of receiving information from the bank regarding the return of the cheque.

6.  The drawer **fails to pay within 15 days** of receipt of the notice.

The **cause of action** arises on the expiry of the 15-day period. **Complaint** must be filed within **one month** from the date on which the cause of action arises (s.142(1)(b)), though the court may condone delay for sufficient cause.

**Punishment:** imprisonment up to **two years**, or a **fine up to twice the amount of the cheque**, or both (2002 amendment).

**Related provisions:**

- **s.139:** statutory **presumption** that the cheque was issued for a legally enforceable debt. The accused can rebut it on the **preponderance of probabilities**, not beyond reasonable doubt (*Rangappa v Sri Mohan*, 2010; *Bir Singh v Mukesh Kumar*, 2019: even a **signed blank cheque** voluntarily given attracts the presumption).

- **s.140:** it is not a defence that the drawer had no reason to believe the cheque would be dishonoured.

- **s.141: Offences by companies:** every person who, at the time of the offence, was in charge of and responsible to the company for the conduct of its business is deemed guilty. The **company must be arraigned** as an accused (*Aneeta Hada v Godfather Travels & Tours*, 2012). A director is liable only if in charge of and responsible for the conduct of the business (*S.M.S. Pharmaceuticals v Neeta Bhalla*, 2005); a **signatory of the cheque** is automatically liable.

- **s.142: Cognizance:** only on a **written complaint** by the payee or holder in due course. **s.142(2) (inserted by the 2015 amendment):** the court within whose local jurisdiction the **branch of the bank where the payee or holder maintains the account** is situated has jurisdiction (in case of a cheque delivered for collection through an account). This overruled *Dashrath Rupsingh Rathod v State of Maharashtra* (2014), which had held that the drawer’s bank branch alone determines jurisdiction. Earlier, *K. Bhaskaran v Sankaran Vaidhyan Balan* (1999) held that the five acts constituting the offence give multiple venues.

- **s.143: Summary trial** (with power to impose a sentence up to one year and fine of up to Rs. 5,000 under the CrPC, now Bharatiya Nagarik Suraksha Sanhita, 2023). **Time limit** for trial: six months (directory). **s.145:** evidence on affidavit. **s.146:** the bank’s slip is *prima facie* evidence of dishonour. **s.147:** the offence is **compoundable** (*Damodar S. Prabhu v Sayed Babalal H.*, 2010: guidelines on costs for compounding; *Meters and Instruments v Kanchan Mehta*, 2017).

- **s.143A: Interim compensation** (inserted by the **Negotiable Instruments (Amendment) Act, 2018**): the court may direct the drawer to pay **interim compensation up to 20% of the cheque amount** to the complainant. For a summary trial, on the plea of not guilty; in other cases, upon framing the charge. It is to be paid within 60 days (extendable by 30 days).

- **s.148:** in an **appeal** by the drawer against conviction, the appellate court may order deposit of a minimum of **20% of the fine or compensation** awarded by the trial court (2018 amendment; applies to pending appeals: *Surinder Singh Deswal v Virender Gandhi*, 2019). If the appeal succeeds, the amount is returned with interest.

**Other leading cases on s.138:**

- **NEPC Micon v Magma Leasing** (1999): closure of the account amounts to dishonour for insufficiency of funds.

- **Modi Cements v Kuchil Kumar Nandi** (1998) and **Laxmi Dyechem v State of Gujarat** (2012): stop-payment instruction or signature mismatch can attract s.138 if the cheque was issued for a debt.

- **MSR Leathers v S. Palaniappan** (2013): a cheque may be presented **multiple times** within its validity, and a fresh cause of action arises each time (but only one complaint on a given cause).

- **Kusum Ingots & Alloys v Pennar Peterson Securities** (2000): the notice is the prerequisite.

- **Indian Bank Association v Union of India** (2014): directions for speedy trial; **Re: Expeditious Trial of Cases under Section 138 of NI Act** (2021): constitution of special courts and procedural reforms.

- **Sampelly Satyanarayana Rao v IREDA** (2016): the debt must be legally enforceable.

**Exam tip:** timelines are a favourite: **3 months** (presentment), **30 days** (notice), **15 days** (payment), **1 month** (complaint), **20%** (interim compensation and appeal deposit), **2 years / twice the amount** (punishment).

### 7.6 Banker–Customer Notes (Peripheral but Tested)

- A banker must honour a customer’s cheque if funds are sufficient; wrongful dishonour exposes the bank to damages (**s.31**).

- **Garnishee orders** and **countermand** (stop-payment) are duties and risk areas; the **banker’s lien** (s.171 ICA) is a general lien.

- The **Information Technology Act** does not apply to negotiable instruments except cheques (First Schedule, post-2002 amendment on **cheque truncation**).

## Chapter 8: Company Law: Incorporation, Prospectus, Shares and Debentures

**Statute:** the **Companies Act, 2013** (assent 29 August 2013; notified in phases from 12 September 2013 and mostly from 1 April 2014). It has **470 sections** and **7 Schedules** (check the latest count in your bare Act), and replaced the Companies Act, 1956. Major amendments: **2015, 2017, 2019, 2020** (decriminalisation), **2022**, and the **Jan Vishwas (Amendment of Provisions) Act, 2023**, which rationalised penalties across many statutes including the Companies Act. Before the exam, check the **latest MCA notifications**, because thresholds and rules are revised often.

### 8.1 Nature and Kinds of Companies

**s.2(20):** a company means a company incorporated under this Act or any previous company law. **Characteristics:** incorporated association; **separate legal personality**; **perpetual succession**; **limited liability** (in most kinds); common seal (optional after the 2015 amendment); transferable shares; capacity to sue and be sued; and separation of ownership from management.

**Classification:**

- **By incorporation:** chartered, statutory, and registered companies.

- **By liability:** company **limited by shares**, **limited by guarantee** (members guarantee a sum on winding up), and **unlimited company**.

- **By members:** **private company** (s.2(68)), **public company** (s.2(71)), and **One Person Company (OPC)** (s.2(62)).

- **By control:** **holding** (s.2(46)) and **subsidiary** (s.2(87)), **associate company** (s.2(6): significant influence, holding of 20% or more of total voting power), and **government company** (s.2(45): at least **51%** of paid-up share capital held by the Central or State governments).

- **Others:** **small company** (s.2(85): paid-up capital up to Rs. 4 crore and turnover up to Rs. 40 crore, as revised in 2022; not a holding or subsidiary company, not a s.8 company, and not governed by a special Act); **s.8 company** (charitable or non-profit objects, licence from the Central Government, no dividend); **Nidhi company**; **producer company**; **foreign company** (s.2(42)); **listed company**; **dormant company** (s.455).

**Private company (s.2(68)):** by its articles it (a) **restricts the right to transfer** its shares, (b) limits the number of members to **200** (excluding employees, and ex-employees who were members while employed), and (c) **prohibits any invitation to the public** to subscribe to its securities. The 2015 amendment **removed the minimum paid-up capital requirement** (earlier Rs. 1 lakh for private and Rs. 5 lakh for public).

**Minimum number of members (s.3):** public company **7**; private company **2**; OPC **1** (with a nominee). A public company has **no maximum**. A private company can have a maximum of 200 members. OPC cannot be incorporated by a non-resident (s.3(1)(c), Rule 3: a natural person who is an Indian citizen and resident in India, with the residence defined as 120 days; the Companies (Incorporation) Second Amendment Rules 2021 permitted NRIs to form OPCs).

**Minimum directors (s.149):** public company **3**, private company **2**, OPC **1**. At least **one director must be resident in India** (stayed at least 182 days in the previous financial year). **At least one woman director** for prescribed classes of companies (listed companies and public companies with paid-up capital of Rs. 100 crore or more or turnover of Rs. 300 crore or more). Maximum **15 directors** (more by special resolution).

### 8.2 Corporate Personality and Lifting of the Corporate Veil

- **Salomon v Salomon & Co. Ltd.** (1897): a company is a distinct legal person, separate from its members; the person who held nearly all shares and was a secured creditor was not liable for company debts. Indian recognition: **Tata Engineering v State of Bihar** (1964) and **Bacha F. Guzdar v CIT** (1955: a shareholder is not an owner of the company’s assets).

- **Lee v Lee’s Air Farming** (1961): a sole shareholder can be an employee. **Macaura v Northern Assurance** (1925): a shareholder has no insurable interest in the company’s assets.

**Lifting the veil:**

- **Statutory:** reduction in membership below the minimum (s.? and continuing liability), **misdescription of the company’s name** (s.12(3)), **fraud** (s.7(7) incorporation by fraud, s.34/s.35 misstatement in prospectus, **s.447** fraud), **fraudulent trading** (s.339), **investigation** (s.216), and **holding–subsidiary** accounts (consolidated).

- **Judicial grounds:** **fraud or improper conduct** (*Gilford Motor Co. v Horne*; *Jones v Lipman*); **sham or façade**; **agency** (*Smith, Stone & Knight v Birmingham Corp.*); **enemy character** (*Daimler Co. v Continental Tyre*); **tax evasion** (*Juggilal Kamlapat v CIT*, 1969; *Re Sir Dinshaw Maneckjee Petit*, 1927); **welfare of workers and public interest** (*State of U.P. v Renusagar Power*, 1988); **group enterprises** (*DHN Food Distributors v Tower Hamlets*). Cases: **Delhi Development Authority v Skipper Construction** (1996); **Subhra Mukherjee v Bharat Coking Coal** (2000); **Vodafone International Holdings v Union of India** (2012: the corporate veil is respected unless there is a sham or tax evasion; look-through is a **‘look at’** analysis); **Singer India v Chander Mohan Chadha** (2004). *Adams v Cape Industries* (1990) restricts veil-lifting in English law, and *Prest v Petrodel* (2013) holds that it applies only for evasion or concealment.

### 8.3 Promoters and Pre-incorporation Contracts

**Promoter (s.2(69)):** a person named as such in a prospectus or annual return, or who has control over the company’s affairs directly or indirectly, or in accordance with whose advice the board is accustomed to act (professional advisers are excluded). **Position:** neither agent nor trustee, but **fiduciary** (*Erlanger v New Sombrero Phosphate Co.*, 1878). **Duties:** full disclosure of secret profits; the company may rescind or recover the secret profit. **Liabilities:** for misstatements in the prospectus (s.34, s.35, s.447).

**Pre-incorporation contracts:** the company is **not bound** (it did not exist) and cannot ratify them (*Kelner v Baxter*; *Newborne v Sensolid*). In India, **s.15(h) and s.19(e)** of the **Specific Relief Act, 1963** allow a company to enforce them if they are warranted by the terms of incorporation, the contract was entered into by the promoters before the company existed for its purposes, and the company has accepted the contract and communicated it. The promoter remains liable until the company adopts the contract by a **fresh contract (novation)**.

### 8.4 Incorporation (ss.3–20)

**Procedure (steps):**

1.  **Name approval** through RUN (Reserve Unique Name), or by SPICe+ (the integrated form INC-32). The name must not be identical or too nearly resemble an existing name or trademark (s.4(2)(a)), must not be undesirable, and must end with ‘**Limited**’ (public), ‘**Private Limited**’ (private), or ‘**OPC**’ in brackets for OPC (s.4(5)).

2.  Obtaining **DIN** (Director Identification Number) and **digital signature certificates (DSC)** for subscribers and directors.

3.  Preparation of the **memorandum** and **articles** (INC-33 and INC-34 in the integrated filing).

4.  **Filing (s.7)** with the Registrar of Companies: the memorandum and articles; an agreement (if any) to appoint a managing director or whole-time director; a **declaration** by an advocate, chartered accountant, cost accountant or company secretary engaged in formation that all requirements have been complied with; an **affidavit** from each subscriber and the first directors that they are not convicted or disqualified; and the **address of the registered office**.

5.  **Certificate of incorporation (s.7(2))**, which also has the **CIN**. It is **conclusive evidence** that all requirements have been complied with (**s.7(2)**). Under s.7(7), if it is found that the company was incorporated by furnishing false information or suppressing facts, the Tribunal may order regulation of liability, winding up, or removal of the name.

6.  **Registered office (s.12):** within **30 days** of incorporation; notice of verification of the registered office within **30 days** of incorporation.

7.  **Commencement of business (s.10A):** inserted in **2019** — a company incorporated after 2019 with share capital must file a **declaration (Form INC-20A)** within **180 days** of incorporation that every subscriber has paid the value of the shares agreed.

**Effect of registration (s.9):** the subscribers become a **body corporate** with **perpetual succession and a common seal**, capable of exercising all functions of an incorporated company, with the **liability of members** limited as stated.

**Memorandum of Association (s.4):**

- **Name clause**; **registered office clause** (the **State** in which the registered office is to be situated); **objects clause** (main objects, and objects incidental or ancillary); **liability clause**; **capital clause** (in a limited-by-shares company: authorised share capital divided into shares of fixed amounts); **association (subscription) clause**: each subscriber agrees to take at least **one share**, and the number of shares taken by each subscriber is stated.

- **Forms:** Tables **A–E** in **Schedule I** (A: limited by shares; B: guarantee, no share capital; C: guarantee with share capital; D: unlimited, no share capital; E: unlimited with share capital).

- **Alteration:** name (s.13(2): special resolution and Central Government approval; **after 2016** the Registrar approves under Rule; **s.16 change of name** if identical or resembling, directed within 3 months); **registered office** (s.13(4): shifting from one State to another requires a **special resolution and the approval of the Regional Director**); **objects clause** (special resolution, s.13(1)); **capital clause** (s.61); **liability clause** (s.? special resolution and consent of the members, and increase of liability needs the member’s consent).

**Doctrine of ultra vires:** acts beyond the powers in the memorandum are **void and incapable of ratification** (*Ashbury Railway Carriage & Iron Co. v Riche*, 1875). Indian case: **A. Lakshmanaswami Mudaliar v LIC** (1963). Remedies: **injunction**, personal liability of directors, and recovery of company property by tracing. Acts **intra vires the company but beyond the directors’ powers** can be ratified by members; acts that are **irregular** may be ratified.

**Articles of Association (s.5):** the regulations for the management of the company’s internal affairs, subordinate to the **Memorandum and the Act**. A **public company** may adopt **Table F** (Schedule I), and companies may adopt provisions. **Entrenchment (s.5(3)):** specified provisions can be altered only if conditions or procedures stricter than special resolution are met; notice to the Registrar. **Binding effect (s.10):** the memorandum and articles bind the company and the members to the same extent as if signed by each member. Cases: **Hickman v Kent or Romney Marsh Sheepbreeders’ Association** (1915: the articles constitute a contract between the company and the members only in their capacity as members); **Eley v Positive Government Security Life Assurance Co.** (1876: an outsider cannot enforce); **Salmon v Quin & Axtens**; **Naresh Chandra Sanyal v Calcutta Stock Exchange** (1971). **Alteration (s.14):** by special resolution, filed with the Registrar within 15 days; conversion of a public company into a private company needs the approval of the Tribunal; the alteration must be *bona fide for the benefit of the company as a whole* (**Allen v Gold Reefs**, 1900; **Brown v British Abrasive Wheel Co.**) and must not be retrospective to the detriment of a member.

**Doctrine of constructive notice:** every person dealing with a company is deemed to have read the registered documents (memorandum and articles) (*Ernest v Nicholls*). **Doctrine of indoor management (Turquand’s rule):** an outsider dealing in good faith may assume that the internal procedures have been regularly complied with (**Royal British Bank v Turquand**, 1856; **Mahony v East Holyford Mining Co.**).

**Exceptions to the Turquand rule:** (a) **knowledge of irregularity** (*Howard v Patent Ivory Manufacturing Co.*); (b) **suspicion** that should have prompted inquiry (*Anand Bihari Lal v Dinshaw & Co.*, 1946); (c) **forgery** (*Ruben v Great Fingall Consolidated*); (d) **documents or acts not within the apparent authority** or outside the memorandum (ultra vires); (e) **no reliance** on the articles and **no actual dealing** with the company (*Rama Corporation v Proved Tin & General Investment Co.*). Also, **the Indian Companies Act s.? (Turquand’s rule) is not codified**.

### 8.5 Prospectus (ss.2(70), 23–42)

**Definition (s.2(70)):** any document described or issued as a prospectus, including a **red herring prospectus** (s.32) or **shelf prospectus** (s.31), or any notice, circular, advertisement or other document **inviting offers from the public** for the subscription or purchase of any securities of a body corporate.

**Modes of raising securities (s.23):** a **public company** may issue securities (a) to the public through a **prospectus** (public offer), (b) through **private placement**, or (c) through a **rights issue** or **bonus issue**. A **private company** may issue securities by private placement, rights issue or bonus issue (not to the public). **s.24:** SEBI regulates the issue and transfer of securities by **listed companies** (and those that intend to list), through the **SEBI (ICDR) Regulations, 2018**.

**Types:**

- **Red herring prospectus (s.32):** does not include complete particulars of the **price** of the securities offered or the **quantum** of securities. It must be filed with the Registrar at least **three days** before the opening of the subscription list. Used in **book-building**.

- **Shelf prospectus (s.31):** for companies (such as public financial institutions and banks) that issue securities in **one or more issues over a period** without a separate prospectus each time. The validity period is **up to one year** (as stated in the prospectus). An **information memorandum** is filed before each subsequent offer.

- **Abridged prospectus (s.33):** a **memorandum containing the salient features** of the prospectus, issued with every application form.

- **Deemed prospectus (s.25):** where a company allots, or agrees to allot, securities to an **issue house** or any other person with a view to their **offer for sale to the public**, any document by which the offer is made is deemed to be a prospectus.

**Contents and registration (s.26):** the prospectus must be **dated**, and signed by every person named as a director or proposed director, and must be **filed with the Registrar** for registration (on or before the date of publication). Required content (Schedule II, ICDR): objects of the issue, the details of capital structure, risk factors, the **reports of the auditors and experts**, financial information, **promoters’ contribution**, **litigation**, the management, and the terms. **Experts’ consent** is required, in writing, before the issue of the prospectus (s.26(5)).

**Dating and effect:** the date on the prospectus is the date of publication (s.26(?)). A prospectus issued more than 90 days after registration is invalid (the old 90-day rule). The **minimum subscription** and **application money** are regulated by SEBI (ICDR); the allotment must be made within the time prescribed, and money must be refunded in case of failure (s.39(3): within **15 days** of the closing of the issue; interest at 15% beyond that period).

**Misstatements in the prospectus:**

- **s.34: criminal liability.** Where a prospectus includes an untrue or misleading statement, every person who authorised the issue is liable under **s.447** (fraud), unless he proves that the statement was immaterial or that he had reasonable grounds to believe it true.

- **s.35: civil liability.** Every person who is a **director at the time of the issue**, who **authorised** the issue, who agreed to become a director, the **promoter**, and the **expert** (for the part contained in his report), is liable to pay **compensation** to every person who subscribed for securities on the faith of the prospectus for any loss or damage sustained by reason of any untrue or misleading statement or **omission**. **Defences (s.35(2))**: withdrew consent before issue; issued without his knowledge; reasonable belief; the statement was an expert’s (and he believed in the expert’s competence).

- **s.36:** punishment for **fraudulently inducing persons to invest money** (under s.447). **s.37: class actions** — compensation to depositors. **s.38:** punishment for **personation** to acquire securities (liable under s.447).

- **Remedies of the investor:** **rescission** of the contract (for misrepresentation or non-disclosure, within a reasonable time and before winding up), **damages** for fraud (*Derry v Peek*), and **statutory compensation** (s.35).

- **Case law:** **Derry v Peek** (1889: fraud needs knowledge or recklessness); **Rex v Kylsant** (1932: a **half-truth** may mislead); **New Brunswick & Canada Railway v Muggeridge** (1860: the duty to disclose all material facts); **Central Railway Co. of Venezuela v Kisch** (1867: promoters’ duty); **Re Pilot**. Also, a **material misstatement** includes **statements of opinion** where the opinion is not honestly held.

### 8.6 Shares (ss.2(84), 43–72)

**s.2(84):** a **share** is a share in the **share capital** of a company, and includes **stock**. A share is **movable property** (s.44) transferable as per the articles, and **has a nominal value**. **Types (s.43):**

- **Equity share capital**: with **voting rights**, or with **differential rights as to dividend, voting or otherwise** (subject to the Companies (Share Capital and Debentures) Rules, 2014).

- **Preference share capital**: (a) a **preferential right** for the payment of dividend (a fixed amount or at a fixed rate) and (b) a **preferential right to return of capital** on winding up. **s.55:** a company limited by shares **cannot issue irredeemable preference shares**; they must be redeemed within **20 years** (**30 years** for infrastructure projects, subject to conditions). Redemption is only out of **profits** available for dividend or from the proceeds of a **fresh issue**, and fully paid. A **Capital Redemption Reserve (CRR)** must be created where redeemed out of profits.

**Kinds of preference shares:** cumulative and non-cumulative; participating and non-participating; convertible and non-convertible; redeemable.

**Share capital terms:** **authorised** (nominal/registered), **issued**, **subscribed**, **called-up**, **paid-up**, **uncalled** and **reserve capital** (only on winding up, s.? and Act).

**Issue of shares:**

- **s.23 and s.39: allotment.** Allotment must be made within the time prescribed after minimum subscription. A return of allotment (**Form PAS-3**) must be filed within **30 days**. An **irregular allotment** is voidable at the instance of the applicant within a reasonable time, and the Act prohibits allotment unless the application money is received.

- **s.52: securities premium account**: the premium on shares must be credited to this account, and can be used only for (a) issuing fully paid **bonus shares**, (b) writing off preliminary expenses, (c) writing off expenses of or **commission** paid or discount allowed on the issue of securities or debentures, (d) providing for the premium on redemption of preference shares or debentures, and (e) **purchase of its own securities** (buy-back).

- **s.53: no issue at a discount** (any such issue is **void**), except **sweat equity shares** (s.54). **s.54: sweat equity shares** may be issued to **employees or directors** at a **discount** or for consideration other than cash (know-how, IPR, value additions), after **one year** from commencement (startups: ten years), by **special resolution**.

- **s.62: further issue of share capital:** (a) **rights issue**: offer to **existing shareholders** in proportion to their holdings, with a notice of not less than **15 days** and not more than **30 days** (the MCA has at times allowed shorter periods) giving them the right to **renounce** in favour of another; (b) **employee stock option (ESOP)**: special resolution; (c) **preferential allotment**: special resolution and compliance with the Rules.

- **s.63: bonus shares:** from **free reserves**, the **securities premium account** or the **capital redemption reserve account** only (**not** from revaluation reserves), authorised by articles; may be issued in lieu of dividend not. Once declared, a bonus issue cannot be withdrawn.

- **s.64 and s.65:** notice of alteration of share capital to the Registrar within 30 days.

- **s.46: share certificate** within **two months** of allotment (or **one month** from the date of lodging an instrument of transfer; **15 days** from a request for a duplicate). A share certificate is **prima facie evidence of title** (s.46(1)) and creates **estoppel** against the company as regards the matters stated (**Re Bahia & San Francisco Railway**; \*\*Sri Mahadeo? \*\*).

- **s.29:** a public company must issue and transfer securities in **dematerialised form** (and unlisted public companies from October 2018). **Depositories Act, 1996**.

**Calls, forfeiture and lien:** the articles (**Table F**) govern calls on shares (at least **14 days’ notice**; no call may exceed 25% of the face value, and an interval of at least one month between calls, under the old rules), **forfeiture** for non-payment of calls (only for non-payment; the power must be exercised in good faith and for the purpose of the call), and **surrender**. The company’s **lien** (Table F) is a first and paramount lien on partly paid shares (not on fully paid shares in a public company). **Transfer** s.56: an instrument of transfer (**Form SH-4**) duly stamped and delivered; the company shall deliver certificates within **one month**. **s.58:** a **private company** may restrict the transfer in its articles, whereas the shares of a **public company are freely transferable**; if a private company refuses to register, an appeal lies to the **Tribunal within 30 days**; **s.58(4)** the transferee may appeal. **Transmission** (s.56(2)) by operation of law (death, insolvency). Distinguish **transfer** (voluntary act) and **transmission** (by operation of law). **s.59:** rectification of the register by the Tribunal. **s.59(4)** the Tribunal may order rectification, and **s.88** is the register of members.

**Alteration and reduction of capital (ss.61–66):**

- **s.61:** a company limited by shares, if authorised by the articles, may by **ordinary resolution**: increase its authorised capital; **consolidate** and divide shares into larger amounts; **convert** fully paid shares into stock and *vice versa*; **sub-divide**; and **cancel unissued shares**.

- **s.66: reduction of share capital:** by **special resolution** (if authorised by articles) and **confirmation by the Tribunal** (NCLT), after notice to the Central Government, SEBI and the creditors. A reduction affecting the creditors requires their consent or discharge. The Tribunal may order publication of reasons.

- **s.67:** a company may **not purchase its own shares** or give financial assistance for the purchase of its shares except by buy-back (s.68) and permitted exceptions (for example by a private company with employee-held shares and a scheme for employees).

- **s.68 (buy-back):** from **free reserves**, the **securities premium account**, or the proceeds of an issue of other shares or specified securities (not from the proceeds of an earlier issue of the same kind); the buy-back must not exceed **25% of the total paid-up capital and free reserves**; a **board resolution** is enough if up to **10%** of the paid-up capital and free reserves, and a **special resolution** beyond that; the **debt-equity ratio** after the buy-back must not exceed **2:1**; **only fully paid-up** shares; the buy-back must be completed **within one year** of the resolution; a **declaration of solvency** is required; the bought-back securities must be **extinguished and physically destroyed within seven days**; a company cannot make another buy-back offer **for a period of one year** after the closure of the previous one; and a company cannot buy back if it has defaulted on repayment of deposits, interest, redemption of debentures, preference shares or payment of dividend until the default is remedied. **s.70:** prohibition on buy-back through subsidiaries and investment companies, and through any fraudulent device.

**Dividends (ss.123–127):** dividend is payable **only out of profits** of the current or previous years (after providing for depreciation), or out of free reserves; **interim dividend** may be declared by the board; it must be **deposited in a separate bank account within five days** of declaration and paid within **30 days** (s.127: failure to distribute within 30 days is an offence); **unpaid or unclaimed dividend** is transferred to the **Unpaid Dividend Account** within **seven days** (s.124), and then, after **seven years**, to the **Investor Education and Protection Fund (IEPF)** (s.125); the shares to which dividends have not been paid for seven consecutive years are also transferred to the IEPF (s.124(6)).

**Members:** a **member** (s.2(55)) is a subscriber to the memorandum or a person who agrees in writing to be a member and whose name is entered in the **register of members**, or a **beneficial owner** whose name is in the register of a depository. **Distinction between member and shareholder** (a shareholder may be a bearer of a share warrant). **s.88:** registers of members and debenture holders. **s.90:** registers of **significant beneficial owners** (**SBO**: an individual who holds at least **10%** of shares, voting rights, or the right to receive or participate in dividends, or exercises significant influence or control).

### 8.7 Debentures (ss.2(30), 71–72)

**Definition (s.2(30)):** a debenture **includes debenture stock, bonds or any other instrument of a company evidencing a debt**, whether constituting a charge on the assets of the company or not. A debenture is a **document acknowledging a debt**; the holder is a **creditor, not a member**.

**Differences between shares and debentures:**

| Basis               | Share                                        | Debenture                                                                  |
|---------------------|----------------------------------------------|----------------------------------------------------------------------------|
| Status of holder    | Owner (member)                               | Creditor                                                                   |
| Return              | Dividend (from profits)                      | **Fixed interest** (payable irrespective of profits, charged on revenue)   |
| Voting right        | Yes (equity)                                 | **No** (s.71(1): no company shall issue debentures carrying voting rights) |
| Issue at a discount | **Not allowed** (s.53), except sweat equity  | **Allowed**                                                                |
| Security            | Not secured                                  | May be secured by a charge                                                 |
| Repayment           | On winding up only (equity), after creditors | Repayable at maturity or on winding up, with priority over shareholders    |
| Conversion          | Not applicable                               | May be convertible (s.71(1) requires a **special resolution**)             |

**Kinds:** **secured** (mortgage) and **unsecured** (naked); **redeemable** and **irredeemable**; **registered** and **bearer**; **convertible** (fully or partly) and **non-convertible**; **fixed charge** and **floating charge debentures**. **Debenture stock:** a borrowing of one aggregate sum, divided into units.

**Provisions of s.71:**

- **s.71(1):** a company may issue debentures with an option to **convert** into shares, wholly or partly, **only with the approval of a special resolution**. **No voting rights** in debentures.

- **s.71(2):** **secured debentures** may be issued subject to prescribed terms; the security must be a **charge** on the company’s assets, **registered** under s.77.

- **s.71(5): debenture trustee** is appointed **before the issue of the prospectus or letter of offer**, to protect the interests of the debenture holders; the **trust deed** is executed **within 60 days** of the closure of the issue (reduced in 2019 from three months). The trustee is regulated by the **SEBI (Debenture Trustees) Regulations, 1993**.

- **s.71(4): Debenture Redemption Reserve (DRR):** the 2019 amendment (Companies (Share Capital and Debentures) Amendment Rules, 2019) **exempted listed companies** from creating DRR for public issues or private placement; **unlisted companies** (other than NBFCs and HFCs regulated by RBI/NHB) must create DRR of **10%** of the value of outstanding debentures maturing within a year. Companies must also invest or deposit **at least 15%** of the amount of debentures maturing during the year ending on 31 March of the next year, **before 30 April**, in specified instruments.

- **s.71(10)–(13):** the debenture holders’ remedies: the **Tribunal** may, on application by a debenture holder or trustee, order the company to **redeem** the debentures forthwith on payment of principal and interest if the company fails to redeem or pay interest; the debenture certificate must be delivered within **two months** of allotment. **s.72:** **nomination** facility for debenture holders.

### 8.8 Charges (ss.77–87)

**Fixed charge:** on **specific, ascertained assets** (such as land and buildings); the company cannot dispose of them without the consent of the charge holder. **Floating charge:** on a **class of assets present and future**, which in the ordinary course of business changes from time to time; the company may deal with the assets until the charge **crystallises** (on default, on the appointment of a receiver, or on winding up). Features of a floating charge are in **Re Yorkshire Woolcombers Association** (1903: (a) a charge on a class of assets, present and future; (b) the class is one which, in the ordinary course of business, would be changing from time to time; (c) the company may carry on business until some step is taken by the chargeholders). Related cases: **Illingworth v Houldsworth**; **Holroyd v Marshall**. **Priority:** a fixed charge usually has priority over a floating charge, and a floating charge is postponed to preferential creditors (workmen’s dues, and secured creditors, **under the Insolvency and Bankruptcy Code, 2016, s.53**).

**Registration (s.77):** every charge created by a company must be **registered with the Registrar within 30 days** of its creation, in the prescribed form (CHG-1 / CHG-9). The Registrar may allow registration **within a further period of 300 days** on payment of additional fees (**Companies (Amendment) Act, 2019**); beyond that, the matter goes to the **Central Government (Regional Director)** under s.87. **Unregistered charges are void** against the liquidator and any creditor (but the debt remains a valid simple contract debt, and the money becomes immediately payable). **s.82:** the **satisfaction of the charge** must be filed within **30 days**. **s.85:** the company must keep a **register of charges** at the registered office. **s.86:** offence.

## Chapter 9: Company Law: Directors and Meetings

### 9.1 The Board and the Directors

**Director (s.2(34)):** a director appointed to the Board of a company. Directors are **agents** of the company in their contracts, **trustees** of its property and funds in their custody, and **fiduciaries** in their dealings. A director is **not an employee** unless he is a whole-time director under a contract of service. Types: executive and non-executive; **managing director (s.2(54))** (substantial powers of management, subject to the Board’s superintendence), **whole-time director (s.2(94))**, **manager (s.2(53))**, **independent director**, **nominee director**, **additional**, **alternate** and **woman director**. **Key Managerial Personnel (s.2(51))**: the CEO, MD or manager; the company secretary; the whole-time director; the CFO; and other prescribed officers.

**Composition (s.149):**

- **Minimum directors:** public **3**; private **2**; OPC **1**. **Maximum: 15**, which can be increased by a **special resolution**.

- **One resident director** (at least 182 days in India in the previous year).

- **Woman director:** at least one for every **listed company** and every other **public company with paid-up share capital of Rs. 100 crore or more, or turnover of Rs. 300 crore or more**.

- **Independent directors (IDs):** **one-third** of the total number of directors in a **listed public company**; and every **public company** with paid-up capital of **Rs. 10 crore or more**, or turnover of **Rs. 100 crore or more**, or aggregate outstanding loans, debentures and deposits exceeding **Rs. 50 crore** must have at least **two** independent directors.

**Independent director (s.149(6)):** a person of integrity, expertise and experience, who is **not** a promoter or related to the promoter or a director of the company; has **no pecuniary relationship** with the company (other than remuneration as ID) in the previous two financial years; none of his relatives has a pecuniary relationship exceeding **2% or more of the gross turnover or total income**, or **Rs. 50 lakh** whichever is lower; and has not been an employee, auditor, or professional in the company in the preceding **three years**. **Tenure (s.149(10)–(11)):** up to **five consecutive years**; may be re-appointed for **one more term of five consecutive years** by **special resolution**; a **cooling period of three years** before being appointed in any other capacity; no **stock options**; not liable to **retire by rotation**. **Schedule IV** contains the Code for Independent Directors; **s.150** provides for a **data bank**; **s.149(12)** limits an ID’s liability to acts of omission or commission which occurred with his knowledge, attributable through board processes, and with his consent or connivance or where he had not acted diligently.

**Appointment (ss.152–161):**

- **DIN (s.153–155):** every director must obtain a **Director Identification Number** (an 8-digit number); a person cannot hold more than one DIN. **Consent** to act (Form DIR-2) is needed before appointment.

- **First directors** are named in the articles; if none, the **subscribers** are deemed to be the first directors until directors are appointed.

- **Appointment** in general meetings by **ordinary resolution** (each director by a separate resolution, s.162, unless unanimously approved otherwise).

- **Retirement by rotation (s.152(6)):** **two-thirds** of the total number of directors of a public company must be liable to retire by rotation (**independent directors are excluded**); **one-third** of those retire at each AGM.

- **s.161:** the Board may appoint **additional directors** (up to the articles’ maximum), who hold office until the next AGM; an **alternate director** (for a director absent from India for not less than **three months**); and fill a **casual vacancy** (until the term of the vacated director would have expired). **Nominee director** by an institution or the Government.

- **s.165:** a person may hold directorships in **at most 20 companies**, of which **not more than 10 can be public companies** (a private company that is a subsidiary of a public company counts as public). SEBI’s LODR limits the number of **listed company** directorships to **seven**.

- **s.196–197 and Schedule V:** the **managing director, whole-time director or manager** must be aged **between 21 and 70 years** (70 or above by special resolution); the **term** shall not exceed **five years** at a time.

**Disqualification (s.164):** a person cannot be appointed as a director if he: (a) is of **unsound mind** declared by a court; (b) is an **undischarged insolvent**; (c) has applied to be adjudicated insolvent and the application is pending; (d) has been **convicted by a court of any offence involving moral turpitude** and sentenced to imprisonment for **not less than six months**, and a period of **five years** has not elapsed since the sentence ended; (e) has been disqualified by a court or Tribunal order; (f) has **not paid any call** on shares held for **six months** from the last day fixed for payment; (g) has been convicted of an offence dealing with **related party transactions (s.188)** at any time in the **preceding five years**; and (h) has not **complied with s.152(3)** (no DIN). **s.164(2):** a director of a company that has **not filed financial statements or annual returns for any continuous period of three financial years**, or that has **failed to repay deposits, redeem debentures on due date or pay interest/dividend and the failure continues for one year or more**, is **disqualified for five years** from being re-appointed in that company and appointed in other companies (**Mukul? v?** – see \*Union of India v Rajeev Kumar? \* for upheld striking-off cases; check recent judgments).

**Vacation of office (s.167):** the office of a director becomes vacant on: disqualification under s.164; absence from **all** board meetings held in **twelve months** (with or without leave); acceptance of an office of profit in contravention of s.188; being disqualified by a court; **resignation (s.168)**, which takes effect from the date of receipt of the notice or the date stated in the notice, whichever is later; and **removal (s.169)**.

**Removal (s.169):** by **ordinary resolution** passed after **special notice (s.115: 14 days)**, in a general meeting. The director has a **right to be heard** and to have a written representation circulated. **Not applicable to** directors appointed by the Tribunal under s.242 or by the principle of **proportional representation (s.163)**. A vacancy so created can be filled by the meeting at which the director is removed.

### 9.2 Powers, Duties and Liabilities

**Duties of directors (s.166):** (1) act in accordance with the **articles**; (2) act in **good faith** to promote the objects of the company for the benefit of its members as a whole, and in the best interests of the company, its employees, shareholders, the community and the protection of the environment; (3) exercise **due and reasonable care, skill and diligence** and **independent judgment**; (4) **avoid conflict of interest**; (5) not achieve or attempt to achieve any **undue gain or advantage**, and if found guilty, pay an amount equal to the gain to the company; and (6) not **assign** the office (assignment is void). **Penalty (s.166(7)):** fine of **not less than Rs. 1 lakh, which may extend to Rs. 5 lakh**.

**Powers of the Board (s.179):** the Board may exercise all powers of the company except those required to be exercised in a general meeting. **s.179(3):** the Board can exercise by **resolutions passed at meetings** certain powers: to make calls; to buy back shares; to issue securities (including debentures); to **borrow money**; to **invest the funds** of the company; to **grant loans or give guarantee**; to approve **financial statements** and the Board’s report; to **diversify the business**; to approve **amalgamation, merger or reconstruction**; to **take over** a company; and other prescribed matters. **s.180: powers requiring a special resolution:** to sell, lease or otherwise dispose of the **whole or substantially the whole of the undertaking**; to invest compensation received on compulsory acquisition; to **borrow money in excess of the aggregate of the paid-up share capital, free reserves and securities premium**; and to **remit or give time for repayment of any debt** due from a director.

**Restrictions and related-party rules:**

- **s.184: Disclosure of interest:** every director must disclose his **concern or interest** in any contract or arrangement at the **first meeting of the Board** in which he participates as a director (and annually, in the first meeting of each financial year, **Form MBP-1**). An **interested director** must not take part in the discussion or vote (s.184(2)). Contravention is punishable with imprisonment up to one year or a fine of Rs. 50,000 to Rs. 1 lakh, or both.

- **s.185: Loans to directors** and persons in whom they are interested are generally prohibited, except as allowed after the 2017 amendment (for example, loans to a wholly owned subsidiary, and guarantees for loans to a subsidiary company, with a **special resolution**, for principal business activities).

- **s.186: Loans and investments:** limited to **60% of the paid-up share capital, free reserves and securities premium**, or **100% of free reserves and securities premium**, whichever is **more**; beyond this requires a **special resolution**. Loans to be charged at interest not lower than the prevailing yield of the one-year, three-year, five-year or ten-year Government security closest to the tenor.

- **s.188: Related-party transactions:** approval by the **Board** (with the interested director not voting) and, beyond the prescribed thresholds, by **ordinary resolution of the shareholders** (related parties do not vote). The company’s transactions at **arm’s length** and in the **ordinary course of business** are exempt from board approval (but not from shareholder approval for listed entities under the SEBI LODR). **s.189:** register of contracts.

**Liabilities:** a director may be liable for breach of **fiduciary duty**, **negligence**, **ultra vires acts**, **misfeasance**, misstatement in prospectus, **fraud (s.447)**, and as an **‘officer who is in default’ (s.2(60))**. **s.447:** punishment for fraud: imprisonment of **six months to ten years**, and a **fine of not less than the amount involved in the fraud, which may extend to three times that amount**; where the fraud involves **public interest**, imprisonment is **not less than three years**.

**Case law:**

- **Re City Equitable Fire Insurance Co.** (1925): the standard of care expected is that of a reasonable person with the director’s own knowledge and experience; a director need not give continuous attention.

- **Regal (Hastings) Ltd. v Gulliver** (1942): a fiduciary must account for secret profits made by use of his position, even if the company could not have taken the opportunity. **Cook v Deeks** (1916): directors cannot take a corporate opportunity for themselves.

- **Foss v Harbottle** (1843): the **rule of majority**; the company (not the shareholder) is the proper plaintiff for wrongs done to it. **Exceptions:** ultra vires acts, fraud on the minority, acts requiring a special majority, infringement of personal rights, and oppression.

- **Oppression and mismanagement (ss.241–244):** a member may apply to the **Tribunal (NCLT)**; **eligibility (s.244)**: in a company with a share capital, **not less than 100 members or one-tenth of the total number of members, whichever is less**, or members holding **not less than one-tenth of the issued share capital**; the Tribunal may **waive** these requirements. Cases: **Shanti Prasad Jain v Kalinga Tubes** (1965); **Needle Industries (India) Ltd. v Needle Industries Newey (India) Holding Ltd.** (1981); **Tata Consultancy Services v Cyrus Investments** (2021: the Supreme Court restored Mr. Cyrus Mistry’s removal as chairman and rejected the oppression claims; the Court held that the removal of a director or chairman for loss of confidence is within the Board’s powers, and no oppression was found).

- **Class action (s.245)** and **derivative suits** by members or depositors.

### 9.3 Meetings: Kinds and Requirements

**Kinds of meetings:** **general meetings** (annual and extraordinary), **Board meetings**, **class meetings**, **creditors’ meetings** (schemes of arrangement, s.230), **meetings of committees**. The **statutory meeting** (s.165 of the 1956 Act) was **abolished** by the 2013 Act.

**Annual General Meeting (s.96):**

- Every company **other than an OPC** must hold an **AGM** in each year, and **not more than fifteen months** may elapse between two AGMs.

- The **first AGM** must be held **within nine months** from the closing of the first financial year; subsequent AGMs **within six months** of the close of the financial year. The **Registrar** may extend the time (**by up to three months**) for AGMs other than the first.

- The AGM must be held during **business hours** (9 a.m. to 6 p.m.) on a day that is **not a national holiday**, at the registered office or any place within the city, town or village where the registered office is located. Under MCA circulars, **virtual AGMs** have been permitted, a measure first introduced during Covid-19 and extended.

- **Default (s.99):** fine up to **Rs. 1 lakh** and a further fine up to **Rs. 5,000 per day** for continuing default. **s.97:** the **Tribunal** can call an AGM on the application of any member if default occurs.

- **Ordinary business at AGM:** consideration of **financial statements and reports** of the Board and auditors; **declaration of dividend**; **appointment of directors in place of those retiring**; and **appointment and fixing of remuneration of auditors**. **Everything else is special business**, which requires an **explanatory statement (s.102)**.

**Extraordinary General Meeting (s.100):** called by the Board on its own, or on a **requisition** by members holding **not less than one-tenth of the paid-up share capital** carrying voting rights (or, if no share capital, one-tenth of the total voting power). The Board must proceed to call a meeting within **21 days** from the date of receipt of the requisition, to be held **within 45 days** of the receipt. If it fails, the **requisitionists** may call the meeting themselves, **within three months** from the date of the requisition, and the company must reimburse their expenses.

**Notice (s.101):** **at least 21 clear days** (in writing or electronic mode), to every member, director, auditor, and others entitled. A meeting may be called on **shorter notice** if consented to by **not less than 95%** of the members entitled to vote (in the case of an AGM, in number; in other meetings, **95% of the paid-up share capital** carrying a right to vote or of the total voting power). **Contents:** day, date, hour, place, and a statement of the business (with explanatory statement for special business). **Omission to give notice** to any member invalidates proceedings, but accidental omission does not (s.101(3)). **Special notice (s.115)** is required for resolutions to **remove a director** (s.169) or appoint an auditor other than the retiring auditor (s.140): **14 days** before the meeting.

**Quorum (s.103):** (a) **public company:** **five** members personally present if the number of members is **not more than 1,000**; **15** if between 1,000 and 5,000; **30** if more than 5,000; (b) **private company:** **two** members personally present. If a quorum is not present within **half an hour**, the meeting is **adjourned to the same day in the next week** at the same time and place (or such other date as the Board determines); at the adjourned meeting, the members present are a quorum (s.103(2)). A meeting called on a requisition by members is **dissolved** if quorum is absent.

**Proxies (s.105):** any member entitled to attend and vote may appoint another person (**need not be a member**) as proxy; a member of a company **without share capital** cannot appoint proxy except as allowed; the proxy has **no right to speak** and **cannot vote on a show of hands** but may demand or join in demanding a **poll**; the instrument (**Form MGT-11**) must be deposited **at least 48 hours before the meeting**; a person can act as proxy for **not more than 50 members**, and for members holding **not more than 10% of the total voting share capital**; proxy is revocable until the commencement of the meeting.

**Voting (ss.107–109) and resolutions (s.114):**

- **Show of hands (s.107):** each member has one vote. **Poll (s.109):** may be demanded by members having not less than **10% of the total voting power** or holding shares on which an aggregate sum of not less than **Rs. 5 lakh** has been paid up; votes are in proportion to the share capital held. **E-voting (s.108)** is mandatory for **listed companies** and companies with **1,000 or more** shareholders (Companies (Management and Administration) Rules, 2014, Rule 20). **Postal ballot (s.110).**

- **Ordinary resolution:** simple majority (votes cast in favour exceed votes cast against). **Special resolution:** the votes cast in favour must be **not less than three times** the votes cast against (**75%** of the votes cast), and the intention to propose it as a special resolution must be specified in the notice. **Resolutions must be filed** with the Registrar within **30 days** (s.117, Form MGT-14) for special resolutions and certain others.

- **Resolution by circulation (s.175):** Board resolutions may be passed by circulation, and must be approved by a **majority of directors** (unless the directors demand a meeting); noted at the next Board meeting.

**Board meetings (ss.173–175):**

- **First Board meeting within 30 days** of incorporation.

- A minimum of **four meetings per year**, with a gap of **not more than 120 days** between two consecutive meetings. **OPC, small companies and dormant companies** must hold at least **one meeting in each half of the calendar year**, with a gap of not less than **90 days**.

- **Notice:** at least **seven days** in writing to every director; a meeting may be convened at **shorter notice** if at least **one independent director** (if the company has any) is present (if absent, decisions are final only on ratification by at least one independent director).

- **Quorum (s.174):** **one-third of the total strength** or **two directors**, whichever is **higher**. **Participation through video conferencing** counts toward the quorum. Certain matters **cannot be dealt with via video conference**: approval of the **annual financial statements, the Board’s report, the prospectus, the audit committee matters, and the approval of takeover, merger or amalgamation** (Rule 4 of Companies (Meetings of Board and its Powers) Rules, 2014, until relaxed by MCA circulars; check the present position).

- **Interested director** must not vote (s.184); decisions are by majority, and the chairman has the **casting vote** if the articles provide.

**Minutes (s.118) and Secretarial Standards:** minutes of all general and Board meetings must be **recorded within 30 days** of the conclusion of the meeting, in minute books, with each page signed and the last page signed and dated by the **chairman** (or the next chairman). Minutes are **conclusive evidence** of the proceedings, unless contradicted (s.118(7)). The **ICSI** issued **Secretarial Standards SS-1 (Board Meetings)** and **SS-2 (General Meetings)** under s.118(10), which are **mandatory**.

**Committees of the Board:** **Audit Committee (s.177)** (every listed company and public companies with paid-up capital of **Rs. 10 crore** or more, turnover of **Rs. 100 crore** or more, or aggregate outstanding loans, debentures and deposits above **Rs. 50 crore**): minimum **three directors**, with **independent directors forming a majority**; **Nomination and Remuneration Committee (s.178)**; **Stakeholders Relationship Committee**; **CSR Committee (s.135)**; **Risk Management Committee** (SEBI LODR).

**Other officers:** **Company Secretary (s.203)**: mandatory for listed companies and public companies with paid-up capital of **Rs. 10 crore** or more; **KMP**.

## Chapter 10: Corporate Social Responsibility (CSR)

### 10.1 Concept and Theories

**Meaning:** CSR is the obligation of a business to act in ways that benefit society and the environment beyond its profit motive and legal minimum. India is widely described as the **first country to legislate a mandatory CSR spending requirement** (Companies Act, 2013, **s.135**, effective **1 April 2014**).

**Key theories and models (frequent concept questions):**

- **Milton Friedman (shareholder theory):** the only social responsibility of business is to increase its profits within the rules of the game.

- **Stakeholder theory (R. Edward Freeman, 1984):** a firm owes duties to all stakeholders (employees, customers, suppliers, community, environment), not only to shareholders.

- **Carroll’s pyramid of CSR (1991):** four layers: **economic** (base), **legal**, **ethical**, **philanthropic** (top).

- **Triple bottom line (John Elkington, 1994):** **People, Planet, Profit**.

- **Gandhian trusteeship** (the Indian roots of CSR): wealth is held in trust for society. Early Indian business philanthropy: the **Tata** and **Birla** groups.

**Soft-law and international frameworks:** the **UN Global Compact** (2000; ten principles on human rights, labour, environment and anti-corruption); **UN Sustainable Development Goals** (2015); the **UN Guiding Principles on Business and Human Rights** (2011); **ISO 26000** (guidance, not a certifiable standard); **OECD Guidelines for Multinational Enterprises**.

**Indian evolution:** MCA **Voluntary Guidelines on CSR, 2009**; DPE guidelines for public sector enterprises (2010); the **National Voluntary Guidelines (NVG) on Social, Environmental and Economic Responsibilities of Business, 2011** (nine principles); **SEBI’s Business Responsibility Report (BRR)**, replaced by the **Business Responsibility and Sustainability Report (BRSR)**, which is mandatory for the **top 1,000 listed companies by market capitalisation** (from FY 2022–23); the **Companies Act, 2013** (s.135, Schedule VII, and the CSR Rules).

### 10.2 Statutory Framework: Section 135, Schedule VII and the CSR Rules

**Sources:** **s.135** of the Companies Act, 2013; **Schedule VII**; the **Companies (Corporate Social Responsibility Policy) Rules, 2014**, substantially amended by the **Companies (CSR Policy) Amendment Rules, 2021** (effective **22 January 2021**); and the **Companies (Amendment) Act, 2019** (unspent CSR provisions) and **2020**.

**Applicability (s.135(1)):** every company, including its **holding or subsidiary**, and a **foreign company** having its branch or project office in India, which in the **immediately preceding financial year** has: (a) **net worth of Rs. 500 crore or more**, or (b) **turnover of Rs. 1,000 crore or more**, or (c) **net profit of Rs. 5 crore or more**. Satisfying **any one** of the conditions suffices. The obligation continues until the company ceases to meet these thresholds for **three consecutive financial years**.

**CSR Committee:**

- The Board of a company to which s.135 applies must constitute a **CSR Committee** of the Board with **three or more directors, at least one of whom is an independent director**. An **unlisted public company or a private company** that is not required to appoint an independent director need have only **two directors**. A **foreign company** must have at least two persons, one resident in India and another nominated by the foreign company.

- Where the company’s **CSR obligation does not exceed Rs. 50 lakh**, the **CSR Committee is not required**, and the functions are discharged by the **Board** (2021 amendment).

- **Functions (s.135(3)):** formulate and recommend a **CSR Policy** indicating the activities to be undertaken (as per Schedule VII); recommend the **amount of expenditure**; and **monitor** the CSR Policy from time to time. The Committee also formulates and recommends an **annual action plan** to the Board.

**Board’s responsibilities (s.135(4)–(5)):** approve the CSR Policy after considering the Committee’s recommendations; disclose the **contents of the policy** in its report and on the **company’s website**; ensure that the activities are undertaken as per the policy; and **ensure that the company spends, in every financial year, at least 2% of the average net profits of the company made during the three immediately preceding financial years**, in pursuance of its CSR Policy. A company **should give preference to the local area** and areas around which it operates. If a company is **newly incorporated** and has not completed three financial years, the average is of the profits for the **immediately preceding financial years** since incorporation.

**Net profit** is calculated as per **s.198** and **excludes** profits arising from **branches outside India** and **dividends received from other Indian companies** that are covered under and complying with s.135.

### 10.3 Schedule VII: Eligible Activities (12 Items)

1.  **Eradicating hunger, poverty and malnutrition**, promoting **health care** including **preventive health care**, **sanitation** (including contribution to the **Swachh Bharat Kosh**) and **making available safe drinking water**.

2.  **Promoting education**, including special education and **employment-enhancing vocation skills**, especially among children, women, elderly and the differently abled, and **livelihood enhancement projects**.

3.  **Promoting gender equality**, empowering women, setting up homes and hostels for women and orphans, **old age homes**, day care centres and such other facilities for senior citizens, and measures for reducing inequalities faced by socially and economically backward groups.

4.  **Ensuring environmental sustainability**, ecological balance, **protection of flora and fauna**, animal welfare, **agroforestry**, conservation of natural resources and maintaining the quality of soil, air and water (including contribution to the **Clean Ganga Fund**).

5.  **Protection of national heritage, art and culture** including restoration of buildings and sites of historical importance and works of art; setting up public libraries; promotion and development of **traditional art and handicrafts**.

6.  Measures for the benefit of **armed forces veterans, war widows and their dependents**, including **Central Armed Police Forces (CAPF)** and **Central Para Military Forces (CPMF)** veterans and their dependents.

7.  **Training to promote rural sports, nationally recognised sports, Paralympic sports and Olympic sports.**

8.  Contribution to the **Prime Minister’s National Relief Fund**, or **PM CARES Fund**, or any other fund set up by the Central Government for **socio-economic development and relief and welfare of the Scheduled Castes, the Scheduled Tribes, other backward classes, minorities and women**.

9.  \(a\) Contribution to **incubators** or **research and development projects** in the field of science, technology, engineering and medicine, funded by the Central Government or State Government or Public Sector Undertaking or any agency of the Central Government or State Government; and (b) contributions to **public-funded Universities; IITs; National Laboratories** and autonomous bodies established under bodies such as the **DAE, DBT, DST, MeitY, ICAR, ICMR and CSIR**, engaged in research in science, technology, engineering and medicine aimed at promoting the SDGs.

10. **Rural development projects.**

11. **Slum area development** (the explanation defines ‘slum area’ as one notified by the Central or State Government).

12. **Disaster management**, including relief, rehabilitation and reconstruction activities.

**Memory aid for the Schedule:** **H**unger/Health, **E**ducation, **G**ender, **E**nvironment, **H**eritage, **V**eterans, **S**ports, **P**M funds, **R**&D, **R**ural, **S**lums, **D**isaster. Items **(viii)** (PM funds) and **(ix)** (R&D) are favourites for ‘which is included’ questions. **Local area preference** and **‘employment-enhancing vocational skills’** are also frequent.

### 10.4 What Does NOT Count as CSR (Rule 2(1)(d) and Rule 4)

- Activities undertaken in the **normal course of business**.

- Any **contribution to a political party** (s.182) or any **political activity**.

- **Projects or programmes or activities that benefit only the employees** of the company and their families.

- **One-off events** such as marathons, awards, charitable contributions, **advertisements, sponsorships of TV programmes or movies** (except as permitted).

- **Activities outside India** (except for training of Indian sports personnel representing any State or Union Territory at national or international level).

- **Expenses incurred for fulfilment of any other statutory obligation** under any law in force in India.

- **Sponsorship activities for deriving marketing benefits** for products or services.

### 10.5 Implementation, Unspent CSR and Compliance

- **Modes of implementation (Rule 4):** directly, or through a **Section 8 company, registered public trust or registered society** established by the company (alone or with others) or by the Central or State Government, or any entity established under an Act of Parliament or State legislature, or **any other Section 8 company, registered trust or society** with an established track record of **three years** in similar activities. **Registration (Form CSR-1)** with the Registrar of Companies is **mandatory for implementing agencies** from **1 April 2021**, each receiving a **unique CSR registration number**. Implementing agencies must also hold **12A and 80G** registrations under the Income-tax Act, 1961.

- **Annual action plan (Rule 5(2)):** to include the list of CSR projects, the manner of execution, the modalities of utilisation of funds, monitoring and reporting, and the details of the need and impact assessment, if any.

- **Ongoing project (Rule 2(1)(i)):** a multi-year project, **not exceeding three years excluding the year of commencement**, with timelines approved by the Board on the CSR Committee’s recommendation.

- **Unspent CSR (s.135(5)–(6)) and Rule 7:**

  - **Ongoing projects:** the unspent amount must be **transferred to a special bank account (the Unspent CSR Account)** within **30 days from the end of the financial year**, to be spent within **three financial years** from the date of transfer. If it is not spent in that period, it must be transferred to a **Fund specified in Schedule VII** within **30 days** of the end of the three financial years.

  - **Non-ongoing projects:** the unspent amount must be transferred to a **Fund specified in Schedule VII** within **six months** from the end of the financial year.

- **Set-off (Rule 7(3)):** where a company spends **in excess** of its 2% obligation, the excess may be set off against the CSR requirement of the **succeeding three financial years**, as per the Board’s resolution. **Surplus arising out of CSR activities** is **not part of business profit** and must be ploughed back or transferred.

- **Administrative overheads:** shall **not exceed 5%** of the total CSR expenditure of the company for the financial year.

- **Impact assessment (Rule 8(3)):** companies with an **average CSR obligation of Rs. 10 crore or more** in the three immediately preceding financial years must undertake an **impact assessment through an independent agency** for projects with an outlay of **Rs. 1 crore or more** that have been completed **not less than one year** before the assessment. The expenditure on impact assessment may be counted up to **5% of the total CSR expenditure for the year, or Rs. 50 lakh, whichever is higher**.

- **Capital assets** created or acquired through CSR funds must be held by the **beneficiaries**, a **registered public trust or society or Section 8 company** (not by the company itself).

- **Reporting:** a **brief outline of the CSR Policy** and the **composition of the CSR Committee** in the Board’s report (**Annexure**); **Form CSR-2** filed with the Registrar (as an addendum to **AOC-4**) from FY 2020–21; and **web display** of the CSR Committee composition, policy and approved projects.

- **Penalty (s.135(7), as amended 2019/2020):** if a company fails to transfer the unspent amount as required, the company is **liable to a penalty of twice the amount required to be transferred, or Rs. 1 crore, whichever is less**, and every **officer in default** is liable to a penalty of **one-tenth of the amount required to be transferred, or Rs. 2 lakh, whichever is less**. The older **‘comply or explain’** regime (non-spending with reasons) was replaced by this **mandatory** regime. The 2020 amendment **decriminalised** the offence (it earlier carried imprisonment).

### 10.6 CSR: Analysis Points and Recent Trends for MCQs

- **Criticism:** CSR as a **statutory 2% ‘tax’** versus a voluntary ethic; spending concentrated in a few states and sectors (education and health dominate; the North-east and aspirational districts get less); ‘box-ticking’; the **capacity of implementing agencies**; the **local-area preference** versus national needs.

- **Trend:** rising share of CSR funds towards **health care, education and rural development**; during the Covid-19 pandemic, **contributions to PM CARES** were counted as CSR, and MCA FAQs clarified that Covid-19 related expenses are eligible under the health care and disaster management items.

- **Linkage with the directors’ duties:** **s.166(2)** requires directors to act in the best interests of the company, its employees, shareholders, the community and the protection of the environment.

- **Link with sustainability disclosures:** BRSR, ESG and the SDGs.

- **Likely traps:** (i) the three alternative thresholds (500 / 1,000 / 5 crore: *net worth / turnover / net profit*); (ii) 2% of the **average net profit of three preceding years**, not of the current year’s profit; (iii) CSR Committee **not needed if the obligation is up to Rs. 50 lakh**; (iv) **30 days / 3 years / 6 months** for unspent amounts; (v) **political contributions** and **employee-only benefits** are excluded; (vi) **overheads 5%**; (vii) **Form CSR-1 and CSR-2**; (viii) penalty **twice the unspent amount or Rs. 1 crore, whichever is less**.

### 11.3 Landmark Cases Master List (One-Line Principle)

- **Carlill v Carbolic Smoke Ball:** general offer, acceptance by performance.

- **Lalman Shukla v Gauri Dutt:** knowledge of the offer is needed.

- **Bhagwandas Kedia:** instantaneous communication; contract formed where acceptance is received.

- **Mohori Bibee v Dharmodas Ghose:** a minor’s agreement is void ab initio.

- **Chinnaya v Ramayya:** a stranger to consideration may sue.

- **M.C. Chacko v SBT:** a beneficiary may enforce in trust-like arrangements.

- **Gherulal Parakh:** wagering agreements are void, not illegal.

- **Central Inland Water Transport v Brojo Nath:** unconscionable terms are void.

- **Satyabrata Ghose:** s.56 is wider than the English frustration doctrine.

- **Energy Watchdog v CERC:** force majeure is distinct from s.56.

- **Fateh Chand; Kailash Nath:** s.74 reasonable compensation.

- **Hadley v Baxendale:** remoteness of damages.

- **State of West Bengal v B.K. Mondal:** s.70 quasi-contract.

- **Mafatlal Industries:** unjust enrichment in refund claims.

- **Bank of Bihar v Damodar Prasad:** the surety’s liability is immediate and co-extensive.

- **Rowland v Divall:** breach of the implied condition of title gives a full refund.

- **Cox v Hickman:** mutual agency is the test of partnership.

- **Salomon v Salomon:** corporate personality.

- **Royal British Bank v Turquand:** indoor management.

- **Foss v Harbottle:** majority rule and the proper plaintiff.

- **Rangappa v Sri Mohan; Bir Singh v Mukesh Kumar:** presumption under s.139.

- **Dashrath Rupsingh Rathod:** jurisdiction under s.138 (superseded by the 2015 amendment).

- **Tata Consultancy Services v Cyrus Investments:** oppression and the removal of the chairman.

### 11.4 Recent Amendments and Developments Snapshot

- **Specific Relief (Amendment) Act, 2018:** specific performance is no longer discretionary; substituted performance; infrastructure courts.

- **NI (Amendment) Acts 2015 and 2018:** jurisdiction (s.142(2)) and interim compensation (ss.143A, 148).

- **Companies (Amendment) Acts 2015, 2017, 2019, 2020:** removal of minimum capital; commencement-of-business declaration (s.10A, 2019); charge-registration timelines; unspent CSR; decriminalisation of many offences; and the small-company thresholds.

- **Jan Vishwas (Amendment of Provisions) Act, 2023:** rationalised penalties and decriminalised minor offences in many Acts (the extent of change varies from Act to Act, so check the schedule).

- **LLP (Amendment) Act, 2021:** small LLPs, lower penalties and compounding.

- **New criminal codes and evidence law:** from **1 July 2024**, the **Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita, 2023**, **Bharatiya Nagarik Suraksha Sanhita, 2023** and **Bharatiya Sakshya Adhiniyam, 2023** replaced the IPC, CrPC and Evidence Act. References to the IPC in s.15 of the Contract Act are read with the BNS, and s.138 NI Act procedure now follows the BNSS.

- **CSR Rules, 2021:** mandatory registration of implementing agencies, the Unspent CSR Account, impact assessment, and the Rs. 50 lakh exemption from forming a CSR Committee.

### 11.5 Tactics for Each Question Type

- **Statement-based:** underline absolute words (‘only’, ‘always’, ‘never’, ‘must’). Many wrong statements swap a **number** (30 and 15 days; 20% and 25%) or a **section** (s.73 and s.74).

- **Match the following:** learn section-to-topic pairs in tables (s.124–147 indemnity and guarantee; s.148–181 bailment; s.172–179 pledge; s.182–238 agency).

- **Assertion–Reason:** decide separately whether A and R are true, and whether R explains A. Doctrines (frustration, Turquand, veil lifting) often yield ‘both true but R does not explain A’.

- **Sequence questions:** for incorporation, the order is: name approval, then DIN and DSC, then MoA and AoA, then filing under s.7, then the certificate, then the registered office within 30 days, then INC-20A within 180 days. For s.138 the order is: dishonour, then notice (30 days), then 15 days to pay, then complaint (1 month).

- **Negative questions:** be ready with the exception lists (s.19 Partnership Act implied-authority exceptions; s.25 exceptions; s.27 goodwill exception; s.30 minors; s.230 agent’s personal liability).
