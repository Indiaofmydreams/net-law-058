## Chapter 1: Nature and Definition of Tort

### 1.1 Meaning and origin

The word *tort* comes from the Latin *tortum*, meaning "twisted" or "wrong", and came into English through Norman French. A tort is a **civil wrong** for which the law provides a remedy, typically in the form of **unliquidated damages** (damages not fixed in advance, left to the court to assess). It protects interests such as bodily integrity, reputation, property, liberty, and economic interests.

In India the law of torts is largely **uncodified and judge-made**, drawing on English common law, modified by Indian conditions (including constitutional values), with statutes covering specific areas (Consumer Protection Act, Motor Vehicles Act, Competition Act, etc.). Section 2(m) of the **Limitation Act 1963** defines tort in a negative way: "a civil wrong which is not exclusively a breach of contract or the breach of a trust."

### 1.2 Key definitions (frequently matched with authors)

- **Winfield:** Tortious liability arises from the breach of a duty primarily fixed by law; this duty is towards persons generally, and its breach is redressible by an action for unliquidated damages. *Key points: duty fixed by law, owed to persons generally, unliquidated damages.*

- **Salmond:** A civil wrong for which the remedy is a common law action for unliquidated damages, and which is not exclusively the breach of a contract or the breach of a trust or other merely equitable obligation.

- **Fraser:** An infringement of a right *in rem* of a private individual giving a right of compensation at the suit of the injured party.

- **Underhill/others:** Emphasise that tort is breach of a duty fixed by law.

**Memory hook:** *Winfield → duty primarily fixed by law, towards persons generally. Salmond → civil wrong, not exclusively contract or trust. Fraser → right in rem.*

### 1.3 Essential elements of a tort

1.  **A wrongful act or omission** by the defendant (conduct which the law treats as wrongful, whether intentional, negligent, or sometimes without fault, as in strict liability).

2.  **Legal damage** to the plaintiff, meaning violation of a legal right. Some torts need proof of actual damage (negligence, nuisance); others are **actionable per se** (trespass, libel).

3.  **A legal remedy** in the form of an action for damages (or other relief such as injunction).

### 1.4 Foundational maxims

- **Ubi jus ibi remedium:** where there is a right, there is a remedy. The foundation of tort law; *Ashby v White* (1703) illustrates it (voter wrongfully denied vote could sue though no loss was proved).

- **Injuria sine damno:** violation of a legal right without actual loss; actionable. Cases: *Ashby v White*; in India, *Bhim Singh v State of J&K*; the classic Indian illustration often cited is *Rogers v Rajendro Dutt* (1860).

- **Damnum sine injuria:** actual loss without violation of a legal right; not actionable. Cases: *Gloucester Grammar School v Bradford* (teacher opening rival school; students left), *Mogul Steamship Co v McGregor Gow & Co* (lawful competition), *Acton v Blundell* and *Chasemore v Richards* (abstracting underground water), *Bradford Corporation v Pickles* (motive irrelevant when exercising a lawful right), *Town Area Committee v Prabhu Dayal* (Indian, demolition of unauthorized construction).

**Concept trap:** Do not confuse *damnum* (loss) with *injuria* (legal wrong). *Damnum sine injuria* is the one that is NOT actionable.

### 1.5 Mental element: intention, motive, malice

- **Intention:** the desire to bring about a consequence, or knowledge that it is substantially certain.

- **Motive:** the underlying reason; generally **irrelevant** in tort (*Bradford Corporation v Pickles*; *Allen v Flood*), because a lawful act does not become unlawful merely because of a bad motive.

- **Malice** (in law: wrongful act done intentionally without just cause) matters in a few torts: **nuisance** (*Christie v Davey*, deliberate banging to annoy neighbours; *Hollywood Silver Fox Farm v Emmett*), **malicious prosecution**, **conspiracy** (*Quinn v Leathem*), **defamation** (defeats qualified privilege and fair comment), and **malicious falsehood**.

- **Negligence:** carelessness in breach of a duty; **Recklessness:** conscious disregard of risk.

### 1.6 Theories of liability: pigeon-hole vs general principle

- **Salmond's "pigeon-hole" theory:** Law of *Torts*. There are only specific named torts (assault, battery, defamation, nuisance etc.). If a wrong does not fit a recognised category, no liability.

- **Winfield's general theory:** Law of *Tort*. There is a general principle that every harm caused by wrongful conduct is actionable unless justified, so new torts can develop.

- **Position today:** The courts are closer to Winfield; the categories remain open, as shown by the growth of negligence (*Donoghue v Stevenson*), privacy-related claims, and constitutional torts in India.

### 1.7 Tort distinguished from other concepts

**Tort vs Crime**

- Tort: private wrong; remedy is compensation; action by injured party; no necessity of mens rea in all torts; no codified list.

- Crime: public wrong; remedy is punishment; prosecution by State; mens rea normally required; defined in statute (now the Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita 2023).

- Same act may be both (assault, defamation, negligent driving causing death, nuisance). *Rule in Smith v Selwyn*, once barred civil suits for felony until prosecution; not followed in modern law.

**Tort vs Breach of contract**

- Contract: duty arises from agreement of parties, owed to specific person (privity), damages may be liquidated, and there is a right to enforce promised performance.

- Tort: duty fixed by law, owed to persons generally, damages unliquidated.

- Overlap: a doctor, carrier, or bailee may be liable in both. *Donoghue v Stevenson* removed the privity limitation that *Winterbottom v Wright* imposed.

**Tort vs Breach of trust:** breach of trust is an equitable obligation remedied by equity; tort is a common law wrong.

**Tort vs Quasi-contract:** quasi-contract (Sections 68–72, Indian Contract Act 1872) is based on unjust enrichment, not wrongdoing.

### 1.8 Remedies in tort

1.  **Judicial remedies**

    - **Damages:** *nominal* (right violated, no loss, e.g., *Constantine v Imperial Hotels*), *contemptuous* (technical right but claim is worthless), *substantial/compensatory*, *aggravated* (injury to feelings), *exemplary/punitive* (only in limited categories laid down in *Rookes v Devlin* (1964): oppressive, arbitrary or unconstitutional action by government servants; conduct calculated to make profit exceeding compensation; where authorised by statute; confirmed in *Cassell v Broome*).

    - **Injunction:** temporary or perpetual, prohibitory or mandatory (nuisance, trespass, defamation).

    - **Specific restitution of property** (recovery of goods or land).

2.  **Extra-judicial remedies (self-help)**

    - Self-defence, prevention of trespass, re-entry on land, **recaption** of goods, **abatement of nuisance**, **distress damage feasant** (seizing trespassing cattle until compensation is paid), and expulsion of trespasser.

**Indian development:** constitutional courts award **public law compensation** under Articles 32 and 226 for violation of Article 21 (e.g., *Rudul Sah*, *Nilabati Behera*); this is distinct from private law tort damages (discussed in Topic 7).

### 1.9 Discharge and survival of tort liability

- **Death:** historical maxim *actio personalis moritur cum persona* (personal action dies with the person). In India, **Section 306, Indian Succession Act 1925** provides that causes of action survive to the executor, **except** defamation, assault, and personal injuries not causing death of the party (and also where the party dies in other personal injury cases).

- **Waiver / Accord and satisfaction / Release / Limitation** (Limitation Act 1963 prescribes periods; for example, one year for defamation, malicious prosecution, false imprisonment; three years for general torts like conversion).

## Chapter 2: General Principles of Tortious Liability

### 2.1 Basic conditions

The three general ingredients are (i) wrongful act or omission, (ii) legal damage, and (iii) a remedy. Within this, the courts also examine **fault** (intention, negligence), **causation** and **remoteness**, and **defences**. Liability may be:

- **Fault-based** (intention or negligence), the general rule.

- **Strict liability** (*Rylands v Fletcher*), where fault need not be proved but exceptions exist.

- **Absolute liability** (*M.C. Mehta*), no exceptions.

- **Vicarious liability:** one person liable for the tort of another.

### 2.2 Wrongful act and omission

An **act** is a positive wrong (battery, defamation). An **omission** is liable only when there is a **legal duty to act** (e.g., occupier's duty to make premises safe, parent's duty to child, a doctor's duty to a patient, a person who creates a danger). Mere moral duty (rescuing a stranger) generally gives no tort liability in English law, although rescuers who are injured can sue the wrongdoer (*Haynes v Harwood*).

### 2.3 Legal damage and its types

- **Actionable per se:** trespass to person/land/goods, libel. Proof of loss is unnecessary.

- **Actionable only on proof of special damage:** negligence, public nuisance (special damage to plaintiff), slander (subject to exceptions), malicious prosecution.

### 2.4 Parties: who can sue and who can be sued

- **The State:** liable under Article 300 of the Constitution, subject to the sovereign function distinction (Topic 7).

- **Corporations:** may sue and be sued; liable for torts committed by employees in the course of business (*Lennard's Carrying Co v Asiatic Petroleum*, alter-ego theory; *Citizens Life Assurance Co v Brown* where the company was held liable for libel).

- **Minors:** liable for torts, but the court considers age and capacity to form intent (e.g., *Walmsley v Humenick*, young child not liable for intentional tort).

- **Persons of unsound mind:** liable if mental state is not such as to exclude the mental element (*Morriss v Marsden*: an insane person who understood the nature of the act was liable).

- **Husband and wife:** in India married persons can sue each other as separate legal persons; interspousal immunity is not recognised in general.

- **Trade unions:** liable (*Taff Vale Railway v ASRS*), although later statute gave partial immunity in England.

- **Foreign sovereigns and diplomats:** immunity, subject to Section 86, CPC and the Diplomatic Relations (Vienna Convention) Act 1972.

- **Joint tortfeasors:** persons who act together in furtherance of a common design, or whose independent acts produce one damage, are jointly and severally liable. The plaintiff may sue any or all; release of one does not release others (in India, under Contribution rules, *Merryweather v Nixan* (no contribution) has been displaced in many jurisdictions; in exams, remember the classical rule and the modern right to contribution).

### 2.5 Vicarious liability

**Meaning:** liability of one person for the wrong of another due to their relationship. Latin maxims: *qui facit per alium facit per se* (he who acts through another acts himself) and *respondeat superior* (let the master answer).

**Master and servant**

- A master is liable for torts committed by the servant **in the course of employment**.

- **Tests of a servant:** control test, organisation/integration test, multiple/economic reality test. A servant is under control as to *how* work is done; an independent contractor only as to *what* is done.

- **Course of employment:** acts authorised, or **unauthorised mode** of doing an authorised act (*Limpus v London General Omnibus Co*, driver racing to obstruct a rival bus; master liable). A *prohibited act* (not merely prohibited mode) is outside employment (*Conway v Wimpey*, driver giving lift against orders; *Twine v Beans Express*). But *Rose v Plenty* (milk float boy; master liable because the act was for the master's business despite prohibition).

- **Acts of fraud/crime:** *Lloyd v Grace, Smith & Co* (solicitor's managing clerk defrauded client; firm liable); *Century Insurance v Northern Ireland Road Transport Board* (tanker driver's smoking during petrol delivery; liable); *Poland v Parr* (force in defence of master's property). In modern English law the test is a **close connection** with the employment (*Lister v Hesley Hall*, 2001; *Various Claimants v Catholic Child Welfare Society*; *Mohamud v Morrisons*).

- **Loaned servants:** *Mersey Docks and Harbour Board v Coggins & Griffith* — liability lies with the person having **control** at the time.

**Other relationships**

- **Principal and agent:** principal liable for agent's torts within authority.

- **Partners:** liable jointly and severally for torts committed in the course of firm's business (Section 26–27, Indian Partnership Act 1932).

- **Independent contractor:** employer generally not liable, with **exceptions**: negligence in selecting contractor; **non-delegable duties** (e.g., hazardous operations, duty to maintain highway, strict liability torts); employer authorising the wrongful act; ultra-hazardous work (*Bower v Peate*).

**Indian illustrations:** *State of Rajasthan v Vidhyawati* (vicarious liability of the State for the negligent driving of a government driver); *Saheli v Commissioner of Police*; *Municipal Corporation of Delhi v Subhagwanti*.

**Rationale (memory):** (i) deep pocket / better ability to pay, (ii) control over employee, (iii) the master benefits from the employee's work, (iv) deterrence and risk distribution through insurance.

### 2.6 Strict, absolute and statutory no-fault liability (overview; details in Topics 6, 8, 9)

Exceptional cases where liability arises regardless of fault: *Rylands v Fletcher* (1868), *M.C. Mehta v UOI* (1987) absolute liability for hazardous industries, Public Liability Insurance Act 1991, NGT Act 2010, Motor Vehicles Act (no-fault compensation), Employees' Compensation Act 1923, and CPA 2019 product liability.

### 2.7 Constitutional torts

Indian courts have recognised a *public law* remedy of monetary compensation for violations of fundamental rights, particularly **Article 21** (life and personal liberty), enforceable by the Supreme Court (Art. 32) and High Courts (Art. 226). Key cases: *Rudul Sah v State of Bihar* (1983), *Bhim Singh v State of J&K* (1985), *Sebastian Hongray v UOI* (1984), *Nilabati Behera v State of Orissa* (1993), *D.K. Basu v State of West Bengal* (1997). The Supreme Court holds that sovereign immunity is not a defence in public law proceedings, and that the compensation is without prejudice to a private law suit in tort.

## Chapter 3: General Defences in Tort

A defence is a justification or excuse that negates liability even when the elements of a tort are present. The classical list is: **volenti non fit injuria, necessity, private defence, act of God, inevitable accident, mistake, statutory authority, plaintiff's default (contributory negligence), judicial and executive acts, parental/quasi-parental authority.**

### 3.1 Volenti non fit injuria (consent)

**Meaning:** to a willing person, no injury is done. If the plaintiff voluntarily agrees to take the risk of harm, he cannot sue.

**Conditions:**

1.  The plaintiff must have **full knowledge** of the risk (not merely that danger exists: *Dann v Hamilton*, passenger who got into car with a drunk driver; the House still considered volenti not established).

2.  The plaintiff must have **freely and voluntarily agreed** to the risk (not under compulsion or pressure of duty).

3.  The act must be **within the scope of the consent** (consent to a medical operation is not consent to a different operation).

**Cases:**

- *Hall v Brooklands Auto Racing Club* — spectator at car race accepted the risk of cars leaving the track.

- *Smith v Baker* — employee's knowledge of danger is not by itself consent; **knowledge is not assent**.

- *Nettleship v Weston* — a driving instructor who checked insurance did not consent to the risk of negligent driving; volenti failed.

- *Wooldridge v Sumner* — sports participant accepts risks of a game played according to rules.

- **Rescue cases:** *Haynes v Harwood*; *Baker v T.E. Hopkins & Son* (doctor who tried to rescue workers in a well; volenti rejected). The rule: rescuer's act is natural and probable and not voluntary assumption of risk.

- **Exceptions:** volenti does not apply in cases of **statutory duty**, **rescue**, and where the defendant's act is **unlawful** (e.g., consent to being unlawfully injured).

- **Consent and medical treatment:** *Samira Kohli v Dr Prabha Manchanda* (2008) — valid consent requires adequate information; consent for diagnostic procedure does not extend to therapeutic procedure unless emergency.

- **Limits under CPA:** exclusion clauses cannot defeat statutory consumer rights.

**Volenti vs contributory negligence:** volenti is a **complete defence** (voluntary assumption of risk); contributory negligence is a **partial defence** (reduces damages).

### 3.2 Necessity

An act done to prevent greater harm may be justified. Two forms: **private necessity** (to protect one's own interest) and **public necessity** (to protect the community).

- *Cope v Sharpe* — trespass to land to prevent a fire from spreading to the landlord's property justified.

- *Leigh v Gladstone* — force-feeding a prisoner on hunger strike held justified (preservation of life).

- *Carter v Thomas* — entering to extinguish a fire.

- *Esso Petroleum v Southport Corporation* — discharge of oil to save the ship.

- *Kirk v Gregory* — mere removal of jewellery to what the defendant thought a safer place was not necessity (unnecessary act causes loss).

- **Medical necessity:** treating an unconscious patient (*F v West Berkshire Health Authority*, in a best-interest context).

- Under Indian law, necessity also appears in criminal law (BNS general exceptions); in tort, it must be **reasonable and proportionate**.

### 3.3 Private defence (self-defence)

Any person may use **reasonable force** to protect self, family, or property from unlawful aggression. The force must be **proportionate**; excessive force is not a defence. *Bird v Holbrook* — spring-gun set to protect a garden; the defendant was liable because the danger was disproportionate and unannounced, also affecting an innocent person. Under criminal law, the right of private defence is codified in the **Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita 2023** (replacing IPC Sections 96–106); the tort rule parallels that.

### 3.4 Act of God (*vis major*)

A natural event, **extraordinary and unforeseeable**, without human intervention, so overwhelming that no reasonable human foresight or care could have prevented it.

- *Nichols v Marsland* (1876) — extraordinary rainfall breaking artificial lakes; defendant not liable.

- *Greenock Corporation v Caledonian Railway* — extraordinary rainfall; not Act of God because it could have been prevented by better works.

- **Essentials:** (i) natural forces, (ii) unforeseeable and extraordinary, (iii) no human agency, (iv) an incapacity to guard against it by reasonable care.

- It is an **exception to strict liability** (*Rylands v Fletcher*) but is **not available in absolute liability** (*M.C. Mehta*).

### 3.5 Inevitable accident

An accident that could not have been avoided by **ordinary care, caution and skill**. The burden of proof lies on the defendant. *Stanley v Powell* — a shot rebounded from a tree and injured a beater; the defendant was not liable. *Brown v Kendall* — accidental injury while separating fighting dogs. *Holmes v Mather* — a runaway horse. Key distinction: **inevitable accident** relates to a *physical event* not preventable with care, whereas **Act of God** is a specific natural event.

### 3.6 Mistake

Mistake of **fact** or **law** is generally **not a defence** in tort, although it can negate intention in rare cases, e.g., malicious prosecution (reasonable and probable cause). *Consolidated Co v Curtis* (an auctioneer sold goods of the wrong owner; liable for conversion); *Hollins v Fowler* (honest purchase for the principal was still conversion). Mistake is a defence in **defamation** only in a limited sense of **unintentional defamation** under statutory frameworks (*Hulton v Jones*, the defendant was liable even though the mistake was innocent).

### 3.7 Statutory authority

Acts authorised by statute are not actionable if done within the powers and without negligence.

- *Vaughan v Taff Vale Railway* — sparks from a locomotive set fire to the plaintiff's woods; railway not liable because it used proper care and the statute authorised the engine.

- *Hammersmith Rail Co v Brand* — vibration damage from railway operation; no remedy under common law.

- *Metropolitan Asylum District v Hill* — hospital for smallpox patients; the statute was **permissive**, not mandatory, so nuisance was not justified.

- **Rule:** if the statute is **mandatory**, the defendant is protected even from inevitable nuisance; if **permissive**, the defendant must exercise powers without causing nuisance.

- **Indian case:** *Municipal Corporation of Delhi v Subhagwanti* (1966) — collapse of the clock tower; statutory authority does not excuse negligence.

### 3.8 Plaintiff's default and contributory negligence

Where plaintiff's own negligence contributes to the harm, the damages are **apportioned** under the **Law Reform (Contributory Negligence) Act 1945** (England); Indian courts follow the same principle of apportionment even without a statute (Motor Vehicles claims often apply it).

- *Butterfield v Forrester* (1809) — plaintiff riding fast, failed to see an obstruction; no recovery under the old law.

- *Davies v Mann* — the **last opportunity rule**; the defendant who had the last clear chance to avoid the accident was liable.

- *British Columbia Electric Railway v Loach* — a modification of the last opportunity rule.

- **Children:** a child is judged by the standard of a child of that age (*Jones v Boyce*, in the context of rescue and reasonable reaction).

- **Standard:** the plaintiff must also take reasonable care of **himself** (e.g., seat belts: *Froom v Butcher*, damages reduced by 15–25% for not wearing seat belts).

- **Effect:** partial defence (reduction of damages), not a complete bar in modern law.

### 3.9 Judicial and executive acts

- **Judicial acts:** a judge acting judicially is immune, even if erroneous or malicious, provided he acts within jurisdiction. In India, the **Judicial Officers' Protection Act 1850** gives protection for acts done in good faith; *Anderson v Gorrie*; *Sirros v Moore*.

- **Executive acts:** acts of the executive in the exercise of authorised statutory power are protected; if they are done in excess of authority, the officer is personally liable.

- **Quasi-judicial acts:** same protection when acting judicially.

### 3.10 Parental and quasi-parental authority

Parents, teachers and persons in loco parentis may inflict **reasonable and moderate chastisement** for the child's correction. The protection extends to teachers (in India, subject to the **Right to Education Act 2009, Section 17**, which prohibits physical punishment and mental harassment). Excessive punishment is actionable and criminal.

## Chapter 4: Specific Torts: Negligence, Nuisance, Trespass and Defamation

### 4.1 Negligence: Definition and nature

Negligence has two senses: (i) a **mental state** (carelessness) and (ii) an independent **tort** (breach of a legal duty to take care resulting in damage). The classic definition is by **Alderson B in *Blyth v Birmingham Waterworks Co* (1856):** negligence is the omission to do something which a reasonable man, guided by considerations which ordinarily regulate human affairs, would do, or doing something which a prudent and reasonable man would not do. Winfield defines it as the breach of a legal duty to take care which results in damage, undesired by the defendant, to the plaintiff.

### 4.2 Negligence: Essential ingredients (three)

1.  **Duty of care** owed by the defendant to the plaintiff.

2.  **Breach of that duty** (failure to meet the standard of a reasonable person).

3.  **Consequential damage** caused by the breach and not too remote.

The **burden of proof** is generally on the plaintiff (exception: *res ipsa loquitur*).

### 4.3 Negligence: Duty of care: development of the test

- ***Donoghue v Stevenson* (1932):** a snail in a ginger beer bottle; Lord Atkin's **neighbour principle**: you must take reasonable care to avoid acts or omissions which you can reasonably foresee would be likely to injure your neighbour, meaning persons so closely and directly affected that you ought reasonably to have them in contemplation. This case created the modern tort of negligence and removed the need for privity of contract (*Winterbottom v Wright* limited).

- ***Anns v Merton LBC* (1978):** a two-stage test (proximity/foreseeability; then policy considerations to negative or limit the duty). Later disapproved.

- ***Caparo Industries v Dickman* (1990):** the modern **three-stage test**: (i) damage must be **reasonably foreseeable**, (ii) there must be **proximity** between the parties, and (iii) it must be **fair, just and reasonable** to impose a duty. Auditors owed no duty to potential investors.

- ***Hedley Byrne v Heller* (1964):** liability for **negligent misstatement** causing pure economic loss where there is a special relationship and reliance (disclaimer protected the defendant there).

- ***Heaven v Pender*:** an early attempt at a general duty principle (Brett MR).

### 4.4 Negligence: Standard of care

The standard is that of the **reasonable man** (*Vaughan v Menlove*: the hay-rick case, an objective standard regardless of the defendant's actual intellect). Factors: probability of harm, gravity of harm, cost of precautions, and social utility of the activity (*Bolton v Stone*, a cricket ball hit out of the ground; *Paris v Stepney Borough Council*, a one-eyed worker owed greater care; *Latimer v AEC*).

- **Professionals:** the standard of an ordinary skilled person in that profession. ***Bolam v Friern Hospital Management Committee* (1957):** a doctor is not negligent if he acts in accordance with a practice accepted as proper by a responsible body of medical opinion. (*Bolitho* added that the opinion must withstand logical analysis.)

- **Children:** judged by a child of the same age (*Mullin v Richards*). **Learners:** *Nettleship v Weston* (objective standard of a competent driver). **Specially skilled:** the standard of one who professes that skill.

### 4.5 Negligence: Breach and *res ipsa loquitur*

***Res ipsa loquitur*** ("the thing speaks for itself") is a rule of evidence by which negligence is **inferred** from the very occurrence when: (i) the thing causing damage was under the defendant's control, (ii) the accident would not ordinarily occur without negligence, and (iii) there is no explanation of the cause. It shifts the **evidential burden** to the defendant. Cases: ***Byrne v Boadle* (1863)** (a barrel of flour fell from a warehouse window), *Scott v London & St Katherine Docks* (sugar bags), ***Shyam Sundar v State of Rajasthan* (1974)** (SC applied the doctrine in a truck accident and held that the doctrine can apply in India), *Pushpabai Purshottam Udeshi v Ranjit Ginning & Pressing Co* (1977), *Municipal Corporation of Delhi v Subhagwanti* (1966). The doctrine **does not apply** where the facts themselves disclose the cause or where the defendant lacks control. It is a rule of **evidence**, not a rule of law that creates liability.

### 4.6 Negligence: Medical negligence in India (high-yield)

- ***Dr. Laxman Balkrishna Joshi v Dr. Trimbak Bapu Godbole* (1969):** a doctor owes the patient a duty to exercise a reasonable degree of skill and knowledge, deciding on treatment and administering it with care.

- ***Indian Medical Association v V.P. Shantha* (1995):** medical services (other than free services to all) fall under "service" in the Consumer Protection Act.

- ***Jacob Mathew v State of Punjab* (2005):** for **criminal negligence** of a doctor, **gross** negligence or recklessness must be shown (a higher threshold than civil negligence); the court approved *Bolam* and laid down guidelines for prosecution (a prior credible opinion from another competent doctor).

- ***Spring Meadows Hospital v Harjol Ahluwalia* (1998):** liability of hospital and doctors; parent as consumer for a child's treatment.

- ***Samira Kohli v Prabha Manchanda* (2008):** informed consent.

- ***Kusum Sharma v Batra Hospital* (2010):** guidelines on medical negligence; the standard is of an ordinary competent doctor.

- ***V. Kishan Rao v Nikhil Super Speciality Hospital* (2010):** expert evidence is not always mandatory; *res ipsa loquitur* may apply.

### 4.7 Negligence: Damage, causation and remoteness (summary; details in Topic 5)

The plaintiff must prove that the breach **caused** the damage: the **"but for" test** (*Barnett v Chelsea & Kensington Hospital*: poisoning case; the hospital's negligence did not cause death because the patient would have died anyway). If multiple causes, **material contribution** (*Bonnington Castings v Wardlaw*). **Novus actus interveniens** (new intervening act) may break the chain.

### 4.8 Negligence: Nervous shock (psychiatric injury)

- *Hambrook v Stokes* (mother's fear for children); *Bourhill v Young* (a bystander who heard an accident was not owed a duty as she was not foreseeably affected); ***McLoughlin v O'Brian* (1983)** (mother saw injured family at hospital shortly afterwards).

- ***Alcock v Chief Constable of South Yorkshire Police* (1992)** (Hillsborough): for secondary victims, control mechanisms: a **close tie of love and affection**, **proximity in time and space** to the event or its immediate aftermath, and **perception through one's own unaided senses** (live television broadcasts were not enough).

- *Page v Smith* (primary victims: no need to prove foreseeable psychiatric injury if some physical injury was foreseeable).

### 4.9 Negligence: Pure economic loss and omissions

Generally not recoverable in negligence unless a special relationship exists: *Spartan Steel v Martin & Co* (cable cut; loss of profit from the physical damage recoverable, but not purely economic loss); *Murphy v Brentwood DC*; *Hedley Byrne* is the major exception. **Omissions:** no general duty to rescue; duty arises where a special relationship, control of a danger, or an assumption of responsibility exists (*Smith v Littlewoods*).

### 4.10 Negligence: Defences

Contributory negligence, volenti, inevitable accident, statutory authority (see Topic 3).

### 4.11 Nuisance: Definition and kinds

Winfield: nuisance is an **unlawful interference with a person's use or enjoyment of land, or some right over, or in connection with it.** Two kinds: **private nuisance** (a tort) and **public nuisance** (a crime as well, and also a tort where special damage is shown).

### 4.12 Nuisance: Private nuisance: elements

1.  **Unreasonable interference:** with the use or enjoyment of land (not liability for mere inconvenience; the test is the standard of ordinary reasonable persons).

2.  **Damage:** either (a) **physical injury to property** (e.g., damage to trees, buildings) or (b) **substantial interference with the comfort and enjoyment** of land (smell, noise, vibrations, smoke).

3.  **Nature of harm:** continuous or recurrent, not a one-time event (though a single event may count if arising from a state of affairs on the land).

Factors in reasonableness: locality (*St Helen's Smelting Co v Tipping*, 1865: for **material injury to property**, locality is irrelevant; for **personal discomfort**, locality matters: "what would be a nuisance in Belgrave Square would not necessarily be so in Bermondsey"), **duration and time**, **intensity**, **sensitiveness of the plaintiff** (*Robinson v Kilvert*: a person with abnormal sensitivity cannot complain; *McKinnon Industries v Walker*), **malice** (*Christie v Davey*; *Hollywood Silver Fox Farm v Emmett*), **utility** of the defendant's conduct (not a defence by itself).

**Who can sue:** only those with a **proprietary interest** in the land (owner, tenant, occupier): ***Hunter v Canary Wharf* (1997)**; mere residents without such an interest cannot. **Who can be sued:** the creator, the occupier, the landlord who authorised the nuisance, and in some cases the occupier who failed to abate a nuisance created by trespassers or natural causes (*Sedleigh-Denfield v O'Callaghan*; *Leakey v National Trust*, measured duty).

### 4.13 Nuisance: Public nuisance

An act or omission that **materially affects the reasonable comfort and convenience of a class of Her/His Majesty's subjects** (earlier IPC Section 268, now BNS Section 270). Examples: obstructing a highway, polluting a river, operating a brothel or a gambling house. A private person can sue only if he suffers **special damage** (damage different in kind, not just degree, from that suffered by the public: *Ricket v Metropolitan Railway*; *Attorney General v PYA Quarries*). In India: **Section 91 of the CPC** allows a suit for public nuisance by the Advocate General, or by two or more persons with the **leave of the court**, without special damage; **Section 133 CrPC** (now BNSS equivalent) allows a Magistrate to order removal of public nuisance.

### 4.14 Nuisance: Public vs private nuisance

- Private: affects an individual's land; only a tort; remedy by the person injured; no need for special damage when interference is substantial.

- Public: affects a class of the public; a crime and a tort; the prosecution is by the State; a private plaintiff must show special damage.

### 4.15 Nuisance: Defences and non-defences

- **Valid defences:** **prescription** (20 years' uninterrupted enjoyment as an easement: *Sturges v Bridgman*, the confectioner's noisy machinery against a doctor; the right begins when the nuisance becomes actionable; Indian Easements Act 1882, Section 15), **statutory authority** (Topic 3), **consent/acquiescence**, **act of a stranger** (limited), **Act of God**.

- **Not defences:** *coming to the nuisance* (*Sturges v Bridgman*), **public benefit** (*Adams v Ursell*, a fried-fish shop), **reasonable care**, that **the plaintiff's use was sensitive**, **others also contribute** (*Lambton v Mellish*).

### 4.16 Nuisance: Remedies and leading cases

**Remedies:** damages, injunction (the primary remedy), **abatement** (self-help, with notice generally required).

- *St Helen's Smelting v Tipping* (1865): physical vs comfort nuisance.

- *Sturges v Bridgman* (1879): locality and prescription.

- ***Cambridge Water Co v Eastern Counties Leather* (1994):** foreseeability of damage is a prerequisite for damages in nuisance.

- *Coventry v Lawrence* (2014): planning permission is not a defence per se.

- **Indian cases:** ***Municipal Council, Ratlam v Vardhichand* (1980)** (Justice Krishna Iyer; a municipality is obliged to provide drains; the public nuisance provisions are an instrument of social justice and a statutory duty can be enforced by a court); ***Ushaben v Bhagyalaxmi Chitra Mandir* (1978, Guj)** (the film *Jai Santoshi Maa*; hurting religious sentiments is not a nuisance in tort).

- **Nuisance vs trespass:** trespass is a direct and immediate interference with possession (actionable per se); nuisance is indirect interference with enjoyment (actionable on proof of damage). **Nuisance vs negligence:** nuisance focuses on the *result* (the unreasonable interference), negligence on the *conduct* (breach of duty).

### 4.17 Trespass: Trespass to the person

- **Assault:** an act creating in the plaintiff's mind a **reasonable apprehension** of immediate battery. Words alone are generally insufficient (but may qualify the act). *Stephens v Myers* (1830: a chairman advancing with a clenched fist; assault although stopped by others); ***Tuberville v Savage*** ("if it were not assize-time, I would not take such language from you": no assault, as the words negatived the intention); *Read v Coker*.

- **Battery:** the actual **application of force** (any physical contact without consent). The slightest touch can amount to battery. *Cole v Turner* (the least touching in anger is a battery); *Wilson v Pringle* (hostile touching; later criticised); *F v West Berkshire* and *Collins v Wilcock* (ordinary social contact is excepted).

- **False imprisonment:** the **total** restraint of a person's liberty without lawful justification (partial obstruction is not enough: *Bird v Jones*). It is not necessary to know of the confinement (*Meering v Grahame-White Aviation*), but knowledge is relevant for damages. *Herd v Weardale Steel* (a miner's refusal to be taken to the surface was not false imprisonment because there was no obligation to provide an exit). **Indian cases:** *Rudul Sah v State of Bihar* (14 years of unlawful detention after acquittal), *Bhim Singh v State of J&K*, *Sebastian Hongray*.

- **Malicious prosecution:** the essentials, as set out by the Privy Council in ***Mohammed Amin v Jogendra Kumar Bannerjee* (1947)**: (i) the defendant prosecuted the plaintiff; (ii) the proceedings **ended in the plaintiff's favour**; (iii) the defendant acted **without reasonable and probable cause**; (iv) the defendant acted with **malice**; (v) the plaintiff suffered damage.

- **Defences:** consent, self-defence, lawful authority, parental/quasi-parental authority, necessity.

### 4.18 Trespass: Trespass to land

Unjustifiable interference with the **possession** of land. Possession (not ownership) is the basis for the suit. **Forms:** entering the land, remaining after a licence ends, placing objects on it, **trespass ab initio** (*Six Carpenters' Case*: a lawful entry becomes a trespass from the start by a subsequent wrongful act). **Airspace and subsoil:** the owner's rights extend to such height and depth as are necessary for the ordinary use and enjoyment of the land (*Kelsen v Imperial Tobacco*: an overhanging advertisement board; *Bernstein v Skyviews*: aerial photography at a height was not trespass). **Leading cases:** ***Entick v Carrington* (1765)** (the officers' warrantless entry was unlawful; the rule that the State must act within the law), *Basely v Clarkson* (a mistaken mowing of a neighbour's land was a trespass). **Defences:** licence, justification by law, necessity, self-help (re-entry), and the plaintiff's lack of possession. **Remedies:** damages, injunction, ejectment, re-entry, **distress damage feasant**.

### 4.19 Trespass: Trespass to goods and conversion

- **Trespass to goods:** direct interference with chattels in the plaintiff's possession.

- **Conversion:** a dealing with goods in a manner inconsistent with the owner's right, which deprives him of possession (*Fouldes v Willoughby*: a ferry operator's removal of horses; a mere moving is not conversion unless exercising dominion; *Hollins v Fowler*).

- **Detinue:** the wrongful detention of goods after a demand for return. (In England, detinue was abolished by the Torts (Interference with Goods) Act 1977; in India the action of detinue continues under general law and the Specific Relief Act.)

- **Remedies:** damages, recovery of the specific chattel (Specific Relief Act, 1963, Sections 7–8), recaption.

### 4.20 Defamation: Definition and nature

Defamation is the **publication of a false statement** which tends to injure the reputation of a person by exposing him to hatred, contempt or ridicule, or which tends to lower him in the estimation of right-thinking members of society generally, or to make them shun or avoid him (*Sim v Stretch*). It protects **reputation** (not feelings). The tort is in Indian law largely governed by English common law principles, while **criminal defamation** was in IPC Sections 499–500, now **BNS Section 356**.

### 4.21 Defamation: Libel and slander

- **Libel:** a defamatory statement in a **permanent form** (writing, print, pictures, effigy; in some systems broadcasts). Actionable **per se**, and may also be a crime.

- **Slander:** in a **transient form** (spoken words, gestures). Generally actionable only on proof of **special damage**, except in four **exceptions** (slander **per se**): (i) imputation of a **criminal offence** punishable with imprisonment, (ii) imputation of a **loathsome disease**, (iii) imputation of **unchastity to a woman** (Slander of Women Act 1891), and (iv) imputation of unfitness in **office, profession, calling or trade**.

- **Indian position:** the distinction is not strictly followed; a majority of Indian decisions treat both libel and slander as actionable **without proof of special damage**, though textbooks sometimes record the English rule. Exam answer: *in India, the distinction is not recognised strictly and slander is also actionable per se (majority view); under English law, the distinction exists.*

### 4.22 Defamation: Essential elements

1.  **The statement must be defamatory** (judged by the right-thinking person test; innuendo: *Tolley v Fry*: a golfer's picture in an advertisement implying that he was paid).

2.  **It must refer to the plaintiff** (*Hulton v Jones*: "Artemus Jones"; the test is whether reasonable persons would think that it refers to the plaintiff, so intention is irrelevant; *Newstead v London Express*; *Knupffer v London Express*: no reference to an identifiable individual in a large class). A company can sue (*South Hetton Coal v North-Eastern News*), but a government body cannot.

3.  **It must be published**, i.e., communicated to at least **one person other than the plaintiff** (*Pullman v Hill*: dictation to a stenographer; communication to the plaintiff alone is not publication; *Mahendra Ram v Harnandan Prasad*: a letter in Urdu sent to a person who could not read it; no publication).

4.  **Malice** is not an element in civil defamation (it matters for aggravated damages and defeating defences). The defendant's lack of intention does not excuse (*Hulton v Jones*).

### 4.23 Defamation: Defences

1.  **Justification (truth):** a complete defence in civil law if the statement is substantially true (burden on the defendant). In **criminal** law, truth must also be **for the public good** (BNS exceptions).

2.  **Fair comment:** on a matter of public interest, an **honest opinion** based on true facts, without malice (*Merivale v Carson*; *Kemsley v Foot*; the Indian provision in the exceptions to BNS Section 356).

3.  **Privilege:**

    - **Absolute:** statements in Parliament, judicial proceedings, State communications, communications between spouses (in some systems), no liability regardless of malice.

    - **Qualified:** a duty or interest to communicate (*Adam v Ward*), fair and accurate reports of proceedings, protection of one's own interests; **defeated by malice**. **Reynolds v Times Newspapers** (1999) developed a public-interest defence for responsible journalism.

4.  **Apology** and offer of amends: mitigating (in England under Defamation Act 1952/1996; in India, under general law, it mitigates damages).

5.  **Consent**; **innocent dissemination** (distributors who exercise reasonable care).

### 4.24 Defamation: Defamation and the Constitution (recent trend)

- ***Subramanian Swamy v Union of India* (2016):** the Supreme Court upheld the constitutional validity of **criminal defamation** (IPC Sections 499–500) as a reasonable restriction on freedom of speech under **Article 19(2)** and protecting the right to reputation as part of **Article 21**. This is a very frequently tested case.

- ***R. Rajagopal v State of Tamil Nadu* (1994):** the right to privacy; the press may publish material from public records, but not private details without consent; public officials have limited protection against defamation claims unless they prove that the publication was made with reckless disregard of the truth.

- Under UK law, the **Defamation Act 2013** introduced a **serious harm** threshold (not part of Indian law).

### 4.25 Defamation: Remedies and limitation

Damages (including aggravated and in rare cases exemplary), injunction (quia timet to prevent publication, granted cautiously), and an apology. Limitation: **one year** for a suit for libel or slander under the Limitation Act 1963.

## Chapter 5: Remoteness of Damages

### 5.1 The problem

Even if the defendant's breach caused the damage in fact (causation), the law refuses to hold him liable for every consequence, however unlikely. **Remoteness of damage** is the legal limit on the consequences for which a defendant is liable. Two stages: **(1) causation in fact** (but-for test) and **(2) legal causation / remoteness** (which of the consequences are recoverable).

### 5.2 Test 1: Directness test – *Re Polemis* (1921)

**Facts:** A stevedore's servant dropped a plank into the hold of a ship, which carried petrol vapour. A spark caused a fire that destroyed the ship. **Held:** the defendant was liable for **all direct consequences** of a negligent act, whether foreseeable or not. Some harm (damage by falling plank) was foreseeable, and the fire was a direct result. **Rule:** foreseeability of the *kind* of damage is irrelevant if the damage is a direct result of the negligent act.

### 5.3 Test 2: Reasonable foreseeability – *Wagon Mound (No. 1)* (1961)

**Facts:** Oil negligently spilled from the ship *Wagon Mound* in Sydney harbour drifted to the plaintiff's wharf, where welding was carried on. Cotton waste floating on the oil caught fire from the molten metal, burning the wharf. The experts believed that oil on water would not ignite. **Held (Privy Council, Viscount Simonds):** *Re Polemis* was **overruled**; the defendant is liable only for damage of a kind that is **reasonably foreseeable**. Fire was not foreseeable; no liability in negligence. **Rule:** reasonable foresight of the **type of damage** is the test of remoteness in negligence (although a person who is negligent is liable for all foreseeable consequences, even if the extent of damage is greater than expected).

### 5.4 5.3A *Wagon Mound (No. 2)* (1967)

In this separate action by the owners of ships damaged by the same fire, the court found that the risk of fire, though small, was **real** and a reasonable person in the position of the ship's engineer would not have disregarded it; liability was established in **nuisance** and negligence. **Rule:** a risk that is foreseeable and not far-fetched, and could be eliminated at no cost, must be guarded against.

### 5.4 Refinements and the "thin skull" rule

- ***Hughes v Lord Advocate* (1963):** boys playing with an unattended paraffin lamp left near a manhole; the lamp exploded in an unforeseen manner and burned the child. **Held:** liable, since the **kind of damage (burns)** was foreseeable, though the exact **manner** of occurrence was not.

- ***Doughty v Turner Manufacturing* (1964):** an asbestos cover slipped into a cauldron, causing an eruption through a chemical reaction. The event was **different in kind** from the foreseeable risk (splash); defendant not liable.

- **Egg-shell skull / thin skull rule:** the defendant must **take the plaintiff as he finds him**. ***Smith v Leech Brain & Co* (1962):** a burn on the lip of a worker, who had a pre-malignant condition, caused cancer and death; the employer was liable since the burn was foreseeable. *Vosburg v Putney* (US). The rule applies to **physical vulnerability** and, in some cases, to **psychological** vulnerability.

- ***Bradford v Robinson Rentals* (1967):** foreseeable harm of cold (frost-bite) was recoverable even if its severity was not foreseen.

- **Extent of harm:** no need to foresee the exact extent (*Vacwell Engineering v BDH Chemicals*: explosion of chemicals, the damage being of a different kind; held that damage was not foreseeable).

### 5.5 Novus actus interveniens (intervening cause)

A new, independent act or event that **breaks the chain of causation**, so that the defendant is not responsible for the later damage.

- **Act of a third party:** the chain is broken if the act is **unforeseeable** and independent (*Knightley v Johns*: the police inspector's negligent order; held the chain was broken). *Scott v Shepherd* (the squib case): a squib thrown into a market and tossed on by others; the first thrower was liable since the subsequent acts were reflex actions.

- **Act of the plaintiff:** if the plaintiff's own act is unreasonable (*McKew v Holland*: a plaintiff with a weak leg went down steep steps and was injured; the chain was broken). A **reasonable** act in a dilemma, caused by the defendant's wrong, does not break the chain (*Haynes v Harwood*, rescue cases).

- **Natural events:** *Carslogie Steamship v Royal Norwegian Government* (a later storm damage).

- ***The Oropesa* (1943):** a captain's decision to launch a lifeboat in a storm, in which the sailor drowned, did not break the chain (a reasonable decision in an emergency).

### 5.6 Causation in fact: the tests

- **"But for" test:** *Barnett v Chelsea & Kensington Hospital Management Committee* (1969).

- **Material contribution to the damage:** *Bonnington Castings v Wardlaw* (1956: silica dust).

- **Material increase of risk:** *McGhee v National Coal Board*; ***Fairchild v Glenhaven Funeral Services* (2002)** (mesothelioma: where multiple employers exposed the claimant to asbestos and the specific source could not be identified, each employer who materially increased the risk was liable, an exception to the but-for test). *Wilsher v Essex AHA* (1988) confined the exception.

- **Loss of a chance:** *Hotson v East Berkshire AHA* (not recoverable in general).

### 5.7 Remoteness in other branches

- **Deceit/fraud:** the defendant is liable for all **direct** consequences (*Doyle v Olby*), as an exception to foreseeability.

- **Contract:** *Hadley v Baxendale*, the rule on damages (Section 73, Indian Contract Act: damage which naturally arose in the usual course of things, or which the parties knew likely to result). Contrast between the tests in contract and tort is often asked.

- **Strict liability:** *Cambridge Water* and *Transco* require foreseeability of the **type of damage** also in nuisance and *Rylands v Fletcher*.

### 5.8 Indian position

Indian courts, in the absence of a unique doctrine, **follow English principles**, generally the *Wagon Mound* foreseeability test in negligence claims, and the **direct consequence** concept where the wrong is intentional. In claims involving **public-law compensation** or **statutory no-fault** compensation, remoteness is less relevant.

## Chapter 6: Strict and Absolute Liability

### 6.1 Concept

As a general rule, liability in tort depends on **fault** (intention or negligence). **Strict liability** is liability for damage even **without fault**, subject to certain exceptions. **Absolute liability** (an Indian innovation) is liability **without any exception**.

### 6.2 *Rylands v Fletcher* (1868)

**Facts:** The defendants employed independent contractors to build a reservoir on their land. The contractors discovered old, unused mine shafts under the site that connected to the plaintiff's coal mine, but did not seal them. When the reservoir filled, water burst through the shafts and flooded the plaintiff's mine. The contractors were negligent but the defendants were not personally at fault.

**Held:** The Court of Exchequer Chamber (**Blackburn J**) laid down the rule: *the person who for his own purposes brings on his lands and collects and keeps there anything likely to do mischief if it escapes, must keep it at his peril, and, if he does not do so, is prima facie answerable for all the damage which is the natural consequence of its escape.* The **House of Lords** (**Lord Cairns**) affirmed with an added qualification that the use of land must be **non-natural**.

### 6.3 Essential ingredients

1.  **Dangerous thing:** something likely to do mischief if it escapes (water, gas, electricity, fire, explosives, noxious fumes, chemicals, rusty wire, vibrations, etc.). (*Rylands*: water; *Miles v Forest Granite* (explosives); *Hale v Jennings* (a chair-o-plane in a fair; *Attorney-General v Corke* (caravan dwellers)); *Crowhurst v Amersham Burial Board* (yew tree poisonous to cattle).)

2.  **Escape:** from a place where the defendant has occupation or control to a place outside his control. ***Read v Lyons* (1947):** the plaintiff, an inspector at a munitions factory, was injured by a shell explosion within the premises; **no escape**, no strict liability.

3.  **Non-natural use of land:** a **special use bringing with it increased danger to others**, not the ordinary use of land or such use as is proper for the general benefit of the community (*Rickards v Lothian*, a water tap in the upper floor overflowed by a malicious act; ordinary domestic use). Industrial storage of chemicals (*Cambridge Water*) can be non-natural; a water supply in a building is natural; *Transco v Stockport MBC* (2003) held that a pipe supplying water to a block of flats was an ordinary use.

4.  **Damage:** to the plaintiff's property (damage to land and chattels). The House of Lords in ***Transco*** doubted claims for personal injuries; the usual rule is that the rule concerns property damage (Indian cases have accepted personal injuries).

### 6.4 Exceptions to the rule

1.  **Plaintiff's own default** (*Ponting v Noakes*: a horse that reached over the boundary and ate yew leaves).

2.  **Act of God** (*Nichols v Marsland*).

3.  **Act of a stranger** (an independent third party beyond the defendant's control: *Box v Jubb*, the overflow caused by a stranger; *Rickards v Lothian*).

4.  **Consent of the plaintiff / common benefit** (*Carstairs v Taylor*: water leaked from the upstairs rain-water box of a building shared by tenants).

5.  **Statutory authority** (*Green v Chelsea Waterworks*: a main pipe laid under statutory duty burst).

6.  (Some authors also treat **natural user** as part of the definition and not as an exception.)

### 6.5 Developments in England

- *Cambridge Water Co v Eastern Counties Leather* (1994): **foreseeability** of the kind of damage is necessary; the rule is an extension of nuisance.

- *Transco v Stockport MBC* (2003): the rule has not been abolished but is confined; the user must be extraordinary and unusual; personal injury not recoverable; a majority of the House left the matter of absorption into negligence undecided.

### 6.6 Indian development: from *Rylands* to absolute liability

- **Application of *Rylands* in India:** Indian courts applied the strict liability rule in many cases but found it inadequate for modern industrial hazards, because (i) the exceptions (Act of God, act of stranger) could allow powerful enterprises to avoid liability, and (ii) it covers only property damage and "escape".

- ***M.C. Mehta v Union of India* (1987) (Oleum Gas Leak case, Shriram Foods and Fertilizers):** after a leak of oleum gas from a Delhi factory caused the death of one person and injuries to many, a **Constitution Bench** (**Bhagwati CJ**) held that an enterprise engaged in a **hazardous or inherently dangerous industry** which poses a potential threat to the health and safety of persons working in the factory and of those living around is **strictly and absolutely liable** to compensate for harm. The Court said it was **not bound by the *Rylands* exceptions**, because the law must evolve to meet changing social conditions (Article 32 powers). **Key features of the rule of absolute liability:**

  - applies to hazardous or inherently dangerous activities;

  - **no exceptions** (Act of God, stranger's act, plaintiff's default, consent are not available);

  - **compensation** must be **correlated to the magnitude and capacity** of the enterprise (the "deep pocket" principle), so that it has a deterrent effect;

  - the enterprise has a **non-delegable duty** to the community to ensure that no harm results, and must **conduct the activity with the highest standards of safety**;

  - no need to show **escape** from the premises: harm within or outside the premises is covered.

- ***Union Carbide Corporation v Union of India* (Bhopal Gas Leak Disaster, 1989):** the Supreme Court approved the settlement of **US \$470 million**; ***Charan Lal Sahu v UOI* (1990)** upheld the **Bhopal Gas Leak Disaster (Processing of Claims) Act 1985** (the State as *parens patriae*); the 1991 review reaffirmed the settlement while directing that the insurance for victims be provided and that the criminal proceedings be revived.

- ***Indian Council for Enviro-Legal Action v Union of India* (1996)** (Bichhri, Rajasthan: the sludge from chemical factories): applied absolute liability and the **polluter pays principle**; the polluters were required to pay for remedial costs.

- ***Vellore Citizens' Welfare Forum v Union of India* (1996):** (tannery pollution): adopted the **precautionary principle**, the **polluter pays principle** and **sustainable development** as part of Indian environmental law, based on Articles 21, 47, 48A and 51A(g).

- ***Bhopal-linked* statutory regimes:** the **Public Liability Insurance Act 1991** (compulsory insurance for owners handling hazardous substances; no-fault liability to pay relief for death, injury and property damage; Environmental Relief Fund); the **National Green Tribunal Act 2010** (Section 17: the Tribunal can award relief and compensation on a **no-fault** basis for damage from hazardous substances; also *polluter pays* in NGT decisions such as the Ganga cases); the **Environment (Protection) Act 1986**.

- **Other illustrations:** *M.P. Electricity Board v Shail Kumari* (2002): strict liability for the death by electrocution from a live wire fallen on the road; *Kaushnuma Begum v New India Assurance* (2001): strict liability principles in motor accidents; **Section 140, MVA** (no-fault liability).

### 6.7 Strict vs absolute liability

- *Rylands v Fletcher*: **non-natural use**, **escape** required, **exceptions** available, **property damage** primarily, not necessarily hazardous industry.

- *M.C. Mehta*: **hazardous industry**, **no escape required** (also applies within premises), **no exceptions**, **compensation as per capacity**, developed for India's conditions through Article 21 and 32.

### 6.8 Other forms of strict or no-fault liability (for completeness)

- **Animals:** the *scienter* rule (liability for harm by a dangerous animal (*ferae naturae*) or by an animal known to be dangerous); the **Cattle Trespass Act 1871** (in India); in England, the Animals Act 1971.

- **Employees' Compensation Act 1923** (injuries during employment, no-fault).

- **Product liability** under the Consumer Protection Act 2019 (Chapter VI).

- **Vicarious liability** (Topic 2).

- **Fire:** liability for the spread of fire arising from negligence (the Fires Prevention (Metropolis) Act 1774 protects against accidental fires).

## Chapter 7: Tortious Liability of the State

### 7.1 Background: the English rule and the Indian Constitution

In old English law, **"the King can do no wrong"** and the Crown could not be sued in tort without its consent, although the **servants** were personally liable. In England the **Crown Proceedings Act 1947** made the Crown liable like a private person. In India, the position stems from **Article 300(1) of the Constitution**: the Government of India or a State Government may sue or be sued in relation to its affairs in the like cases as the Dominion of India and the corresponding Provinces might have done if the Constitution had not been enacted, subject to any law enacted by Parliament or State legislatures. The earlier provisions: **Section 65 of the Government of India Act 1858**, **Section 32 of the GIA 1915**, **Section 176 of the GIA 1935**.

### 7.2 Sovereign vs non-sovereign functions (the pre-constitution cases)

- ***Peninsular & Oriental Steam Navigation Co v Secretary of State for India* (1861, Calcutta HC):** the leading early case. A servant of the Government negligently drove a carriage from a government workshop, injuring the plaintiff's horse. **Held:** the Government was liable, because the act was in the course of a **non-sovereign function** (an undertaking that private persons might carry on); the immunity would apply only to **acts done in the exercise of sovereign powers** (e.g., declaring war, concluding treaties).

- ***Secretary of State v Hari Bhanji* (1882, Madras HC):** followed the P&O case; a distinction between sovereign and non-sovereign functions (dealing with the customs).

- ***Nobin Chunder Dey v Secretary of State* (1875):** the licensing of ganja trading was a sovereign act; no liability.

### 7.3 After the Constitution: the Supreme Court cases

- ***State of Rajasthan v Vidhyawati* (1962):** a government driver negligently drove the jeep carrying a government officer, killing a pedestrian. **Held:** the State was **vicariously liable**, as the State is in the same position as any other employer; the maintenance of the vehicle was not an exercise of sovereign power. The Court rejected the idea that the immunity of the Crown in England applied in India, noting that the Constitution creates a democratic republic, not an absolute monarchy.

- ***Kasturi Lal Ralia Ram Jain v State of Uttar Pradesh* (1965):** the police seized gold from the plaintiff on suspicion of theft; the plaintiff was released, but the gold was misappropriated by the head constable, who fled to Pakistan. **Held:** the State was **not liable** because the act (seizure of property) was done in the exercise of **statutory powers of the police**, which were **sovereign powers**; the P&O principle was applied. The Court expressed regret for the injustice and suggested that the legislature should intervene. This case has been **extensively criticised** and its scope has been narrowed.

- ***Saheli, a Women's Resources Centre v Commissioner of Police, Delhi* (1990):** death of a child due to police beating; the State was held liable to pay compensation (**₹ 75,000**); the *Kasturi Lal* principle was distinguished as the case involved violation of the right to life.

- ***N. Nagendra Rao & Co v State of Andhra Pradesh* (1994):** the Government seized fertiliser stock under the Essential Commodities Act, which deteriorated through the negligence of the officers. **Held:** the State was liable; *Kasturi Lal* was confined to the situation where the act is truly sovereign (exercise of powers of **inalienable sovereign functions**); the **statutory function** does not mean sovereign function; the old distinction is **unsustainable** in a welfare State.

- ***Common Cause v Union of India* (1999):** the Court reaffirmed the principle that the State must be held accountable.

- ***State of Maharashtra v Kanchanmala Vijaysing Shirke* (1995)**; ***M.S. Grewal v Deep Chand Sood* (2001)** (the drowning of school students on an excursion; the school and the State were held liable for the negligence of the supervisors).

### 7.4 Constitutional torts and public-law compensation (Article 21)

The Supreme Court has evolved a remedy of **compensation in public law** for violation of the fundamental rights, enforceable under **Articles 32 and 226**. This is **distinct from private-law tort** because it is **not dependent on sovereign/non-sovereign distinction** and does not need a trial of facts in a civil suit.

- ***Rudul Sah v State of Bihar* (1983):** the petitioner was kept in jail for **14 years** after acquittal; the Supreme Court awarded **₹ 30,000** as interim compensation under Article 32, the first case of monetary relief in a habeas corpus petition.

- ***Bhim Singh v State of Jammu & Kashmir* (1985):** the illegal detention of an MLA to prevent him from attending the Assembly; compensation of **₹ 50,000**.

- ***Sebastian M. Hongray v Union of India* (1984):** the disappearance of two persons from Army custody (Manipur); exemplary costs of **₹ 1 lakh** to each of the two wives.

- ***Nilabati Behera v State of Orissa* (1993):** the custodial death of a young man; the Court awarded **₹ 1.5 lakh** and declared that the defence of sovereign immunity **is not available** in a claim under public law for the violation of fundamental rights; the compensation is a **remedy under public law** and may be adjusted against any amount awarded in a civil suit.

- ***D.K. Basu v State of West Bengal* (1997):** guidelines for arrest and detention (identification of police personnel, memo of arrest, medical examination, informing a relative, a register, etc.); custodial violence violates Articles 21 and 22.

- ***Chairman, Railway Board v Chandrima Das* (2000):** the rape of a Bangladeshi national in the Yatri Niwas by railway employees; the Union of India was liable to pay **₹ 10 lakh**; the Court held that fundamental rights under Article 21 are available to **non-citizens** also.

- ***People's Union for Democratic Rights v Union of India*** and other cases on police encounters and custodial deaths also apply this principle.

### 7.5 Procedural aspects

- **Section 79, CPC:** suits by or against the Government are filed in the name of the Union of India or the State.

- **Section 80, CPC:** two months' **prior notice** is needed before the suit (the court may grant leave in urgent cases).

- **Section 82, CPC:** execution of decrees against the Government; **Section 86, CPC:** suits against foreign States and ambassadors require the consent of the Central Government.

- **Article 361:** personal immunity of the President and Governors for official acts, although the Government can be sued.

### 7.6 Law reform

The **First Law Commission (1956)** (M.C. Setalvad) recommended abolishing the distinction between sovereign and non-sovereign functions and making the State liable like a private employer. The **Government Liability Bill 1965** (and later proposals) lapsed. The Supreme Court has therefore largely developed the position through case law.

### 7.7 Current position (summary for answers)

1.  The State is **vicariously liable** for torts of its servants in non-sovereign functions (*Vidhyawati*).

2.  For **sovereign functions** (defence, administration of justice, legislative acts, acts of State), immunity may still apply in a narrow sense (*Kasturi Lal*), but the sovereign category is **now very restricted**.

3.  In **public law**, the State is liable to pay **compensation** for the violation of fundamental rights, irrespective of the sovereign immunity (*Rudul Sah*, *Nilabati Behera*).

4.  **Public servants** are personally liable for their wrongful acts, subject to statutory protections requiring sanction/notice (Section 197 CrPC, now BNSS Section 218; Section 80 CPC).

Basu*; *Chandrima Das*; Section 80 CPC; Assertion–Reason on public law compensation vs private law damages.

## Chapter 8: Consumer Protection Act

### 8.1 Critical note on the syllabus

The syllabus names the **Consumer Protection Act 1986 (CPA 1986)**. That Act was **repealed and replaced by the Consumer Protection Act 2019 (CPA 2019)**, which came into force on **20 July 2020** (Consumer Protection Rules 2020 followed on 24 July 2020). Many MCQs now test the **2019 Act** directly or contrast the two. Learn both: first the 1986 framework, then the 2019 upgrades.

### 8.2 Background and objectives

The CPA 1986 was enacted for the better protection of the interests of consumers and the establishment of consumer councils and authorities for the settlement of consumer disputes. The remedy is **in addition to**, and not in derogation of, other remedies (**Section 3 of the 1986 Act; Section 100 of the 2019 Act**). It is **social-welfare legislation** and is interpreted liberally.

### 8.3 Key definitions under the CPA 1986 (Section 2)

- **Consumer** (2(1)(d)): (i) a person who **buys goods** for a consideration (paid, promised, partly paid or under a deferred payment system), including a user of the goods with the approval of the buyer, but **not** a person who obtains goods for **resale or any commercial purpose**; (ii) a person who **hires or avails of any service** for a consideration, including a beneficiary with the approval of the hirer, excluding services availed for a commercial purpose. **Free services** and services under a **contract of personal service** are excluded.

- **Complainant** (2(1)(b)): a consumer; a registered voluntary consumer association; the Central/State Government; one or more consumers having the same interest; or the legal heir/representative of a deceased consumer.

- **Complaint** (2(1)(c)): a written allegation of an unfair or restrictive trade practice, defective goods, deficiency in service, excess price charged, or hazardous goods offered for sale.

- **Defect** (2(1)(f)): any fault, imperfection or shortcoming in the quality, quantity, potency, purity or standard required to be maintained by law or claimed by the trader.

- **Deficiency** (2(1)(g)): any fault, imperfection, shortcoming or inadequacy in the quality, nature and manner of performance required by law or undertaken by contract in relation to a service.

- **Service** (2(1)(o)): service of any description made available to potential users, including banking, financing, insurance, transport, processing, supply of electrical or other energy, telecom, boarding or lodging, entertainment, amusement, or purveying of news or other information; **not** free services or contracts of personal service.

- **Unfair trade practice** (2(1)(r)): false representation, misleading advertisement, false claims about standard or quality, offering gifts or prizes not intended to be given, non-compliance with prescribed standards, hoarding, etc.

- **Restrictive trade practice** (2(1)(nnn)): a practice that tends to manipulate price or conditions of delivery or to affect the flow of supplies in an unjust manner (e.g., tie-in sale).

### 8.4 Consumer rights (Section 6, CPA 1986; Section 2(9), CPA 2019)

Drawn from President Kennedy's 1962 Consumer Bill of Rights and the UN Guidelines for Consumer Protection (1985):

1.  **Right to safety:** protection against marketing of goods and services hazardous to life and property.

2.  **Right to be informed:** about quality, quantity, potency, purity, standard and price.

3.  **Right to choose:** access to a variety of goods and services at competitive prices.

4.  **Right to be heard:** consumer interests receive due consideration at appropriate fora.

5.  **Right to seek redressal:** against unfair or restrictive trade practices or unscrupulous exploitation.

6.  **Right to consumer education.**

The 2019 Act defines "consumer rights" in Section 2(9) in similar terms (protection from hazardous goods and services, information, assured access at competitive prices, to be heard, to seek redressal, and consumer awareness).

### 8.5 Redressal machinery under the CPA 1986

**Three-tier quasi-judicial system:**

- **District Forum:** headed by a person who is, or has been, or is qualified to be a District Judge, with two other members (one a woman). Pecuniary jurisdiction up to **₹ 20 lakh** (after the 2002 amendment).

- **State Commission:** President is or has been a High Court Judge. Original jurisdiction above ₹ 20 lakh and up to **₹ 1 crore**; appeals from District Forums.

- **National Commission:** President is or has been a Supreme Court Judge. Original jurisdiction above **₹ 1 crore**; appellate and revisional jurisdiction over State Commissions.

- **Appeal to the Supreme Court** from the National Commission (Section 27A) within 30 days.

- **Limitation:** 2 years from the cause of action (Section 24A).

- **Consumer Protection Councils** (Central, State, District): advisory bodies promoting consumer rights.

- **Reliefs (Section 14):** removal of defects, replacement, refund of price, compensation for loss or injury, removal of deficiency, discontinuance of unfair trade practice, withdrawal of hazardous goods, punitive damages, costs.

- **Procedure:** summary; not bound by CPC technicalities, but natural justice applies.

### 8.6 Consumer Protection Act 2019: key changes (highest-yield part)

1.  **Wider "consumer" (Section 2(7)):** covers buyers of goods or services through **any mode**, offline or online, including **electronic means, teleshopping, direct selling and multi-level marketing**. Free goods/services and purchases for **resale or commercial purpose** are excluded; "commercial purpose" does not include goods bought and used exclusively for earning a livelihood by **self-employment**.

2.  **Central Consumer Protection Authority (CCPA)** (Sections 10–22): a regulator to promote, protect and enforce **consumer rights as a class**. Powers: inquiries and investigations (through an **Investigation Wing headed by a Director General**), search and seizure, **recall of goods**, **refunds**, discontinuance of unfair practices, safety notices, **class action**, and penalties for **false or misleading advertisements** (up to **₹ 10 lakh** on a manufacturer or endorser, with a ban on the endorser of up to **one year**; for subsequent contraventions up to **₹ 50 lakh** and a ban of up to **three years**). Endorsers have a **due diligence** defence (Section 21). The CCPA does **not** adjudicate individual disputes.

3.  **Product liability (Chapter VI, Sections 82–87):** the **product manufacturer**, **product service provider** and **product seller** are liable for harm from defective products or deficient services. A manufacturer is liable for a manufacturing defect, design defect, deviation from manufacturing specifications, non-conformity with an express warranty, or failure to give adequate instructions or warnings. The seller's liability is in Section 86; **Section 87** lists exceptions (e.g., misuse, alteration, claimant's knowledge of danger).

4.  **Unfair contract (Section 2(46)):** terms causing a **significant change in the consumer's rights** (excessive security deposit, disproportionate penalty, unilateral termination, etc.). The **State and National Commissions** can declare such terms **null and void** (Sections 49(2) and 59(2)).

5.  **E-commerce and direct selling:** e-commerce is defined (Section 2(16)); the **Consumer Protection (E-Commerce) Rules 2020** and **Direct Selling Rules 2021** impose disclosure and grievance-redressal duties.

6.  **Mediation (Chapter V, Sections 74–81):** **consumer mediation cells** attached to the Commissions; a mediated settlement is final and no appeal lies (Section 80).

7.  **Easier filing:** complaints can be filed **electronically** (e-Daakhil), hearings by **video-conferencing**; the complaint may be filed where the complainant **resides or personally works for gain** (Section 34(2)(d)).

8.  **Pecuniary jurisdiction is based on the value of the consideration paid** (not the value of goods/services plus compensation claimed). The original 2019 Act provided District up to ₹ 1 crore and State up to ₹ 10 crore. Under the **2021 Jurisdiction Rules** (from 30 December 2021): **District Commission** up to **₹ 50 lakh**; **State Commission** above ₹ 50 lakh up to **₹ 2 crore**; **National Commission** above **₹ 2 crore** (*verify current limits*).

9.  **Admissibility:** the District Commission must decide admissibility within **21 days**; otherwise the complaint is deemed admitted.

10. **Appeals:** District → State within **45 days** (Section 41); State → National within **30 days** (Section 51); National → Supreme Court within **30 days** (Section 67). An appellant against a money award must **deposit 50 percent** of the amount.

11. **Limitation (Section 69):** **two years**, with power to condone delay for sufficient cause.

12. **Composition:** District Commission: President and at least two members; State Commission: President and at least four members; National Commission: President and at least four members.

13. **Penalties (Chapter VII):** non-compliance with Commission orders (Section 72: imprisonment of one month up to three years, or fine of ₹ 25,000 to ₹ 1 lakh, or both); **misleading advertisements** (imprisonment up to 2 years and fine up to ₹ 10 lakh; repeat offence up to 5 years and ₹ 50 lakh); **adulterated goods** (Section 90: up to 1 year and ₹ 1 lakh if no injury; up to 7 years and ₹ 3 lakh for grievous hurt; for death, not less than 7 years up to life and fine not less than ₹ 10 lakh); **spurious goods** (Section 91: similar graded punishments).

### 8.7 Recent regulatory developments (trend)

- **Guidelines for Prevention and Regulation of Misleading Advertisements, 2022** (CCPA).

- **Guidelines for Prevention and Regulation of Dark Patterns, 2023** (30 November 2023): 13 specified dark patterns such as false urgency, basket sneaking, confirm shaming, forced action, subscription trap, interface interference, bait and switch, drip pricing, disguised advertisement, nagging, trick question, SaaS billing and rogue malware.

- CCPA guidelines on **misleading advertisements in the coaching sector** and on **greenwashing** (2024) (*verify exact titles and dates*).

- **National Consumer Helpline** and the e-Daakhil portal.

### 8.8 Leading cases

- ***Lucknow Development Authority v M.K. Gupta* (1994):** statutory development authorities are covered; housing construction is "service"; compensation can be awarded for harassment.

- ***Indian Medical Association v V.P. Shantha* (1995):** doctors and hospitals charging fees are covered; free service to all patients in government hospitals and contracts of personal service are excluded; where some patients pay and others do not, all are covered.

- ***Laxmi Engineering Works v P.S.G. Industrial Institute* (1995):** meaning of **commercial purpose**; dominant-intention test; machinery bought for **self-employment** is not commercial.

- ***Spring Meadows Hospital v Harjol Ahluwalia* (1998):** parents can be consumers for a child's treatment.

- ***Consumer Education and Research Centre v Union of India* (1995):** right to health of workers is part of Article 21.

- ***State of Karnataka v Vishwabharathi House Building Co-op Society* (2003):** constitutional validity of the Act upheld.

- ***Bangalore Development Authority v Syndicate Bank* (2007):** the Act and allotment of sites.

- ***Ireo Grace Realtech v Abhishek Khanna* (2021)** and ***Pioneer Urban Land v Govindan Raghavan* (2019):** one-sided builder–buyer clauses are unfair; buyers may claim refund with interest for inordinate delay.

- ***Bar of Indian Lawyers v D.K. Gandhi PS National Institute of Communicable Diseases* (2024):** the Supreme Court held that advocates' services are not "service" under the CPA; the profession is *sui generis*. The decision was later subject to further consideration, so *verify the present status* before the exam.

Expect: **who is a consumer** (including exclusions); **the six consumer rights**; the **three-tier hierarchy and appeal periods**; **CCPA's powers** and the advertising penalty; **pecuniary jurisdiction** (basis: consideration paid; revised limits); **product liability** categories; **mediation**; **unfair contract**; **e-commerce**; **limitation of 2 years**; *IMA v V.P. Shantha*; *LDA v M.K. Gupta*; *Laxmi Engineering*; Assertion–Reason on whether free services or commercial purposes are covered; "which is NOT a power of the CCPA."

## Chapter 9: Motor Vehicles Act, 1988

### 9.1 Overview

The **Motor Vehicles Act 1988 (MVA)** consolidates the law on motor vehicles: licensing, registration, traffic control, **no-fault compensation** (Chapter X), **compulsory third-party insurance** (Chapter XI) and **Motor Accident Claims Tribunals (MACT)** (Chapter XII). It was substantially amended by the **Motor Vehicles (Amendment) Act 2019** (many provisions in force from **1 September 2019**). The policy is that road-accident victims must receive **speedy and certain compensation** without the ordeal of proving negligence.

*Section numbers below follow the commonly cited text; because the 2019 amendment rearranged some provisions, verify section numbering against the current bare Act where an exam question turns on a number.*

### 9.2 No-fault liability

**Principle:** compensation is payable on death or permanent disablement **irrespective of any wrongful act, neglect or default** of the owner or any other person. It is an exception to the fault principle, similar in spirit to **strict liability**.

- **Section 140 (as it stood after the 1994 amendment):** the owner is liable to pay **₹ 50,000** for death and **₹ 25,000** for permanent disablement; the claimant need not plead or prove wrongful act or negligence. (The original 1988 figures were ₹ 25,000 and ₹ 12,500.)

- **Section 141:** the right to claim under other laws is unaffected, but no-fault compensation paid is **adjusted** against the final award.

- **Section 142:** meaning of **permanent disablement**; **Section 143:** application of the Chapter to claims under the **Workmen's Compensation Act** (now Employees' Compensation Act); **Section 144:** **overriding effect**.

- **2019 amendment (Section 164):** no-fault compensation of **₹ 5 lakh** for **death** and **₹ 2.5 lakh** for **grievous hurt** (*verify the current section arrangement*).

- **Section 163A (structured formula):** compensation in death or permanent disablement cases according to the **Second Schedule** (based on age and income), where the claimant **need not prove negligence**; applicable to persons with annual income up to ₹ 40,000. ***Deepal Girishbhai Soni v United India Insurance* (2004):** remedies under Section 163A and Section 166 are **mutually exclusive**; the claimant must choose. ***Ningamma v United India Insurance* (2009):** the owner/borrower who is driving is not a **third party** for Section 163A.

- **Section 161 (hit-and-run):** compensation from the **Solatium Fund**. Earlier ₹ 25,000 (death) and ₹ 12,500 (grievous hurt); the **2019 amendment** raised these to **₹ 2 lakh** and **₹ 50,000**.

- **Section 164B (2019):** the **Motor Vehicle Accident Fund** for treatment and compensation of road-accident victims, with compulsory insurance for all road users.

### 9.3 Compulsory third-party insurance (Chapter XI)

- **Section 146:** **necessity for insurance against third-party risks:** no person shall use a motor vehicle in a public place unless a **policy of insurance** complying with the Chapter is in force (with limited exemptions for government vehicles).

- **Section 147:** **requirements of policies and limits of liability.** The policy must cover liability for **death or bodily injury** to any person (including owners of goods or their representatives carried in the vehicle) and for **damage to third-party property**. Liability for death and injury is **unlimited**; property damage is subject to a prescribed limit.

- **Section 149:** **duty of insurers to satisfy judgments and awards** in respect of third-party risks, even if the insurer is entitled to avoid the policy, subject to the limited defences in **Section 149(2)** (breach of specified conditions such as use for hire or reward, or use by an unlicensed driver). The insurer then has the right to **recover from the insured** (the **"pay and recover"** principle).

- **Section 150:** rights of third parties against insurers on the insolvency of the insured.

- **Key cases:** *Skandia Insurance Co v Kokilaben Chandravadan* (1987): an insurer cannot escape its liability to the third party through policy exclusions; ***National Insurance Co v Swaran Singh* (2004):** the insurer must prove that the breach was **fundamental** and that the owner was aware of it (e.g., fake driving licence); otherwise it must pay the third party and may recover from the owner; *Oriental Insurance v Meena Variyal* (2007).

- **Penalty for driving without insurance (Section 196):** imprisonment up to **three months**, or fine up to **₹ 2,000**, or both (as amended in 2019).

### 9.4 Motor Accident Claims Tribunals (Chapter XII)

- **Section 165:** the State Government may constitute **Claims Tribunals** to adjudicate claims for compensation arising from accidents (death, bodily injury, damage to third-party property).

- **Section 166:** **application for compensation** by (a) the **injured person**, (b) the **owner of damaged property**, (c) all or any **legal representatives of the deceased**, or (d) a duly authorised **agent**. It may be made to the Tribunal where the accident occurred, or where the claimant resides or carries on business, or where the defendant resides. A Section 166 claim is **fault-based**, so the claimant must prove negligence.

  - **2019 amendment:** the **six-month limitation** in Section 166(3) was **omitted**, so there is no limitation period; the **Detailed Accident Report** filed by the police may be treated as the claim application.

- **Section 167:** option to claim under the **Workmen's/Employees' Compensation Act** **or** the MVA, **not both**.

- **Section 168:** the Tribunal's award must be **just compensation**, and it specifies whether the insurer, owner or driver pays.

- **Section 169:** **summary procedure**, not bound by strict rules of evidence. **Section 170:** **impleading the insurer**. **Section 171:** **interest**. **Section 172:** **compensatory costs** for false or vexatious claims or defences.

- **Section 173:** **appeal to the High Court within 90 days**; an insurer's appeal requires a deposit of **₹ 25,000 or 50 percent** of the amount awarded, whichever is less.

- **Section 174:** recovery as arrears of land revenue. **Section 175:** **bar on civil court jurisdiction** over matters that the Tribunal can decide.

- **Reporting duties:** the police must prepare a **Detailed Accident Report** within a short time (48 hours for the interim report under the 2019 scheme) and forward it to the Tribunal.

### 9.5 Determination of compensation: principles from cases

- ***Sarla Verma v Delhi Transport Corporation* (2009):** standard **multipliers** by age and **deduction for personal expenses** (one-third for 2–3 dependants; one-fourth for 4–6; one-fifth for more than 6; one-half for a bachelor).

- ***National Insurance Co v Pranay Sethi* (2017)** (Constitution Bench): upheld *Sarla Verma*; **future prospects** must be added: for a person with a **permanent job** 50% (below 40 years), 30% (40–50), 15% (50–60); for **self-employed or fixed-salary** persons 40%, 25%, 10%; none above 60. **Conventional heads:** loss of estate **₹ 15,000**, funeral expenses **₹ 15,000**, loss of consortium **₹ 40,000**, increasing by **10% every three years**.

- ***Magma General Insurance v Nanu Ram* (2018):** loss of consortium includes **parental and filial** consortium, not only spousal.

- ***R.D. Hattangadi v Pest Control (India)* (1995):** heads of compensation in personal injury (pecuniary and non-pecuniary: pain and suffering, loss of amenities, loss of expectation of life).

- **Contributory negligence** reduces compensation proportionately; in **composite negligence** the claimant may recover from any of the joint tortfeasors.

- The **owner is vicariously liable** for the driver's negligence in the course of employment (*Pushpabai Purshottam Udeshi v Ranjit Ginning*, 1977); *res ipsa loquitur* is also invoked.

### 9.6 The Motor Vehicles (Amendment) Act 2019: key changes (trend)

- **Higher penalties** for traffic offences; where a **juvenile** commits an offence, the guardian or owner is deemed guilty and the vehicle registration may be cancelled.

- **Good Samaritan protection (Section 134A):** a person who voluntarily helps an accident victim is **not liable** to civil or criminal action.

- **"Golden hour"** (Section 2(12A)): the hour following a traumatic injury; **cashless treatment** during the golden hour (Section 162). A national cashless-treatment scheme has been framed in recent years (*verify the latest notified scheme*).

- **Motor Vehicle Accident Fund** (Section 164B); **enhanced hit-and-run compensation**; **removal of limitation** for claims.

- Provisions on **aggregators**, **recall of defective vehicles**, **digital licensing** and **road safety**.

### 9.7 Exam focus for Topic 9

Expect: the **no-fault principle** and amounts (old vs revised); **Section 163A vs Section 166** (*Deepal Girishbhai Soni*); **Section 149** (pay and recover; *Swaran Singh*); MACT application, **limitation (now omitted)**; **appeal to the High Court in 90 days**; **Section 175** (civil court bar); **hit-and-run** amounts; **Good Samaritan** and **golden hour**; **Motor Vehicle Accident Fund**; *Sarla Verma* and *Pranay Sethi*; Assertion–Reason on mutually exclusive claims.

## Chapter 10: Competition Act, 2002

### 10.1 Background and objectives

The **Competition Act 2002** replaced the **Monopolies and Restrictive Trade Practices Act 1969 (MRTP Act)**, which took a control-based approach suited to the licensing era. After the 1991 liberalisation and the **Raghavan Committee Report (2000)**, the law shifted to the **promotion of competition**. **Duties of the CCI (Section 18):** eliminate practices having an **adverse effect on competition**, **promote and sustain competition**, **protect the interests of consumers**, and ensure **freedom of trade** carried on by other participants in markets in India. The competition provisions (Sections 3 and 4) came into force on **20 May 2009**, and the combination provisions on **1 June 2011**. ***Brahm Dutt v Union of India* (2005)** upheld the Act's validity and the separation of advisory and adjudicatory functions, after which the 2007 amendment settled the present structure.

### 10.2 Institutional framework

- **Competition Commission of India (CCI)** (Section 7): a quasi-judicial body with a **Chairperson and not less than two and not more than six other members**. The **Director General (DG)** (Section 16) assists in investigations.

- **Appeals:** CCI orders are appealable to the **National Company Law Appellate Tribunal (NCLAT)** (Section 53A; the Competition Appellate Tribunal (COMPAT) was merged into NCLAT by the **Finance Act 2017**, from 26 May 2017), and then to the **Supreme Court** (Section 53T) within 60 days.

- **Competition advocacy** (Section 49): the Commission can give opinions and spread awareness.

- **Extra-territorial reach (Section 32):** the **effects doctrine**; the Act applies to conduct outside India having an appreciable adverse effect on competition in India.

- **Sectoral regulators (Sections 21 and 21A):** mutual reference between the CCI and regulators like TRAI, SEBI and CERC. *CCI v Bharti Airtel* (2018): the Supreme Court held that **TRAI** has primary jurisdiction on certain telecom matters, and the CCI should await its determination. *CCI v Steel Authority of India* (2010): the Section 26(1) *prima facie* opinion is **administrative**, not adjudicatory, so no hearing is required at that stage.

### 10.3 Anti-competitive agreements (Section 3)

**Section 3(1):** no enterprise, association of enterprises, person or association of persons shall enter into any agreement in respect of production, supply, distribution, storage, acquisition or control of goods or provision of services which **causes or is likely to cause an appreciable adverse effect on competition (AAEC) within India**. **Section 3(2):** any such agreement is **void**.

**Section 3(3) – Horizontal agreements** (between enterprises at the same stage of the production chain) are **presumed** to have AAEC (a **rebuttable presumption**) if they: (a) directly or indirectly **determine purchase or sale prices**; (b) **limit or control** production, supply, markets, technical development, investment or services; (c) **share markets** by geography, type of goods or number of customers; or (d) amount to **bid rigging or collusive bidding**. A **cartel** (Section 2(c)) is an association of producers, sellers, distributors, traders or service providers who, by agreement among themselves, limit, control or attempt to control production, distribution, sale or price. The **proviso** excludes **joint ventures** that increase **efficiency** in production, supply, distribution or control.

**Section 3(4) – Vertical agreements** (between enterprises at different stages of the chain) are judged by the **rule of reason**, so AAEC must be proved. Listed examples: **tie-in arrangement**, **exclusive supply agreement**, **exclusive distribution agreement**, **refusal to deal**, **resale price maintenance (RPM)**. The **2023 Amendment** widened these provisions and added **hub-and-spoke** liability in Section 3(3): an enterprise not in the same line of business that participates in or facilitates a cartel can also be liable.

**Section 3(5) – Exceptions:** (i) the right to restrain infringement of, or impose **reasonable conditions** to protect, **intellectual property rights** (copyright, patents, trademarks, geographical indications, designs, semiconductor layouts); (ii) the right to export, where the agreement relates exclusively to production, supply, distribution or control of goods **for export**.

**Factors for determining AAEC (Section 19(3)):** creation of entry barriers; driving existing competitors out; foreclosure of competition; accrual of benefits to consumers; improvements in production or distribution; promotion of technical, scientific and economic development.

**Recent enforcement trend:** the cement cartel order (2012; penalty about ₹ 6,307 crore), *Maruti Suzuki* (2021; ₹ 200 crore for resale price maintenance through its discount control policy), and cartel cases in beer, film and cable industries.

### 10.4 Abuse of dominant position (Section 4)

**Section 4(1):** no enterprise or group shall **abuse its dominant position**. Dominance itself is **not** prohibited; **abuse** is.

- **Dominant position** (Explanation to Section 4): a position of **strength** in the **relevant market** in India that enables an enterprise to (i) **operate independently of competitive forces**, or (ii) **affect its competitors, consumers or the relevant market** in its favour.

- **Relevant market** (Section 2(r)): the **relevant product market** (Section 2(t): products regarded as interchangeable or substitutable by reason of characteristics, prices and intended use) and/or the **relevant geographic market** (Section 2(s): area where conditions of competition for supply or demand are distinctly homogeneous). Section 19(5)–(7) list the relevant factors.

- **Factors for dominance (Section 19(4)):** market share; size and resources of the enterprise; size and importance of competitors; economic power and commercial advantages; vertical integration; consumer dependence; monopoly or dominant position by statute; entry barriers; countervailing buying power; market structure and size; social obligations and costs.

- **Abuse (Section 4(2)):** (a) **unfair or discriminatory conditions or prices** (including **predatory pricing**) in purchase or sale; (b) **limiting or restricting** production, services, or **technical or scientific development**; (c) practices resulting in **denial of market access**; (d) making contracts subject to **supplementary obligations** unconnected with the subject of the contract (tie-in or bundling); (e) **using dominance in one relevant market to enter into, or protect, another relevant market** (leveraging).

- **Cases:** ***Belaire Owners' Association v DLF* (2011):** CCI penalised DLF (₹ 630 crore) for unfair conditions in apartment buyer agreements; the finding of abuse was upheld on appeal (*verify procedural history*). ***CCI v Co-ordination Committee of Artists and Technicians of West Bengal Film and Television* (2017):** the Act applies to trade associations. ***Excel Crop Care v CCI* (2017):** penalty to be based on **relevant turnover**. **Digital markets:** the CCI's *Google* (Android and Play Store) orders of 2022 (penalties of about ₹ 1,337 crore and ₹ 936 crore respectively), later reviewed by the NCLAT and the Supreme Court, and the 2024 *Meta/WhatsApp* privacy-policy order (about ₹ 213 crore) (*verify the current status of these appeals*).

- **Digital competition (trend):** a draft **Digital Competition Bill** proposing *ex ante* regulation of **systemically significant digital enterprises** was released in 2024 (its status should be checked).

### 10.5 Combinations (Sections 5 and 6)

- **Section 5:** a **combination** is an **acquisition** of control, shares, voting rights or assets, an acquisition of **control** by a person already controlling a competing enterprise, or a **merger or amalgamation**, that crosses the **asset or turnover thresholds** specified in the Act (in India and worldwide, for the parties and for the group). The thresholds have been **revised upwards** by notification, and the **de minimis (small target) exemption** was updated in 2024 to **₹ 450 crore of assets or ₹ 1,250 crore of turnover** in India (*verify current thresholds*).

- **Deal value threshold (DVT):** introduced by the **2023 Amendment**, a transaction with a **value above ₹ 2,000 crore** where the target has **substantial business operations in India** must be notified even if the asset or turnover tests are not met (in force from **10 September 2024**).

- **Section 6(1):** no combination shall cause or be likely to cause **AAEC within the relevant market in India**; such a combination is **void**.

- **Section 6(2):** notice to the CCI before the combination takes effect. The earlier requirement to file within 30 days of the trigger event was first exempted in 2017 and then removed by the 2023 Act. A **standstill (suspensory) obligation** applies: the combination cannot take effect until the CCI approves or the time limit expires.

- **Time limits (2023 Amendment):** the CCI must form its **prima facie opinion within 30 calendar days** and decide within **150 days** (earlier 210 days) from notice; otherwise the combination is **deemed approved**.

- **Section 20:** inquiry into combinations; **Section 29:** investigation procedure; **Section 31:** orders (approval, approval with modification, or disapproval). **Section 43A:** penalty for failure to notify (up to 1 percent of total turnover or assets, whichever is higher). **Section 20(4)** lists factors in assessing the effect of a combination (actual and potential competition, market share, entry barriers, failing-business defence, benefits, etc.).

- **Green channel route** (Combination Regulations): automatic approval for combinations with no horizontal, vertical or complementary overlaps.

### 10.6 Penalties and enforcement

- **Section 27:** after an inquiry the CCI may issue a **cease and desist** order, impose a **penalty up to 10 percent of the average turnover** (earlier, of the last three preceding financial years), direct modification of agreements, and so on. For a **cartel**, the penalty may be up to **three times the profit for each year of continuance, or 10 percent of the turnover for each year, whichever is higher**. **Section 28** allows the division of an enterprise enjoying dominance. Compensation claims go to the NCLAT (Section 53N).

- **Section 46 (lesser penalty / leniency):** reduced penalty for cartel members making full, true and vital disclosure. The **2023 Amendment** adds **Leniency Plus**: additional reduction where the applicant discloses a second cartel.

- **Sections 48A and 48B (2023 Amendment): settlement and commitment** mechanisms for abuse of dominance and vertical agreements (**cartels are excluded**).

- **Global turnover:** the 2023 Amendment allows penalties to be calculated on **global turnover** (not only relevant turnover, as under *Excel Crop Care*).

- **Time limit:** information must be filed within **three years** of the cause of action (Section 19(6)).

- **Other powers:** interim orders (Section 33), search and seizure (DG), penalties for non-compliance (Sections 42, 43).

### 10.7 The Competition (Amendment) Act 2023: summary (very high yield)

1.  **Deal value threshold** (₹ 2,000 crore) for notifiable combinations.

2.  **Settlement and commitment** procedure (Sections 48A and 48B).

3.  **Global turnover**-based penalties and a revised definition of "turnover".

4.  **Hub-and-spoke cartels** and wider scope of Section 3.

5.  **Leniency Plus** and revised lesser-penalty framework.

6.  **Shorter time limits** for combination review (30 days *prima facie*, 150 days overall).

7.  A wider definition of **"control"** (**material influence**) and of **"agreement"** to include concerted practices.

8.  Stronger **DG investigation powers** and penalties for non-cooperation.

9.  Appeals to the NCLAT with a reported **pre-deposit requirement** of 25 percent of the penalty (*verify*).

The 2023 Amendment has been brought into force in phases, so verify the notified dates for each provision.

### 11.3 Concept traps examiners love

- **Damnum sine injuria** is not actionable; **injuria sine damno** is actionable.

- **Volenti** is a complete defence; **contributory negligence** is a partial defence.

- **Motive** is irrelevant in tort except in nuisance, conspiracy, defamation and malicious prosecution.

- **Slander** generally needs special damage (English rule) with four per se exceptions; Indian courts treat the distinction loosely.

- **Statutory authority:** permissive vs mandatory statutes.

- **Rylands** has exceptions; **M.C. Mehta absolute liability** has none.

- **Public-law compensation** (Articles 32/226) is not barred by sovereign immunity; **private-law** suits against the State follow Article 300 and *Vidhyawati/Kasturi Lal*.

- The **CCPA** protects consumers as a class; the **Commissions** decide individual complaints.

- **Section 163A** claims need no proof of negligence; **Section 166** claims do.

- **Dominance is not prohibited; abuse is.** Horizontal agreements carry a *presumption* of AAEC; vertical agreements follow the *rule of reason*.
