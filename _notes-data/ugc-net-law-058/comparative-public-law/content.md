## Chapter 0: How This Unit Is Examined

### 0.1 Four kinds of questions

UGC NET Law questions from this unit are almost always one of four kinds:

- **Memory-based:** who said it, which case, which article, which year, which country has which feature (e.g., "Which country has an Ombudsman called Justitieombudsmannen?").

## Chapter 1: Comparative Law: Relevance, Methodology, Problems and Concerns in Comparison

### 1.1 Meaning and Nature of Comparative Law

Comparative law is the study of the similarities and differences between the legal systems, rules, institutions and doctrines of two or more jurisdictions, with a view to understanding, evaluating, improving or harmonising them. It is both a **method** (a way of studying law) and, according to some, a **science** (a body of knowledge with its own subject matter). This "method versus science" debate is itself a recurring theoretical point.

Key points on the nature of the discipline:

- Comparative law is not a branch of law in the sense of containing rules that bind anyone. It does not create rights and duties. It is an **intellectual activity** whose results are used by legislators, judges, scholars and international bodies.

- It is different from **foreign law**. Foreign law is the study of one other country's law. Comparative law necessarily involves comparing at least two systems and drawing out similarities and differences.

- It is different from **private international law (conflict of laws)**, which decides which country's law applies to a cross-border dispute. Comparative law decides nothing; it only compares.

- It is different from **public international law**, which governs relations between states.

- **Comparative public law** (the focus of this unit) compares constitutions, governmental structures, federalism, separation of powers, judicial review, administrative accountability and similar subjects. A major part of it is **comparative constitutional law**.

### 1.2 Historical Development (Memory Points)

- Early seeds: Aristotle collected and compared the constitutions of 158 Greek city-states (the "Politics" draws on it). Montesquieu's *The Spirit of the Laws* (1748) compared laws in relation to climate, geography and culture and is considered a foundation of the comparative approach.

- **Sir Henry Maine** (*Ancient Law*, 1861) used a historical-comparative method for studying legal institutions (status to contract).

- **1869:** Société de Législation Comparée founded in Paris.

- **1900:** The **First International Congress of Comparative Law, Paris**, organised by **Édouard Lambert and Raymond Saleilles**. Comparative law is said to have become a modern discipline here. Saleilles and Lambert are also associated with the idea of a "common law of mankind" (*droit commun de l'humanité*).

- **Édouard Lambert** (France) – ambition of a world common law; headed the Lyon Institute of Comparative Law.

- **Ernst Rabel** (Germany) – developed the **functional method** in comparative private law.

- **Zweigert and Kötz** – *An Introduction to Comparative Law* – the most cited text; developed the functional method and the "legal families" (style) theory; the **praesumptio similitudinis** (presumption of similarity).

- **René David** – classification into legal families (Romano-Germanic, Common Law, Socialist, and others).

- **Gutteridge** (*Comparative Law*, 1946) – English scholar who described comparative law as a method and used it to argue for law reform.

- **Alan Watson** – *Legal Transplants* (1974) – laws migrate across borders easily.

- **Otto Kahn-Freund** – "On Uses and Misuses of Comparative Law" (1974) – the transplant of a rule is difficult if the political and social context differs; coined the idea of "degrees of transferability".

- **Pierre Legrand** – argued that legal transplants are impossible in a true sense because law is embedded in culture and meaning; the rule changes meaning when it moves.

- **Rodolfo Sacco** – theory of **legal formants** (statutes, judicial decisions, scholarly writings are separate sources which can conflict within one legal system).

- **Lawrence Friedman** – "legal culture" – internal and external legal culture.

### 1.3 Relevance and Purposes (Why Compare?)

1.  **Legislative reform:** Legislatures look at foreign models while drafting. The Indian Constituent Assembly borrowed heavily: parliamentary system from the UK; fundamental rights, judicial review and Preamble from the USA; Directive Principles from Ireland; federal scheme with a strong Centre and residuary powers from Canada; Concurrent List from Australia; emergency provisions from Germany (Weimar); amending procedure from South Africa; Rule of Law and law-making procedure from the UK; "procedure established by law" from Japan.

2.  **Judicial interpretation:** Courts cite foreign precedents as persuasive authority. The Indian Supreme Court has regularly referred to American, English, Canadian and South African decisions (e.g., on privacy, dignity, proportionality, sexual orientation).

3.  **Unification and harmonisation:** Organisations such as UNIDROIT, UNCITRAL and the Hague Conference work on harmonising private and commercial law. The EU's attempt to build a common European private law is a famous example.

4.  **International law:** Article 38(1)(c) of the ICJ Statute lists "general principles of law recognised by civilised nations" as a source of international law. Identifying such principles requires comparing national laws.

5.  **Legal education and academic understanding:** Understanding one's own system better through contrast.

6.  **Constitution making and transitions:** South Africa, Eastern Europe and post-colonial states used comparative models while drafting new constitutions.

7.  **Conflict of laws and international commerce:** helps lawyers handle cross-border transactions.

8.  **Combating parochialism:** reveals that a rule thought universal is merely local.

### 1.4 Types and Levels of Comparison

- **Macro-comparison:** compares whole legal systems, their style, sources, methods, and legal families.

- **Micro-comparison:** compares specific institutions or rules (e.g., rules on judicial review in two countries).

- **Horizontal comparison:** compares systems of equal standing (e.g., India with the USA).

- **Vertical comparison:** compares different levels of the same hierarchy (e.g., national law vs state or provincial law; national vs international law).

- **Descriptive comparative law:** records similarities and differences without evaluation.

- **Applied comparative law:** uses comparison to solve a problem or reform law.

- **Theoretical/Evaluative comparative law:** explores the reasons behind similarities and differences and seeks a general theory.

### 1.5 Methodology (Most Testable Part)

#### (a) Functional Method (dominant approach)

- Basic principle: **"The legal system of every society faces essentially the same problems, and solves these problems by quite different means though very often with similar results."** (Zweigert and Kötz)

- Law is compared by asking: *What problem does this rule solve?* Rules that solve the same problem are comparable even if their form differs. The common standard is **tertium comparationis** (a third, neutral element or common denominator used for comparison, here the function or problem).

- Linked idea: **praesumptio similitudinis** – the presumption that different legal systems reach practically similar results for the same factual problem. If the result differs, the comparatist must re-check whether the analysis was correct.

- Chief proponents: Ernst Rabel, Zweigert and Kötz.

- Criticisms: assumes universal problems and ignores culture, politics, ideology and meaning; "law in books" may be compared more than "law in action".

#### (b) Analytical Method

Breaks down legal concepts and structures into elements (rights, duties, powers, status) for logical comparison. Linked to Bentham and Austin's analytical jurisprudence.

#### (c) Structural Method

Compares the structure of institutions and norms (e.g., court hierarchy, legislative design). Often used in comparative constitutional law, such as comparing bicameral structures.

#### (d) Historical Method

Studies legal systems as products of history. Associated with Maine and Savigny's historical school. Useful for understanding why India follows common-law traditions despite its diversity.

#### (e) Law-in-Context (Socio-legal) Method

Studies law along with social, economic, political and cultural conditions. Associated with Kahn-Freund and Legrand's culturalist approach. Compares "law in action".

#### (f) Critical/Culturalist Method

Includes critical legal studies, feminist and post-colonial approaches. It questions the neutrality of comparison and its Western bias.

#### (g) Comparison by Legal Families

- **Romano-Germanic (Civil law):** codified; France, Germany, Italy, Spain, Latin America, Switzerland (mixed features).

- **Common law:** judge-made law and precedent (*stare decisis*); England, USA, India, Canada (except Quebec), Australia.

- **Socialist law:** historically in USSR and Eastern Bloc; now largely declined, remains in some forms (China, Cuba, Vietnam).

- **Religious and traditional law:** Islamic law, Hindu law, Jewish law.

- **Customary/Far Eastern law:** Japan, China, Korea blends.

- **Mixed systems:** South Africa (Roman-Dutch and common law), Scotland, Louisiana, Quebec, Israel, Sri Lanka.

Zweigert and Kötz classify by "legal style" using factors: historical development, characteristic mode of thought, distinctive institutions, legal sources and ideology.

### 1.6 Problems and Concerns in Comparison

1.  **Language:** legal terms are untranslatable (e.g., "equity", "trust", "consideration", "*Rechtsstaat*", "*état de droit*"). Mistranslation gives a false match.

2.  **Context and culture:** a rule works because of its society; stripping it of context produces misleading results.

3.  **Choice of comparable units:** comparing a unitary and a federal system or a codified and uncodified constitution requires care to avoid mismatching functions.

4.  **Ethnocentrism/bias:** comparatist sees foreign systems through own categories; Western-centric classification of legal families ignores African and Asian systems.

5.  **Law in books vs law in action:** formal texts can differ from actual practice (e.g., a constitution declares separation of powers but practice differs).

6.  **Availability and reliability of sources:** translations, outdated sources, lack of case law.

7.  **Legal transplants:** transplanted rules may be rejected (*legal irritants* – Gunther Teubner) or may be transformed. Teubner argued that transplants act as irritants that trigger new evolution in the host system rather than harmonising it.

8.  **Superficial comparison:** comparing without deep knowledge, or "cherry-picking" foreign decisions to justify a preferred result (a concern in judicial use of foreign law).

9.  **Time:** laws change quickly; the comparative picture becomes outdated.

10. **Ideological presumption:** assuming that all legal systems must converge ("convergence thesis") can ignore deep differences.

### Memory Capsule: Chapter 1

- Functional method = Zweigert and Kötz, Ernst Rabel; **tertium comparationis**; **praesumptio similitudinis**.

- 1900 Paris Congress = Lambert and Saleilles.

- Legal transplants = **Alan Watson** (supporter); **Legrand** (sceptic); **Kahn-Freund** (cautious); **Teubner** (legal irritants).

- Legal formants = **Rodolfo Sacco**.

- Comparative law is not a source of binding law; it is a method.

- Trap: Comparative law ≠ private international law ≠ foreign law.

## Chapter 2: Forms of Government: Presidential and Parliamentary; Unitary and Federal

### 2.1 Classification Basis

Forms of government can be classified on two main axes:

- **Relationship between the legislature and the executive:** parliamentary, presidential, semi-presidential (hybrid) and directorial/collegial.

- **Distribution of power between the centre and units:** unitary, federal, and quasi-federal or confederal.

Older classification: Aristotle classified by number of rulers and their purpose (monarchy, aristocracy, polity; and degenerate forms – tyranny, oligarchy, democracy). Modern comparative law uses the functional classification above.

### 2.2 Parliamentary Government

**Meaning:** The executive (Cabinet/Council of Ministers) is drawn from the legislature, is collectively responsible to it, and remains in office as long as it enjoys the confidence of the lower house.

**Key features:**

- **Dual executive:** a nominal head (Monarch/President) and a real executive (Prime Minister and Cabinet).

- **Fusion of powers:** ministers are members of the legislature.

- **Collective responsibility** of the Council of Ministers; **individual responsibility** of each minister.

- Executive can be removed by a **vote of no confidence**; the executive can advise **dissolution** of the lower house.

- Prime Minister is leader of the majority party or coalition.

- **Party discipline** and the concept of an **Opposition** (in the UK, "His Majesty's Loyal Opposition" with a shadow cabinet).

- Flexible and responsive but may produce instability in coalitions.

**Thinkers:** **Walter Bagehot**, *The English Constitution* (1867) – distinguished the **dignified** and **efficient** parts of the constitution; described the Cabinet as "a hyphen which joins, a buckle which fastens" the legislative to the executive. **A.V. Dicey** – parliamentary sovereignty and conventions. **Ivor Jennings** – *Cabinet Government*, *The Law and the Constitution*.

**Examples:** United Kingdom (the model – Westminster model), India, Canada, Australia, Japan, Germany (chancellor system), Italy.

### 2.3 Presidential Government

**Meaning:** The executive power is vested in a President who is both head of state and head of government, elected independently of the legislature for a fixed term, and not responsible to the legislature for his tenure (removal only by impeachment).

**Key features:**

- **Single executive** – President.

- **Strict separation of powers** and **checks and balances.**

- Fixed tenure; the President cannot dissolve the legislature, and the legislature cannot remove him by a no-confidence vote.

- Cabinet members are **not members of the legislature**; they are secretaries/advisors appointed by the President (in the USA, with Senate confirmation).

- Stable executive but risk of **deadlock/divided government** (Juan Linz argued that presidential systems are more prone to democratic breakdown).

**Examples:** USA (the model), Brazil, Mexico, Argentina, Indonesia, Philippines, Nigeria.

**U.S. specifics:** Article II vests executive power in the President. The President is elected indirectly by the **Electoral College**. Removal by **impeachment** (House impeaches; Senate tries; two-thirds majority to convict). The 22nd Amendment limits the President to two terms. Veto power with congressional override by two-thirds.

### 2.5 Semi-Presidential (Hybrid) and Other Systems

- **Semi-presidential system:** A directly elected President with real powers coexists with a Prime Minister and Cabinet responsible to the legislature. **Maurice Duverger** coined the term. **France under the Fifth Republic (1958)** is the standard example. **Cohabitation** occurs when the President and the Prime Minister come from rival political camps (France: 1986–88, 1993–95, 1997–2002).

- **Switzerland (directorial/collegial executive):** The **Federal Council** of seven members is elected by the Federal Assembly for a fixed four-year term and cannot be dismissed by it. The presidency rotates annually among the seven members (first among equals). It is neither purely parliamentary nor presidential.

- **Parliamentary monarchy vs parliamentary republic:** UK (monarchy) and Germany/India (republic).

### 2.6 Unitary Government

**Meaning:** Sovereignty is concentrated in a central government; sub-units (local bodies, provinces) exist only by the will of the centre and can be created, altered or abolished by it.

**Features:**

- Single legislature with supreme power; no constitutional division of powers.

- Single citizenship, and uniform laws and courts.

- Local bodies exercise delegated powers.

- Can be flexible, efficient and cost-effective; suitable for small, homogeneous states.

- **Devolution** (UK: Scotland, Wales, Northern Ireland) does not make the UK federal because Parliament remains sovereign and can legally repeal devolution statutes. The UK is commonly described as a **unitary state with devolution**.

**Examples:** UK, France (although decentralised since the 1982 reforms), Japan, Italy, China (unitary by constitution), Sri Lanka.

### 2.7 Federal Government

**Meaning:** Powers are constitutionally divided between a national government and regional units (states, provinces, cantons), each being supreme within its own sphere.

**Essential features (K.C. Wheare's** *Federal Government*, 1946):

1.  A **written constitution** (generally rigid) – supremacy of the Constitution.

2.  **Division of powers** between centre and units.

3.  An **independent judiciary** to interpret the Constitution and settle disputes (guardian of federalism).

4.  **Rigid amendment procedure** (units usually participate in amendment).

5.  **Dual government** with each level acting directly on the citizen.

6.  Usually a **bicameral legislature** with the second chamber representing the units (Senate, Council of States, Ständerat).

**K.C. Wheare's definition:** the federal principle is the method of dividing powers so that the general and regional governments are each, within a sphere, co-ordinate and independent. He called the Indian Constitution **quasi-federal** (federal in form but unitary in spirit).

**Other characterisations of India:** Ivor Jennings – "a federation with a strong centralising tendency". Morris-Jones – "bargaining federalism". Granville Austin – "cooperative federalism". Supreme Court in **S.R. Bommai v Union of India (1994)** – federalism is part of the basic structure; the Constitution is federal in structure with a unitary bias.

**Classification:**

- **Coming-together federations** (independent states unite): USA, Switzerland, Australia – units are strong; residuary powers with units.

- **Holding-together federations** (a unitary state decentralises to preserve unity): India, Belgium – centre is stronger; residuary powers with the centre. (This terminology is associated with **Alfred Stepan**.)

- **Confederation:** a loose alliance of sovereign states where units retain sovereignty (e.g., the USA under the Articles of Confederation, 1781–89; Switzerland before 1848; the EU is often described as sui generis, with confederal features).

- **Bagehot** → "The English Constitution"; dignified and efficient parts. **Dicey** → rule of law and parliamentary sovereignty. **K.C. Wheare** → quasi-federal India.

- UK is **unitary** despite devolution; **France** is unitary but semi-presidential.

- **Switzerland**: collegial executive (not parliamentary, not presidential).

- Presidential system → **Linz** (dangers); semi-presidential → **Duverger**.

- India has **single citizenship** unlike the USA (dual citizenship – national and state). India's integrated judiciary vs the USA's dual court system (federal and state).

## Chapter 3: Models of Federalism: USA, Canada and India

### 3.1 United States of America

#### Origins and Structure

- The Constitution of 1787 (in force 1789) replaced the weak **Articles of Confederation**. It is the oldest written federal Constitution – the classical **dual federalism** model, a "coming-together" federation.

- **Enumerated powers** of Congress are listed in Article I, Section 8; **residuary powers belong to the States** (Tenth Amendment: powers not delegated to the United States nor prohibited to the States are reserved to the States or the people).

- **Supremacy Clause (Article VI):** the Constitution, federal laws and treaties are the supreme law of the land; state judges are bound.

- **Equal representation** of states in the Senate (two each), regardless of population; no state can be deprived of equal Senate suffrage without its consent (Article V).

- **Amendment procedure (Article V):** proposal by two-thirds of both Houses (or a convention called by two-thirds of the state legislatures), and ratification by three-fourths of the states.

- **Dual court system:** federal courts and state courts operate side by side; the Supreme Court is the final interpreter of the Constitution.

- **Dual citizenship:** citizen of the USA and of the State of residence (Fourteenth Amendment).

- Each State has its own Constitution.

#### Phases of American Federalism (Highly Testable)

1.  **Dual federalism ("layer-cake" – associated with Edward Corwin):** till about the 1930s; strict separation of national and state spheres.

2.  **Cooperative federalism ("marble-cake" – Morton Grodzins):** from the New Deal (1930s); shared functions through grants-in-aid and joint programmes.

3.  **Creative federalism** (Johnson's Great Society, 1960s) and **New Federalism** (Nixon, Reagan, and the later revival of state powers by the Rehnquist Court).

4.  Modern debates on **coercive and competitive federalism**.

#### Landmark Cases

- **McCulloch v Maryland (1819):** Chief Justice **John Marshall** upheld Congress's power to create a national bank under the **Necessary and Proper Clause** (implied powers) and held that states cannot tax federal institutions ("the power to tax involves the power to destroy").

- **Gibbons v Ogden (1824):** broad reading of the **Commerce Clause**; Congress controls interstate commerce.

- **United States v Lopez (1995):** first major limit on the Commerce Clause since the New Deal (Gun-Free School Zones Act struck down).

- **New York v United States (1992) and Printz v United States (1997):** Congress cannot command state legislatures or officials to enforce federal programmes (the **anti-commandeering doctrine**).

- **NFIB v Sebelius (2012):** upheld the Affordable Care Act's individual mandate under the taxing power; limited coercive spending conditions on states regarding Medicaid expansion.

- **Erie Railroad v Tompkins (1938):** federal courts apply state substantive law in diversity cases.

#### Summary

Strong states, strict federal structure, equal Senate representation, rigid amendment, residuary powers with states, and judicial review as the guardian of federalism.

### 3.2 Canada

#### Origins and Structure

- **Constitution Act, 1867 (formerly the British North America Act, 1867):** Confederation of Ontario, Quebec, Nova Scotia and New Brunswick. Canada is a **parliamentary federation** and a constitutional monarchy; the Governor General represents the Crown.

- **Patriation (1982):** the **Constitution Act, 1982** (including the **Charter of Rights and Freedoms**) ended the need to approach the UK Parliament for amendments. The **Patriation Reference (1981)** held that the federal government's unilateral patriation was legal but violated a convention requiring substantial provincial consent. Quebec did not sign the 1982 agreement.

- **Distribution of powers:** **Section 91** gives enumerated powers to the federal Parliament and also the **residuary power – "peace, order and good government" (POGG)**; **Section 92** lists exclusive provincial powers (property and civil rights, local and private matters, administration of justice, etc.); **Section 93** deals with education; **Section 95** gives concurrent power over agriculture and immigration. The design intentionally gave the centre more power than in the USA, reacting to the American Civil War (Sir John A. Macdonald's vision of a strong centre).

- **Federal paramountcy:** in case of conflict between valid federal and provincial laws, the federal law prevails.

- **Senate:** appointed (by the Governor General on the advice of the Prime Minister), regionally distributed, not elected, and so weaker as a representative of the provinces than the US Senate. This is a regular criticism.

- **Amendment procedure (Part V, Constitution Act, 1982):** general formula (the **7/50 rule**): resolutions of both Houses of Parliament and the legislatures of at least two-thirds of provinces having at least 50% of the population; special formulas apply, including unanimity for certain matters (the office of the Crown, composition of the Supreme Court, the amending formula itself).

- **Notwithstanding clause (Section 33 of the Charter):** allows Parliament or a provincial legislature to override certain Charter rights for five years (renewable).

- **Judiciary:** a unified court system under the Supreme Court of Canada, with judges appointed federally. It is not a dual system like the USA.

- **Asymmetrical federalism and Quebec:** Quebec has distinct civil law, language and cultural recognition. The **Quebec Secession Reference (1998)** held that Quebec has no unilateral right to secede under Canadian or international law, but a clear majority on a clear question creates a duty to negotiate. The Court identified four underlying constitutional principles: **federalism, democracy, constitutionalism and the rule of law, and protection of minorities.**

#### Role of the Privy Council (Memory Points)

Early interpretation by the **Judicial Committee of the Privy Council** (Lord Watson and Viscount Haldane) favoured provinces and narrowed federal power by a restrictive reading of POGG and trade and commerce. In the **Labour Conventions case (1937)**, the Privy Council held that the federal government cannot implement treaties in areas of provincial jurisdiction merely by signing them. Appeals to the Privy Council ended in 1949; the Supreme Court of Canada later restored a more centralist balance.

#### Summary

A centralised federation with strong provinces in practice; parliamentary; residuary power with the centre; appointed Senate; asymmetry for Quebec; the Charter's notwithstanding clause is distinctive.

### 3.3 India

#### Constitutional Structure

- **Article 1:** "India, that is Bharat, shall be a **Union of States**." Dr. B.R. Ambedkar explained in the Constituent Assembly that "Union" was used because the Indian federation is **not the result of an agreement between states, and no state has a right to secede.** He said the Constitution can be both unitary and federal according to the requirements of time and circumstances.

- **Seventh Schedule:** **Union List, State List and Concurrent List** (Article 246). Residuary powers lie with **Parliament** (Article 248 and Entry 97 of the Union List). The Concurrent List is borrowed from Australia; the scheme of division from the Government of India Act, 1935.

- **Doctrines for resolving conflicts:** pith and substance, colourable legislation, incidental and ancillary powers, harmonious construction, and repugnancy (**Article 254**: a Union law prevails over a State law on a Concurrent subject, unless the State law has received Presidential assent).

- **Article 3:** Parliament can form new states and alter areas, boundaries or names of states by simple law; the State's views are only to be ascertained and are not binding (*Babulal Parate v State of Bombay*, 1960). This gives the description **"an indestructible Union of destructible States"**.

- **Emergency provisions (Articles 352, 356, 360):** in a national emergency the federal structure converts into a unitary one; Parliament can legislate on State subjects (Article 250). **Article 356** (President's Rule) is constrained by **S.R. Bommai v Union of India (1994).**

- **Other unitary features:** single citizenship, integrated judiciary, All-India Services (Article 312), Governors appointed by the President (Article 155), Parliament's power to legislate on a State List subject in the national interest (Article 249, by a two-thirds Rajya Sabha resolution), a single Election Commission, the CAG, and the financial dependence of States on the Centre.

- **Cooperative and fiscal federalism institutions:** **Inter-State Council (Article 263)**, **Finance Commission (Article 280)**, **GST Council (Article 279A, 101st Amendment, 2016)**, **Zonal Councils (States Reorganisation Act, 1956)**, **NITI Aayog** (replaced the Planning Commission in 2015), **inter-State water disputes (Article 262)**, and **inter-State trade (Articles 301–307)**.

- **Amendment (Article 368):** most amendments are by Parliament alone; those affecting federal features (Seventh Schedule, representation of States in Parliament, Article 368 itself, election of the President, provisions on the Supreme Court and High Courts, and so on) need **ratification by at least half of the State legislatures.**

- **Rajya Sabha** is a Council of States with representation roughly proportional to population (not equal, unlike the US Senate).

#### Key Cases and Commissions

- **State of West Bengal v Union of India (1962):** the Constitution is not truly federal; States are not sovereign; the Union is dominant (federal in structure but unitary in essence).

- **S.R. Bommai v Union of India (1994):** federalism is a **basic feature**; Article 356 is subject to judicial review; Parliament must approve the Proclamation within two months.

- **Kesavananda Bharati v State of Kerala (1973):** federal character recognised as part of the basic structure.

- **State of Rajasthan v Union of India (1977):** upheld dismissal of State governments by the Centre in specific circumstances (later restricted by Bommai).

- **Government of NCT of Delhi v Union of India (2018 and 2023):** Constitution Benches emphasised cooperative federalism; in 2023 the Court held that the elected Delhi government controls "services" other than police, public order and land. Parliament then enacted the **Government of NCT of Delhi (Amendment) Act, 2023**, which was challenged.

- **Mineral Area Development Authority v Steel Authority of India (2024):** a nine-judge Bench held that States have the power to tax mineral rights (Entry 50 of the State List), subject to limits Parliament may impose under Entry 54 of the Union List. Considered a pro-State ruling.

- **Sarkaria Commission (1983–88):** on Centre–State relations; recommended sparing use of Article 356, a permanent Inter-State Council, and norms for appointing Governors.

- **M.M. Punchhi Commission (2007–10):** also advocated sparing use of Article 356 and guidelines for Governors; stressed cooperative federalism.

- **Rajamannar Committee (Tamil Nadu, 1969)** and the **Anandpur Sahib Resolution (1973)** sought greater State autonomy.

#### Characterisations of Indian Federalism

- **Quasi-federal** – K.C. Wheare.

- **Federation with a centralising tendency** – Ivor Jennings.

- **Cooperative federalism** – Granville Austin.

- **Bargaining federalism** – Morris-Jones.

- **Asymmetrical federalism** – special provisions for certain States (Articles 371 to 371J) and the earlier special status of Jammu and Kashmir (Article 370 was abrogated in 2019 and the abrogation upheld in *In Re Article 370*, 2023).

- **Federal in structure, unitary in spirit** – the standard exam phrase.

## Chapter 4: Rule of Law: Formal and Substantive Versions

### 4.1 Meaning and Origins

The **rule of law** means that government and all persons are subject to law, and that law must meet certain standards of fairness, clarity and non-arbitrariness. The core idea is the opposite of **arbitrary power**: the state may act only on the authority of law.

- Ancient roots: Aristotle ("it is better for the law to rule than any single citizen"); Magna Carta (1215); Sir Edward Coke (the King is under God and the law – *Prohibitions del Roy*, 1607; *Dr. Bonham's Case*, 1610).

- Related continental concept: **Rechtsstaat** (Germany), **état de droit** (France), the state governed by law, with limits on administration through legality.

- **Rule of law ≠ rule by law:** "rule by law" treats law as a mere instrument of rulers, even if oppressive; the rule of law constrains rulers themselves.

### 4.2 A.V. Dicey (Classical Formulation)

Dicey, *Introduction to the Study of the Law of the Constitution* (1885), identified three meanings:

1.  **Supremacy of regular law over arbitrary power** – no person is punishable except for a distinct breach of law established in the ordinary legal manner before the ordinary courts. It excludes wide discretionary powers of the government.

2.  **Equality before the law** – all classes are subject to the ordinary law administered by the ordinary courts; officials are not above the law and have no special privileges. Dicey contrasted this with the French *droit administratif* (a separate system of administrative courts), which he thought gave officials special protection.

3.  **The constitution is the result of the ordinary law of the land** – rights of individuals arise from judicial decisions in ordinary cases (in England, the *predominance of the legal spirit*), not from a written declaration of rights.

**Leading English cases linked to Dicey's principles:** *Entick v Carrington* (1765) – executive cannot act without legal authority; *Wilkes v Wood* (1763); *Leach v Money*; *Liversidge v Anderson* (1942) – the majority deferred to executive satisfaction while **Lord Atkin's dissent** is celebrated for upholding the rule of law; *M v Home Office* (1994) – ministers are subject to the courts' contempt jurisdiction; *Re Factortame*.

**Criticisms of Dicey:**

- He misunderstood *droit administratif* (it is in fact a system protecting citizens against the administration).

- Modern government needs **delegated legislation, administrative discretion and tribunals**, which contradict his first principle.

- Officials and the Crown had immunities (though reduced by the **Crown Proceedings Act, 1947**).

- Equality is not absolute; many persons (judges, diplomats, members of Parliament) have special immunities.

- Ignores social and economic inequality. **Ivor Jennings** (*The Law and the Constitution*) criticised Dicey's approach as being rooted in 19th century laissez-faire. **W.A. Robson** and **Lord Hewart** (*The New Despotism*, 1929) debated the growth of administrative power.

### 4.3 Formal (Thin) Conceptions of the Rule of Law

Formal theories look at the **form, procedure and characteristics of law**, not its moral content. A legal system can satisfy the formal rule of law even if the laws are unjust, provided they are general, public, prospective, clear and applied fairly.

**Lon Fuller – *The Morality of Law* (1964): eight principles (the "inner morality of law"):**

1.  **Generality** – rules, not ad hoc decisions.

2.  **Promulgation** – laws must be publicised.

3.  **Non-retroactivity** – prospective laws.

4.  **Clarity** – laws must be understandable.

5.  **Non-contradiction** – no conflicting rules.

6.  **Possibility of compliance** – no demand for the impossible.

7.  **Constancy through time** – no frequent changes.

8.  **Congruence** between declared rules and official action.

Fuller's eight failures are illustrated through the story of **King Rex**. Fuller argues these are a procedural natural law ("inner morality"), distinct from the "external morality" of law (the content). The **Hart–Fuller debate** (1958) concerned whether immoral Nazi-era laws are law at all.

**Joseph Raz – "The Rule of Law and its Virtue" (1977):** adopts a formal, positivist approach. Law must be capable of guiding conduct: prospective, open and clear; relatively stable; made under open, stable, clear and general rules; independence of the judiciary; principles of natural justice; courts have review power; courts easily accessible; discretion of crime-preventing agencies should not pervert the law. Raz said that the rule of law is **"a virtue of law, not a moral virtue in itself"** and compared it to the sharpness of a knife – it makes law efficient, whether for good or ill; it does not guarantee a just law. He believed rule of law should not be conflated with democracy, justice, equality or human rights.

**F.A. Hayek – *The Road to Serfdom* (1944) and *The Constitution of Liberty* (1960):** rule of law means that the government in all its actions is bound by rules fixed and announced beforehand, so that one can foresee how the authority will use its coercive powers. Strongly opposed to the planning state and discretionary powers; a formal approach linked to economic liberalism.

**Other formal approaches:** **Albert Venn Dicey** (largely formal), **John Rawls** (a rule-of-law account in *A Theory of Justice* as part of formal justice), **Jeremy Waldron** (procedural features, such as hearings and reasoned decisions).

**Advantages of formal conceptions:** clarity, neutrality, wide acceptance, and a minimal, objective standard for all legal systems.

**Criticism:** a tyranny could comply (the rule of law in a "thin" sense can coexist with oppressive laws, like apartheid legislation).

### 4.4 Substantive (Thick) Conceptions of the Rule of Law

Substantive theories include **moral and political values** – especially fundamental rights, democracy, equality and dignity – within the rule of law itself. A law that violates fundamental rights fails the rule of law.

- **Ronald Dworkin** (*A Matter of Principle*, 1985): distinguishes the **"rule-book" conception** (formal: legal rules published, citizens subject to them) from the **"rights" conception** (substantive: the rule of law presupposes moral rights of citizens against the state, enforceable in courts). He argued that the rights conception requires judges to interpret law in the light of political morality.

- **T.R.S. Allan** (*Law, Liberty and Justice*; *Constitutional Justice*): the rule of law is bound up with equality, fairness and dignity; legal reasoning embodies a common-law constitutionalism.

- **Lord Bingham** (*The Rule of Law*, 2010): offered eight sub-rules: (1) the law must be accessible, intelligible, clear and predictable; (2) legal rights should be resolved by law, not discretion; (3) equality before the law; (4) powers must be exercised lawfully, fairly and for the purpose conferred; (5) protection of fundamental human rights; (6) means for resolving disputes without prohibitive cost; (7) fair trial; (8) compliance with obligations in international law.

- **Declaration of Delhi (1959), International Commission of Jurists (ICJ)** – a dynamic and substantive concept: the rule of law should be used to safeguard and advance the civil and political rights of the individual and to establish social, economic, educational and cultural conditions in which the individual's legitimate aspirations and dignity may be realised. It emphasised an independent judiciary and legal profession. This is a classic exam question as it is linked to India and to the **Delhi Congress of Jurists, 1959**.

- **Paul Craig** ("Formal and Substantive Conceptions of the Rule of Law", 1997) provided the now-standard analytical distinction. **Brian Tamanaha** (*On the Rule of Law*, 2004) proposed a spectrum from **thin** (rule by law, formal legality) through **thicker** (democracy and individual rights) to **thickest** (social welfare and substantive equality).

**Criticisms of substantive conceptions:** they merge the rule of law with a general theory of justice, making it vague and contested; any good political value could be said to be part of it.

### 4.6 Rule of Law in India, UK and USA

**India:** The Preamble, **Article 14** (equality before law and equal protection – the Supreme Court in **E.P. Royappa v State of Tamil Nadu (1974)** treated arbitrariness as antithetical to equality), **Article 21** (due process after **Maneka Gandhi v Union of India, 1978**), Article 32 and 226 (judicial review), and Article 13 incorporate the rule of law in a substantive manner. **Kesavananda Bharati (1973)** and **Indira Nehru Gandhi v Raj Narain (1975)** held the rule of law to be part of the basic structure. **ADM Jabalpur v Shivkant Shukla (1976)** (habeas corpus case during the Emergency) represented a failure of the rule of law and was expressly overruled by the nine-judge bench in **K.S. Puttaswamy v Union of India (2017).** **Rule of law as a reasoned and fair procedure**: *Maneka Gandhi*, *Olga Tellis*, *Vishaka*. Indian courts follow a substantive version of the rule of law.

**UK:** Rule of law is an unwritten constitutional principle. **Constitutional Reform Act 2005, section 1** says the Act does not adversely affect "the existing constitutional principle of the rule of law". **R (Jackson) v Attorney General (2005)** – Lord Steyn and Lord Hope's obiter remarks on whether the rule of law is the ultimate controlling factor over parliamentary sovereignty. **R (Miller) v Prime Minister (2019)** also upheld constitutional principles.

**USA:** The rule of law is embodied in the **Due Process Clauses (Fifth and Fourteenth Amendments)**, equal protection, judicial review (*Marbury v Madison*, 1803), and the principle of a "government of laws and not of men" (**John Adams**, Massachusetts Constitution of 1780). American constitutionalism treats the rule of law as having both formal and substantive aspects (substantive due process).

## Chapter 5: Separation of Powers: India, UK, USA and France

### 5.1 Concept and Theoretical Background

The doctrine of separation of powers holds that the three functions of government – **legislative, executive and judicial** – should be exercised by separate organs, so that no single body holds all the power, thereby preventing tyranny and protecting liberty.

Three aspects (M.J.C. Vile, *Constitutionalism and the Separation of Powers*, 1967): **separation of functions**, **separation of personnel**, and **checks and balances**. Vile described the doctrine as a "pure doctrine" and noted that no system implements it fully.

**Evolution of the doctrine:**

- **Aristotle:** three elements of a constitution – the deliberative, the magisterial (executive) and the judicial.

- **John Locke** (*Two Treatises of Government*, 1690): legislative, executive and federative (foreign affairs) powers; judiciary was not separately recognised; supremacy of the legislature.

- **Montesquieu** (*The Spirit of the Laws*, 1748), based on his study of the English constitution: legislative, executive and judicial powers. "When the legislative and executive powers are united in the same person, or in the same body of magistrates, there can be no liberty." Also, there is no liberty if the judicial power is not separated from the legislative and the executive. Montesquieu misread the English system, which then had a fusion rather than strict separation.

- **William Blackstone** (*Commentaries on the Laws of England*): linked separation to liberty.

- **James Madison** (*Federalist Papers* Nos. 47 and 51): The accumulation of all powers in the same hands "may justly be pronounced the very definition of tyranny"; he explained that the Constitution has **partial agency**, not complete separation, with checks and balances ("ambition must be made to counteract ambition").

- **Ivor Jennings:** distinguished the **pure doctrine** from the **functional** view and observed that in the UK there is no strict separation.

- **Contemporary views:** Bruce Ackerman's critique of the American separation of powers ("The New Separation of Powers", 2000) advocated a parliamentary model with independent "integrity branch" and "distributive branch"; Eoin Carolan and Jeremy Waldron have analysed the doctrine in a modern sense.

**Related concepts:**

- **Checks and balances:** the mechanisms by which each branch limits the others (veto, judicial review, impeachment, confirmation).

- **Fusion of powers:** typical of parliamentary systems.

- **Delegated legislation and administrative adjudication:** common challenges to strict separation.

- **Independence of the judiciary** is the most universally accepted core of the doctrine.

### 5.2 United States

The American model is the closest to the textbook doctrine, but is better described as **"separated institutions sharing powers"** (Richard Neustadt). Article I vests legislative power in Congress, Article II executive power in the President, and Article III judicial power in the Supreme Court and lower federal courts.

**Separation of personnel:** the Incompatibility Clause (Article I, Section 6) – no person holding an office under the United States may be a member of Congress.

**Checks and balances (examples):**

- President's **veto** over legislation (overridden by two-thirds of both Houses); Senate's **advice and consent** to appointments and treaties; **impeachment** by the House and trial by the Senate; Congress's control over appropriations; **judicial review** (*Marbury v Madison*, 1803) over both Congress and the President; presidential nomination of federal judges (with Senate confirmation) and life tenure of judges during good behaviour.

**Landmark cases:**

- **Marbury v Madison (1803):** judicial review of legislation established.

- **Myers v United States (1926):** President's removal power over executive officers.

- **Humphrey's Executor v United States (1935):** Congress may limit presidential removal of members of independent regulatory commissions.

- **Panama Refining Co. v Ryan and A.L.A. Schechter Poultry Corp v United States (1935):** applied the **non-delegation doctrine** (invalidating excessive delegation of legislative power).

- **Youngstown Sheet & Tube Co. v Sawyer (1952):** the steel seizure case – President Truman could not seize steel mills without congressional authority; **Justice Jackson's three-category concurrence** on presidential power (maximum authority when acting with Congress's authorisation; a "zone of twilight" when Congress is silent; lowest ebb when acting against Congress's will).

- **United States v Nixon (1974):** executive privilege is not absolute; the President must comply with a judicial subpoena in a criminal trial.

- **Morrison v Olson (1988):** upheld the independent counsel statute.

- **INS v Chadha (1983):** a one-House legislative veto is unconstitutional (violates bicameralism and presentment).

- **Clinton v City of New York (1998):** the Line Item Veto Act was held unconstitutional.

- **Mistretta v United States (1989):** upheld the Sentencing Commission and delegation to it (the "intelligible principle" test).

- **Recent developments:** **Loper Bright Enterprises v Raimondo (2024)** overruled the *Chevron* deference doctrine, shifting interpretive authority from agencies to courts; **Trump v United States (2024)** addressed presidential immunity from criminal prosecution for official acts.

### 5.3 United Kingdom

The UK has a **fusion of powers**, not a strict separation. The doctrine operates mainly as a principle of **judicial independence** and mutual restraint.

- **Executive and legislature:** The Prime Minister and ministers must be members of Parliament; the Cabinet is drawn from and responsible to the House of Commons. This is the core of Bagehot's "close union, the nearly complete fusion" of executive and legislature.

- **Judiciary and legislature:** historically, the **House of Lords** was both the upper chamber of the legislature and the highest court (through the Law Lords). Under the **Constitutional Reform Act, 2005**, a **Supreme Court of the United Kingdom** was created and began functioning in **October 2009**, separating the final court of appeal from Parliament.

- **Lord Chancellor:** formerly head of the judiciary, a Cabinet minister and Speaker of the House of Lords (all three roles). The 2005 Act changed this: the Lord Chief Justice became head of the judiciary of England and Wales; the Lord Chancellor's role was reformed, and a Speaker of the House of Lords was introduced. A **Judicial Appointments Commission** now handles appointments.

- **Judicial independence:** guaranteed by the **Act of Settlement, 1701** (security of tenure for judges during good behaviour) and statutory protections; judges can be removed only by an address of both Houses.

- **Parliamentary sovereignty** (Dicey) means Parliament can make or unmake any law; courts do not review the validity of Acts of Parliament (though they may issue declarations of incompatibility under the **Human Rights Act, 1998, s 4**).

- **Cases:**

  - **Duport Steels v Sirs (1980)** – Lord Diplock: "Parliament makes laws, judges interpret them"; the constitutional principle of separation of powers is "a cardinal feature of the British constitution".

  - **M v Home Office (1994)** – courts can hold a minister in contempt.

  - **R v Secretary of State for the Home Department, ex p Fire Brigades Union (1995)** – the executive cannot frustrate Parliament's statutory intention using prerogative powers.

  - **R (Miller) v Secretary of State for Exiting the EU (2017)** – the government cannot trigger Article 50 using the royal prerogative without parliamentary authorisation.

  - **R (Miller) v The Prime Minister; Cherry v Advocate General (2019)** – the advice to prorogue Parliament was unlawful, as it frustrated Parliament's constitutional function.

  - **Anisminic v Foreign Compensation Commission (1969)** – courts will not accept an ouster clause that stops review of errors of law.

- **Delegated legislation:** large in volume; Henry VIII clauses and the **Statutory Instruments Act 1946** exist; the Donoughmore Committee (1932) examined the powers of ministers.

### 5.4 France

France is the home of Montesquieu, but its model of separation is distinctive: the traditional emphasis is on the **separation of the administration from the ordinary judiciary**, not on separation between judiciary and the legislature.

- **Declaration of the Rights of Man and of the Citizen (1789), Article 16:** "A society in which the guarantee of rights is not assured, nor the separation of powers determined, has no constitution at all."

- **Law of 16–24 August 1790:** prohibits ordinary judges from interfering with the operations of the administration. The result is a **dual court system:** ordinary (judicial) courts headed by the **Court of Cassation** and administrative courts headed by the **Conseil d'État**, with the **Tribunal des conflits** deciding jurisdictional disputes. This system is called **droit administratif**.

- **Historic distrust of judges:** because of the pre-Revolutionary *parlements*; Édouard Lambert's *Le gouvernement des juges* (1921) criticised judicial power; judges were long limited to applying law rather than reviewing it.

- **Fifth Republic (1958):** a strong executive with a "rationalised parliamentarism". **Article 34** lists areas in which Parliament legislates (the *domaine de la loi*), and **Article 37** gives the residual field to executive regulation (*pouvoir réglementaire autonome*) – a dramatic reversal of parliamentary supremacy. **Article 16** gives the President emergency powers; **Article 49(3)** allows the Government to pass a bill without a vote unless a motion of censure is passed; the executive can legislate by **ordinances** (Article 38) with parliamentary authorisation; the President can dissolve the National Assembly (Article 12).

- **Constitutional Council (Conseil constitutionnel):** reviews the constitutionality of laws; its **1971 *Freedom of Association* decision** gave constitutional force to the Preamble (rights); the **2008 reform** introduced **QPC (question prioritaire de constitutionnalité)**, an *ex post* review through a priority question raised in litigation.

- **Judicial independence:** guaranteed by the President acting with the **Conseil supérieur de la magistrature**.

- **Features:** President and Prime Minister (semi-presidential); ministers are not allowed to simultaneously serve as members of Parliament (Article 23) – a stronger separation of personnel than in the UK.

### 5.5 India

The Indian Constitution does not expressly adopt the doctrine, but a **functional separation** exists with checks and balances, and the Supreme Court treats the doctrine as part of the **basic structure.**

- **Textual indications:** Article 50 (Directive Principle – State to separate the judiciary from the executive in public services); Articles 53 and 73 (executive power); Article 79 (Parliament); Article 121 and 211 (no discussion in Parliament or legislatures on the conduct of judges); Article 122 and 212 (courts cannot inquire into parliamentary proceedings); Article 124 and 217 (independent judges, removal by a special procedure); Article 361 (immunity of the President and Governors).

- **Fusion in a parliamentary system:** ministers are members of the legislature (Article 75, 164) – as in the UK.

- **Overlaps and checks:** ordinance-making power of the President/Governor (Articles 123, 213); delegated legislation; tribunals; legislative power to validate invalid laws (but not to overrule judicial decisions directly); impeachment of the President (Article 61) and removal of judges (Article 124(4)); judicial review of legislative and executive action (Articles 13, 32, 226); the Rajya Sabha and the Lok Sabha controlling executive through questions, no-confidence motions and committees.

**Leading cases:**

- **In re Delhi Laws Act (1951):** a legislature cannot delegate its **essential legislative functions**; permissible delegation is limited to ancillary functions.

- **Ram Jawaya Kapur v State of Punjab (1955):** Chief Justice **Mukherjea** held that the Indian Constitution has not recognised the doctrine of separation of powers in its absolute rigidity, but the functions of the different parts or branches of government have been sufficiently differentiated. Hence, the executive cannot assume the functions of other organs essentially belonging to them (a classic exam citation).

- **Golaknath v State of Punjab (1967):** and **Kesavananda Bharati v State of Kerala (1973):** the separation of powers is a basic feature.

- **Indira Nehru Gandhi v Raj Narain (1975):** the Thirty-ninth Amendment (taking the election of the Prime Minister beyond judicial scrutiny) was struck down as violating the separation of powers, as it amounted to the exercise of a judicial function by the legislature.

- **Minerva Mills v Union of India (1980):** judicial review and balance between Fundamental Rights and DPSPs form the basic structure.

- **Special Reference No. 1 of 1964 (Keshav Singh case / U.P. Legislative Assembly case):** reconciled legislative privileges and the powers of the High Courts.

- **State of Bihar v Bal Mukund Sah (2000):** the Court said that the doctrine is a basic feature, and that a judicial function cannot be assigned to non-judicial authorities.

- **L. Chandra Kumar v Union of India (1997):** tribunals' decisions are subject to High Court scrutiny; judicial review is a basic structure.

- **Supreme Court Advocates-on-Record Association v Union of India (2015):** struck down the 99th Amendment and the **National Judicial Appointments Commission (NJAC)** Act as violating judicial independence and the basic structure.

- **Raja Ram Pal v Lok Sabha (2007):** limited judicial review of the expulsion of members of Parliament in the "cash for query" case.

- **Madras Bar Association cases and the Tribunals Reforms Act, 2021:** the Court has repeatedly struck down provisions that reduce the independence of tribunals.

## Chapter 6: Independence of Judiciary, Judicial Activism and Accountability: India, UK and USA

### 6.1 Independence of the Judiciary: Concept

Judicial independence means that judges can decide cases impartially, free from pressure by the executive, legislature, litigants, media or their own colleagues. It has two dimensions:

- **Institutional independence:** the judiciary as a branch is protected from interference (control over budget, administration, appointments).

- **Individual (decisional) independence:** each judge is free to decide without fear of removal, reduction of salary or career penalty.

Conventional safeguards: **mode of appointment**, **security of tenure**, **fixed and protected salary**, **removal only through a special procedure**, **prohibition on post-retirement practice or employment** (in some systems), **immunity** for judicial acts, **power to punish for contempt**, and **separation from the executive** (Article 50 in India).

International standards: **UN Basic Principles on the Independence of the Judiciary (1985)**; **Bangalore Principles of Judicial Conduct (2002)** (independence, impartiality, integrity, propriety, equality, competence and diligence); the **Beijing Statement (1995)** for the Asia-Pacific region; the **Latimer House Principles** (Commonwealth).

Independence is accompanied by **accountability** – in an open system, judges must be answerable through reasoned judgments, open courts, appeals, impeachment, codes of conduct, and public scrutiny. The tension between independence and accountability is the theme of this topic.

### 6.2 India

#### Constitutional Safeguards

- **Appointment:** Article 124(2) – Supreme Court judges are appointed by the President after consultation with such judges of the Supreme Court and High Courts as deemed necessary; the Chief Justice is always consulted (except for the Chief Justice himself). Article 217 for High Courts (consultation with the Chief Justice of India, the Governor and the Chief Justice of the High Court).

- **Tenure:** retirement at **65** (Supreme Court) and **62** (High Court). Article 124(4) and (5): removal only by an order of the President after an **address by each House of Parliament supported by a majority of the total membership of that House and by at least two-thirds of the members present and voting**, on the ground of **proved misbehaviour or incapacity**; procedure regulated by the **Judges (Inquiry) Act, 1968.**

- **Salary:** determined by Parliament (Article 125); allowances, pension and rights cannot be varied to the disadvantage of a judge after appointment (financial emergency exception). Salaries are charged on the **Consolidated Fund of India** (non-votable).

- **Conduct of judges** cannot be discussed in Parliament except on a motion for removal (Article 121); the Supreme Court has the power to punish for its contempt (Article 129); the Court controls its own staff and procedure (Articles 145 and 146).

- **Post-retirement bar:** a retired Supreme Court judge cannot plead or act before any court or authority in India (Article 124(7)); retired High Court judges cannot practise in the court where they held office (Article 220).

- **Article 50 (DPSP):** separation of the judiciary from the executive in the public services.

- **Articles 32, 226, 227, 136, 142, 143:** wide powers, including the power to do complete justice (Article 142).

- **Judicial review and independence of the judiciary** are part of the **basic structure** (Kesavananda; Minerva Mills; L. Chandra Kumar).

#### Judicial Accountability in India

- **Removal by impeachment:** the procedure under the Judges (Inquiry) Act, 1968: a motion signed by 100 Lok Sabha members or 50 Rajya Sabha members; the presiding officer constitutes a three-member committee (a Supreme Court judge, a High Court Chief Justice, and a distinguished jurist). No judge has been successfully removed so far.

  - **Justice V. Ramaswami (1991–93):** first judge subjected to impeachment proceedings; the motion failed in the Lok Sabha as Congress members abstained.

  - **Justice Soumitra Sen (2011):** the Rajya Sabha passed the motion; he resigned before the Lok Sabha could vote.

  - **Justice P.D. Dinakaran (2011):** resigned before the inquiry concluded.

  - In 2018, notice of a motion for removal of **CJI Dipak Misra** was rejected by the Chairman of the Rajya Sabha.

- **K. Veeraswami v Union of India (1991):** judges of the higher judiciary are "public servants" under the Prevention of Corruption Act, but no criminal case can be registered against a High Court or Supreme Court judge without prior consultation with the Chief Justice of India.

- **C. Ravichandran Iyer v Justice A.M. Bhattacharjee (1995):** the Court emphasised that judicial dignity must be maintained and recognised the need for an in-house procedure.

- **In-house procedure (1999):** adopted by the Supreme Court for dealing with complaints against judges. **Restatement of Values of Judicial Life (1997)** – a code of conduct adopted by the Full Court. A 1997 resolution requires judges to declare their assets.

- **Contempt of Courts Act, 1971:** protects the administration of justice; criticised as contrary to freedom of expression (cf. **In Re Prashant Bhushan (2020)**); **Articles 129 and 215** give constitutional status to contempt jurisdiction.

- **Right to Information:** **Central Public Information Officer, Supreme Court of India v Subhash Chandra Agarwal (2019)** – the office of the Chief Justice of India is a "public authority" under the RTI Act, though information about collegium deliberations may be withheld under exemptions subject to a public-interest test.

- **Judicial Standards and Accountability Bill, 2010** (lapsed) proposed binding standards and a complaints mechanism.

- Other accountability tools: reasoned judgments, appeals, review and curative petitions (**Rupa Ashok Hurra v Ashok Hurra, 2002**), and public scrutiny.

### 6.3 Judicial Activism

**Meaning:** A judicial philosophy in which judges go beyond the traditional role of interpreting law, take an active role in promoting social justice and rights, correct failures of other branches, and sometimes create new law or policy. The term was coined by **Arthur Schlesinger Jr.** in a 1947 *Fortune* magazine article on the US Supreme Court. Opposite concept: **judicial restraint** (deference to the political branches). Related concept: **judicial overreach**, where the court is accused of violating separation of powers.

#### Indian Judicial Activism: Phases and Tools

- **Early period (1950–70s):** a restrictive and positivist approach (**A.K. Gopalan v State of Madras, 1950**; **ADM Jabalpur, 1976**).

- **Expansion after 1978 (post-Emergency):** **Maneka Gandhi v Union of India (1978)** (Articles 14, 19 and 21 are interrelated; procedure must be just, fair and reasonable). Justices **V.R. Krishna Iyer, P.N. Bhagwati, O. Chinnappa Reddy and D.A. Desai** led this approach.

- **Public Interest Litigation (PIL)/Social Action Litigation:** relaxed **locus standi**; **epistolary jurisdiction** (treating letters as petitions); **S.P. Gupta (1981)** recognised standing for public-spirited persons; **Hussainara Khatoon v State of Bihar (1979)** – speedy trial and legal aid for undertrials; **Bandhua Mukti Morcha v Union of India (1984)** – bonded labour; **M.C. Mehta v Union of India** (environment; absolute liability in the *Oleum Gas leak case*, 1986); **Olga Tellis v Bombay Municipal Corporation (1985)** – right to livelihood; **Sunil Batra v Delhi Administration (1978)** – prisoners' rights; **Vishaka v State of Rajasthan (1997)** – guidelines against sexual harassment at work (leading to the 2013 Act); **D.K. Basu v State of West Bengal (1997)** – guidelines on arrest and custody; **Lalita Kumari v Government of Uttar Pradesh (2013)** – mandatory registration of FIR for cognisable offences; **Common Cause v Union of India (2018)** – passive euthanasia and living wills; **Navtej Singh Johar (2018)** – decriminalising consensual same-sex acts.

- **Expansion of Article 21:** right to life includes dignity, livelihood, health, education (**Unni Krishnan, 1993**), clean environment, privacy (**Puttaswamy, 2017**), speedy trial, free legal aid, and many others.

- **Tools:** the doctrine of the basic structure; continuing mandamus; appointment of commissions; use of Article 142 to do "complete justice"; judicial legislation through guidelines.

- **Criticisms:** judicial overreach (policy-making without expertise or accountability), "judicial populism", encroachment on legislative and executive domain, and inconsistent standards. Supporters argue that activism filled a vacuum left by a weak executive and legislature.

- **Judicial restraint:** the Court has also recognised limits – e.g., **Divisional Manager, Aravali Golf Club v Chander Hass (2008)** (judges must show restraint and not usurp power).

### 6.4 United Kingdom

#### Independence

- **Act of Settlement, 1701:** judges hold office during good behaviour (*quamdiu se bene gesserint*) and can be removed only by an address of both Houses of Parliament. This is the historical foundation of security of tenure.

- **Constitutional Reform Act 2005 (CRA):** **section 3** imposes a statutory duty on the Lord Chancellor, ministers and others to uphold continued judicial independence, and bars ministers from seeking to influence judicial decisions through special access; **section 1** affirms the rule of law; created the **UK Supreme Court (functioning October 2009)**; the **Lord Chief Justice** became head of the judiciary in England and Wales; the **Judicial Appointments Commission** (2006) recommends candidates on merit; the Lord Chancellor's role is limited; the **Judicial Appointments and Conduct Ombudsman** handles complaints.

- **Retirement:** the mandatory retirement age for judges has been 70, raised to 75 for most judicial offices by recent legislation (the Public Service Pensions and Judicial Offices Act, 2022).

- **Sub judice convention:** matters before courts are not discussed in Parliament; judges are not criticised except on a substantive motion.

#### Accountability

- **Removal:** a superior court judge can be removed only by the Crown on an address by both Houses (Senior Courts Act, 1981, s 11(3); CRA 2005 for Supreme Court justices). It is virtually never used.

- **Judicial Conduct Investigations Office (JCIO)** (since 2013) investigates complaints about personal conduct (not judicial decisions); disciplinary sanctions are imposed by the Lord Chancellor with the Lord Chief Justice.

- **Appeals** and **reasoned public judgments** are the main routes for correcting decisions; **open justice** and media reporting.

#### Judicial Activism in the UK

The UK's tradition of parliamentary sovereignty makes overt activism rare, but the following are considered activist trends:

- **Lord Denning (Master of the Rolls, 1962–82):** a creative, justice-oriented judge (promissory estoppel in *Central London Property Trust v High Trees*, 1947; the Mareva injunction).

- **Lord Reid, "The Judge as Law Maker" (1972):** rejected the "fairy tale" that judges merely find, not make, law.

- **Growth of judicial review:** *Ridge v Baldwin* (1964); *Anisminic v Foreign Compensation Commission* (1969); *GCHQ (Council of Civil Service Unions v Minister for the Civil Service, 1985)* where **Lord Diplock** classified grounds as **illegality, irrationality and procedural impropriety.**

- **Human Rights Act, 1998:** courts interpret legislation compatibly (section 3) and issue declarations of incompatibility (section 4) without invalidating laws.

- **Constitutional principle cases:** **Factortame (1990)** (disapplied an Act of Parliament in conflict with EU law); **R (Jackson) v AG (2005)**; **R (Miller) 2017 and 2019**; **R (UNISON) v Lord Chancellor (2017)** (access to justice as a constitutional right).

- **Criticism:** concerns of judicial overreach (e.g., Miller) fed the **Judicial Review and Courts Act 2022** (limiting some remedies). The 2016 "Enemies of the People" headline about judges after the first Miller decision illustrated political pressure.

### 6.5 United States

#### Independence

- **Article III:** judges of the Supreme Court and inferior courts hold office **during good behaviour** and receive compensation that cannot be diminished during their continuance in office.

- **Appointment:** nominated by the President with the **advice and consent of the Senate** (Article II, Section 2) – highly political.

- **Removal:** only by **impeachment** (House) and conviction (Senate by two-thirds) for "treason, bribery, or other high crimes and misdemeanors" (Article II, Section 4). **Justice Samuel Chase** was impeached in 1804 and acquitted in 1805 – a key precedent for judicial independence. No Supreme Court justice has been removed.

- **Size of the Court:** fixed by Congress (nine since 1869). **Franklin D. Roosevelt's Court-packing plan (1937)** failed but is remembered as "the switch in time that saved nine".

- **State judges:** in many States, judges are elected or face retention elections (more accountable but potentially less independent).

- **Ethics:** the federal judiciary follows the **Code of Conduct for United States Judges**; the Supreme Court adopted its first **Code of Conduct in November 2023** (with no formal enforcement mechanism).

#### Judicial Review and Judicial Activism

- **Marbury v Madison (1803)** established judicial review; **Martin v Hunter's Lessee (1816)** and **Cooper v Aaron (1958)** support the Supreme Court's role as the final interpreter of the Constitution.

- **Schlesinger (1947)** identified judicial "activists" and "champions of self-restraint" on the Court.

- **Lochner era (1905–1937):** *Lochner v New York* – the Court struck down social and economic legislation using substantive due process (**conservative activism**); ended with *West Coast Hotel v Parrish* (1937).

- **Warren Court (1953–69), Chief Justice Earl Warren (liberal activism):** **Brown v Board of Education (1954)**; **Mapp v Ohio (1961)** (exclusionary rule applied to States); **Baker v Carr (1962)** (reapportionment is justiciable – "one person, one vote"); **Gideon v Wainwright (1963)** (right to counsel); **Griswold v Connecticut (1965)** (privacy); **Miranda v Arizona (1966)** (warnings).

- **Later developments:** **Roe v Wade (1973)** recognised abortion rights; it was overruled in **Dobbs v Jackson Women's Health Organization (2022)**. **Bush v Gore (2000)** and **Citizens United v FEC (2010)** are also controversial.

- **Judicial restraint:** associated with **Justices Holmes, Frankfurter and Learned Hand**; **originalism/textualism** (Antonin Scalia) is presented as restraint though critics see selective activism. **Alexander Bickel**, *The Least Dangerous Branch* (1962), articulated the **counter-majoritarian difficulty**.

- **Self-limiting doctrines:** **standing, ripeness, mootness, political question doctrine** (*Baker v Carr*; *Nixon v United States*, 1993), and the **Ashwander** rules of constitutional avoidance (Justice Brandeis).

## Chapter 7: Systems of Constitutional Review: India, USA, Switzerland and France

### 7.1 Concept and Models

**Constitutional review** (including **judicial review**) is the power of a court or other body to examine whether laws, executive acts or sometimes constitutional amendments conform to the Constitution, and to invalidate or refuse to apply those that do not. It is the mechanism that makes a constitution **supreme**.

**Classification of systems:**

1.  **American (decentralised/diffuse) model:** every court can examine constitutionality during an ordinary case (concrete, *a posteriori*); the highest court has the final say; the court refuses to apply the unconstitutional law. Countries: USA, India (largely), Canada, Australia, Japan.

2.  **European/Kelsenian (centralised) model:** a **special constitutional court** has exclusive jurisdiction; designed by **Hans Kelsen** for the **Austrian Constitution of 1920**; decisions have *erga omnes* effect (annulment). Countries: Germany (Federal Constitutional Court, 1951), Italy, Spain, South Africa.

3.  **Political/preventive model:** a political-legal body reviews laws **before** promulgation – **France** (Conseil constitutionnel) is the classic example.

4.  **Parliamentary supremacy with no review of statutes:** traditionally the UK, and partly **Switzerland** for federal laws.

5.  **Weak-form/"new Commonwealth model"** (Stephen Gardbaum): courts review rights but the legislature has the final word (Canada's notwithstanding clause; UK's Human Rights Act; New Zealand).

**Other dimensions:**

- **Abstract review** (without a concrete case, at the request of officials) vs **concrete review** (arising in a real dispute).

- **A priori** (before a law comes into force) vs **a posteriori** (after).

- **Incidental** (raised in a case) vs **principal** (direct challenge).

- **Strong-form** (final judicial word) vs **weak-form** (legislature can respond).

- **Counter-majoritarian difficulty** (Bickel) – the core theoretical objection: unelected judges overturning the will of elected bodies. Defence: protection of rights and constitutional supremacy (**Hamilton, Federalist No. 78** – the judiciary is the "least dangerous" branch, having neither force nor will, but merely judgment).

### 7.2 United States

- **Source:** not expressly provided in the text; derived from Article III (judicial power), Article VI (supremacy clause) and **Marbury v Madison (1803)** – Chief Justice John Marshall: it is emphatically the province and duty of the judicial department to say what the law is; an Act repugnant to the Constitution is void. The Court held that part of **Section 13 of the Judiciary Act of 1789** was unconstitutional because it expanded the Court's original jurisdiction beyond Article III.

- **Diffuse, concrete, *a posteriori* review:** any court can review; the Supreme Court is the final authority; **Article III requires "cases or controversies"**; the Court does not give **advisory opinions** (a tradition from the 1793 *Correspondence of the Justices* and *Hayburn's Case*).

- **Review of state laws and decisions:** **Fletcher v Peck (1810)** (first state law struck down); **Martin v Hunter's Lessee (1816)**; **Cohens v Virginia (1821)**. A federal law was struck down for the first time in *Marbury*; the second time in **Dred Scott v Sandford (1857).**

- **Cooper v Aaron (1958):** judicial supremacy asserted.

- **Justiciability doctrines:** standing (**Lujan v Defenders of Wildlife, 1992**), ripeness, mootness, and the **political question doctrine** (*Luther v Borden*, 1849; *Baker v Carr*, 1962; *Nixon v United States*, 1993).

- **Levels of scrutiny:** **rational basis, intermediate scrutiny, strict scrutiny** (footnote 4 of **United States v Carolene Products, 1938**).

- **Certiorari:** the Court chooses most of its cases (the "rule of four").

- **Review of constitutional amendments:** generally the Court does not substantively strike down amendments (**National Prohibition Cases, 1920**); there is no "basic structure" doctrine.

- **Strong-form review** with the Supreme Court as the ultimate interpreter; constitutional amendment (Article V) or changes in the Court's composition are the main political responses.

### 7.3 India

- **Source:** express constitutional provisions – **Article 13** (laws inconsistent with or in derogation of fundamental rights are void), **Article 32** (right to constitutional remedies – itself a fundamental right; Dr. Ambedkar called it the "heart and soul" of the Constitution), **Article 226** (High Courts – wider, extends to "any other purpose"), **Article 131** (original jurisdiction in Centre–State disputes), **Articles 132–136** (appeals, special leave), **Article 143** (advisory jurisdiction – President's reference), **Article 246 and the Seventh Schedule** (distribution of powers), **Article 254** (repugnancy), **Article 368** (amendment).

- **Nature:** a hybrid – diffuse like the USA but with the Supreme Court at the apex, **direct constitutional jurisdiction** (Articles 32 and 226) and an **advisory jurisdiction** (Article 143). It has no separate constitutional court.

- **Judicial review of ordinary legislation:** grounds – violation of fundamental rights, lack of legislative competence, violation of other constitutional provisions; **presumption of constitutionality** (*Charanjit Lal Chowdhury v Union of India*, 1951); **proportionality** and the "arbitrariness" doctrine (**Shayara Bano, 2017**).

- **Doctrines:** **pith and substance; colourable legislation; severability** (**R.M.D. Chamarbaugwala v Union of India, 1957**); **doctrine of eclipse** (**Bhikaji Narain Dhakras v State of Madhya Pradesh, 1955**: a pre-Constitution law inconsistent with fundamental rights is not dead but "eclipsed", and revives if the inconsistency is removed); **doctrine of waiver** (**Behram Khurshed Pesikaka v State of Bombay, 1955**: fundamental rights cannot be waived); **prospective overruling** (**Golaknath, 1967**); **territorial nexus**; **harmonious construction**; **reading down.**

- **Review of constitutional amendments (basic structure doctrine):**

  - **Shankari Prasad v Union of India (1951)** and **Sajjan Singh v State of Rajasthan (1965):** Parliament can amend any part, including fundamental rights; an amendment is not "law" under Article 13(2).

  - **I.C. Golaknath v State of Punjab (1967):** Parliament cannot amend fundamental rights (an amendment is "law"); prospective overruling used.

  - **24th Amendment (1971):** added Article 13(4) and amended Article 368, declaring that Article 13 does not apply to amendments.

  - **Kesavananda Bharati v State of Kerala (1973):** a 13-judge bench by 7:6 held that Parliament can amend any part of the Constitution but cannot alter its **basic structure** (an idea associated with the German scholar **Dietrich Conrad**'s lectures in India). Basic features include supremacy of the Constitution, rule of law, separation of powers, judicial review, federalism, secularism, democracy, free and fair elections and independence of the judiciary.

  - **Indira Nehru Gandhi v Raj Narain (1975):** the 39th Amendment invalidated.

  - **Minerva Mills v Union of India (1980):** struck down parts of the 42nd Amendment (Article 368(4) and (5)); harmony between Fundamental Rights and Directive Principles is part of the basic structure; limited amending power is itself a basic feature.

  - **Waman Rao v Union of India (1981):** Ninth Schedule laws placed before 24 April 1973 are immune; those after can be reviewed.

  - **I.R. Coelho v State of Tamil Nadu (2007):** Ninth Schedule laws enacted after 24 April 1973 are open to challenge if they violate fundamental rights or the basic structure.

  - **L. Chandra Kumar (1997):** judicial review by the High Courts and Supreme Court is part of the basic structure.

  - **Supreme Court Advocates-on-Record Association (2015):** 99th Amendment struck down.

- **Review of executive action:** presidential proclamations (S.R. Bommai, 1994), ordinances (**Krishna Kumar Singh v State of Bihar, 2017**), and administrative action generally.

- **Nature:** strong-form review with the Supreme Court as the final authority; criticised at times as overreach.

### 7.4 Switzerland

Switzerland is distinctive: **judicial review is limited** because of its commitment to **popular sovereignty and direct democracy.**

- **Federal Supreme Court (Bundesgericht), Lausanne:** the highest court; the system also includes the Federal Criminal Court (Bellinzona), Federal Administrative Court (St. Gallen) and Federal Patent Court.

- **Cantonal laws:** the Federal Supreme Court **can review cantonal laws and acts** for compatibility with the Federal Constitution, federal law and international law (both **abstract review** directly within a time limit after promulgation, and **concrete/incidental review**). This protects federal supremacy.

- **Federal laws:** under **Article 190 of the Federal Constitution (1999)**, the Federal Supreme Court and other authorities **must apply federal acts and international law.** So **federal statutes cannot be set aside** for being unconstitutional – a result of the principle that the people (through referendum) and Parliament are sovereign. However, courts may interpret federal laws in conformity with the Constitution, and in practice give priority to international human rights law (the **Schubert practice**, 1973, and the **PKK case, 1999**, on the primacy of the European Convention on Human Rights).

- **No separate constitutional court** and no judicial review of federal acts. Instead, democratic controls operate: federal acts are subject to the **optional referendum** (50,000 signatures within 100 days); **constitutional amendments** require a **mandatory referendum** with a **double majority** (majority of the people and the majority of cantons – *Ständerat/Ständemehr*); a **popular initiative** (100,000 signatures within 18 months) can propose a partial revision of the Constitution (Article 139); an initiative can be declared invalid only if it violates unity of form or matter, or mandatory provisions of international law (*jus cogens*).

- **Why limited review?** The democratic tradition of 1848; trust in the referendum as the guardian; federal laws are seen as the people's will. Proposals to introduce constitutional jurisdiction over federal laws (including in the 1999 revision) were rejected.

- **Federal Council** (executive) and **Federal Assembly** (Parliament) carry out *internal* checks (e.g., the Federal Office of Justice advises on constitutionality).

### 7.5 France

France historically rejected judicial review of statutes (law as an expression of the general will – Rousseau; distrust of judges after the Revolution; fear of *gouvernement des juges*). The **Fifth Republic (1958)** created a distinctive model.

- **Conseil constitutionnel (Constitutional Council)** – Title VII, **Articles 56–63** of the 1958 Constitution.

  - **Composition:** **nine members** serving **non-renewable nine-year terms**, one-third renewed every three years. The **President of the Republic, the President of the National Assembly and the President of the Senate** each appoint three. **Former Presidents of the Republic** are *ex officio* members for life (Article 56). Members are often experienced politicians and jurists, not necessarily judges.

  - **A priori (preventive) review:** **before promulgation**; **mandatory** for **organic laws (lois organiques)** and the **standing orders of Parliament** (Article 61(1)); **optional** for ordinary laws – referred by the President of the Republic, the Prime Minister, the Presidents of the two Houses, or (since the **1974 reform**) **60 deputies or 60 senators** (Article 61(2)). Treaties are checked under **Article 54**. The Council normally decides within one month.

  - **A posteriori review – *Question prioritaire de constitutionnalité* (QPC):** introduced by the **Constitutional Law of 23 July 2008** (in force 1 March 2010; **Article 61-1**). A litigant can raise before any court a claim that a statutory provision violates rights and freedoms guaranteed by the Constitution; the **Conseil d'État** or **Cour de cassation** filters and refers it. A provision declared unconstitutional is repealed from the date of publication of the decision or a date fixed by the Council (**Article 62**).

  - **Binding effect:** **Article 62** – an unconstitutional provision cannot be promulgated or implemented; decisions are not subject to appeal and bind all public, administrative and judicial authorities.

  - **Decision of 16 July 1971 (Freedom of Association):** a landmark – the Council struck down part of a statute for the first time and gave constitutional status to the **Preamble of the 1958 Constitution**, thus incorporating the **1789 Declaration of the Rights of Man and the Citizen** and the **Preamble of the 1946 Constitution**, forming the **bloc de constitutionnalité**. After this, the Council became a protector of rights.

  - **Other functions:** supervises presidential elections and referendums, adjudicates disputes on parliamentary elections (Articles 58–60), and polices the division between law and regulation (Articles 37 and 41).

  - **Limits:** the Council **does not review laws adopted by referendum** (Decision of **6 November 1962**) and, in principle, **constitutional amendments** (Decision of **26 March 2003**); it does not check **conventionality** (compatibility of statutes with treaties) – this is done by ordinary and administrative courts (**Decision of 15 January 1975 on abortion; Cour de cassation, Jacques Vabre (1975)** and **Conseil d'État, Nicolo (1989)** under Article 55).

- **Administrative courts:** the **Conseil d'État** reviews the legality of administrative acts (decrees and regulations) – the French dual system (*droit administratif*).

- **Features:** semi-political composition; mainly preventive and abstract; *erga omnes* effect; opposition referrals since 1974 greatly enhanced its role.

## Chapter 8: Amendment of the Constitution: India, USA and South Africa

### 8.1 Concept: Why and How Constitutions Are Amended

A constitution must be stable but also adaptable. **Amendment** is the formal procedure by which the text is changed, and it distinguishes **constituent power** (the power to make or change the constitution) from ordinary legislative power.

- **Rigid constitution:** needs a special procedure for amendment (USA, Switzerland, Australia). **Flexible constitution:** amended by the ordinary legislative process (UK). **Lord Bryce** (*Flexible and Rigid Constitutions*) introduced this classification; **K.C. Wheare** and **Dicey** also discuss it.

- **Mixed/partly rigid and partly flexible:** India (Nehru said that the Constitution should not be so rigid that it cannot adapt; it is neither too rigid nor too flexible).

- Methods of amendment: by the legislature with a special majority; by the legislature plus ratification by units (federations); by referendum (Switzerland, Australia); by a special convention; by judicial interpretation (informal amendment).

- **Entrenchment:** certain provisions are protected by a stricter procedure (e.g., the equal suffrage in the US Senate; sections 1 and 74 of the South African Constitution; basic structure in India).

### 8.2 India

#### Article 368 and Categories of Amendment

Part XX (Article 368) deals with the power of Parliament to amend the Constitution and its procedure. The Constitution (Twenty-fourth Amendment) Act, 1971 amended the heading and text to read "Power of Parliament to amend the Constitution and procedure therefor". An amendment bill can be introduced only in **Parliament** (either House), not in a State legislature, and does not need the President's prior recommendation. There is **no provision for a joint sitting** in the event of a deadlock. The President **must give assent** (24th Amendment).

There are **three categories:**

1.  **Amendment by simple majority of Parliament** (outside Article 368, like ordinary legislation): admission or establishment of new States, and alteration of boundaries (Articles 2, 3 and 4); abolition or creation of Legislative Councils in States (Article 169); the Second Schedule (salaries of constitutional officers); quorum in Parliament; Fifth and Sixth Schedules (administration of Scheduled and Tribal areas); citizenship (Articles 5–11); official language; Union territories; parliamentary privileges; and some others.

2.  **Amendment by special majority:** a majority of the **total membership of each House** *and* a majority of **not less than two-thirds of the members present and voting** in each House. This covers most provisions, including **Fundamental Rights and Directive Principles.**

3.  **Amendment by special majority plus ratification by at least half of the State legislatures** (by simple majority in those legislatures): provisions concerning the **federal structure**, such as the election of the President (Articles 54, 55); the extent of the executive power of the Union and States (Articles 73 and 162); the **Supreme Court and High Courts** (Chapter IV of Part V and Chapter V of Part VI); the Union Territories' High Courts (Article 241); the distribution of **legislative powers** between the Union and States (Chapter I of Part XI); the **Seventh Schedule**; the **representation of States in Parliament**; and **Article 368 itself**. The Constitution (101st Amendment) Act, 2016 (GST) was ratified by States for this reason.

**Kihoto Hollohan v Zachillhu (1992):** Paragraph 7 of the Tenth Schedule (barring the courts' jurisdiction) was held invalid because the amendment did not obtain ratification by half of the States.

#### Judicial Limitations: Evolution of the Basic Structure Doctrine

### 8.3 United States

**Article V** provides a two-stage procedure:

- **Proposal:** (a) by **two-thirds of both Houses of Congress**, or (b) by a **convention** called by Congress on the application of **two-thirds of the State legislatures** (never used).

- **Ratification:** (a) by the legislatures of **three-fourths of the States**, or (b) by **conventions in three-fourths of the States**, as Congress decides. Only the **21st Amendment** (repealing Prohibition) was ratified by state conventions.

- **The President has no formal role** (*Hollingsworth v Virginia*, 1798); no presidential signature or veto applies.

- **Entrenched clause:** no State, without its consent, can be deprived of its equal suffrage in the Senate. (An earlier clause protecting the slave trade expired in 1808.)

- **Number:** 27 amendments. The first ten (**Bill of Rights**) were ratified in 1791. Important ones: **13th** (1865, abolition of slavery), **14th** (1868, citizenship, due process and equal protection), **15th** (1870, race and voting), **16th** (1913, income tax), **17th** (1913, direct election of Senators), **18th** (1919, Prohibition) and **21st** (1933, repeal), **19th** (1920, women's suffrage), **22nd** (1951, two-term limit), **25th** (1967, presidential disability and succession), **26th** (1971, voting age 18), and **27th** (ratified 1992, Congressional pay; first proposed in 1789).

- **Judicial role:** the Court treats most questions as political. **Hawke v Smith (1920):** ratification must be by the legislature, not by a popular referendum, when Congress specifies legislatures; **Dillon v Gloucester (1921):** ratification within a reasonable time; **Coleman v Miller (1939):** the question of time limits for ratification is political; **National Prohibition Cases (1920)**: upheld the 18th Amendment. No "basic structure" doctrine exists in the US.

- **Difficulty:** very rigid; many proposed amendments fail (e.g., the Equal Rights Amendment did not achieve ratification by three-fourths of the States within the deadline).

- **Informal amendment:** through judicial interpretation (*Marbury*, *Brown*), custom and convention (e.g., the two-term tradition before the 22nd Amendment), and statutes.

### 8.4 South Africa

- **Constitution of the Republic of South Africa, 1996** (Act 108 of 1996) is the supreme law (section 2). It was adopted by the Constitutional Assembly in May 1996; the **Constitutional Court refused to certify** the first text in the **First Certification Judgment (September 1996)** and certified the amended text in December 1996. It came into force on **4 February 1997.** This unusual certification procedure was required by the Interim Constitution (1993) and its **34 Constitutional Principles.**

- **Section 74 – Amendment procedure (three tiers):**

  1.  **Section 1 (founding provisions: human dignity, equality, non-racialism and non-sexism, supremacy of the Constitution, universal adult suffrage, multi-party democracy) and Section 74(1) itself:** at least **75% of the members of the National Assembly** *and* the support of at least **six of nine provinces** in the **National Council of Provinces (NCOP)**.

  2.  **Chapter 2 (Bill of Rights):** at least **two-thirds of the National Assembly** *and* **six provinces** in the NCOP (as amended; the Bill of Rights is entrenched at this level).

  3.  **Other provisions:** at least **two-thirds of the National Assembly**; also the NCOP's support of **six provinces** if the amendment relates to a matter affecting the NCOP, provincial boundaries, powers, functions or institutions, or specifically deals with a provincial matter.

- **Public participation:** at least **30 days** before a Bill amending the Constitution is introduced, the proposal must be published in the Gazette for public comment and submitted to provincial legislatures (section 74(5)). An amendment bill may only contain amendments and matters connected with them (section 74(8)).

- **Judicial review of amendments:** the Constitutional Court reviews the **procedural validity** of amendments. In **United Democratic Movement v President of the Republic of South Africa (2002)** (the "floor crossing" amendments), the Court upheld the amendments and discussed (without deciding) whether there are substantive limits on amendment, acknowledging the Indian basic structure doctrine; the Court said that a constitutional amendment that destroys the basic structure might be invalid. Earlier, **Premier of KwaZulu-Natal v President of the Republic of South Africa (1995)** dealt with procedural issues. Section 74 shows that the Constitution's founding values (section 1) are entrenched.

- **Recent attempt:** the **Constitution Eighteenth Amendment Bill** (to permit land expropriation without compensation by amending section 25) failed in December 2021 for lack of the required two-thirds majority.

- **Features:** a tiered, "value-based" entrenchment; open, participatory; the Constitution of South Africa is moderately rigid; the Indian Constitution's amendment procedure was one of the models for the South African approach (the Indian amending procedure, in turn, drew from the South African Constitution of 1909 as the Constituent Assembly borrowed).

## Chapter 9: Ombudsman: Sweden, UK and India

### 9.1 Concept and Features

**Ombudsman** is a Swedish word meaning a representative or agent of the citizen. The Ombudsman is an independent official, usually appointed by the legislature, who investigates **complaints against administrative action** (maladministration) and recommends remedies, without being a court. The institution protects citizens against bureaucratic injustice, arbitrariness, delay and corruption.

**Essential features:**

- **Independence** from the executive and being appointed by (and accountable to) the legislature.

- **Impartiality** and non-partisan nature.

- **Accessibility** to citizens: low-cost, informal, simple procedure.

- **Investigative power:** access to files and officials.

- **Recommendatory power:** most Ombudsmen cannot overturn decisions or enforce remedies; they rely on persuasion, publicity and reporting to the legislature ("sunlight as a disinfectant").

- **Jurisdiction** over maladministration rather than merits of policy; exclusion of matters before courts and policy decisions.

- **Reports** to the legislature: annual and special.

**Types:** classical/parliamentary Ombudsman; executive Ombudsman; hybrid (human rights and administrative); organisational and sectoral Ombudsman (banking, insurance, police, military, children, consumer).

**Global spread:** Sweden (1809); Finland (1919); Denmark (1955); Norway (1962); **New Zealand (1962)**, the first in the Commonwealth, with Sir Guy Powles as the first Ombudsman; the UK (1967); Tanzania; Canada's provinces from 1967; and many others. The International Ombudsman Institute was founded in 1978.

### 9.2 Sweden (The Original Model)

- **Origins:** The idea traces to **King Charles XII** (1713), who created the **"Highest Ombudsman"** (*Högste Ombudsmannen*) while in exile in Bender (Turkey) after the Battle of Poltava, to supervise officials. It was later renamed the **Chancellor of Justice (Justitiekanslern)** in 1719 (an executive office appointed by the Government that continues today).

- **Modern Parliamentary Ombudsman (JO – *Justitieombudsmannen*):** created by the **Instrument of Government of 1809** (after the deposition of Gustav IV Adolf) as an officer of the **Riksdag (Parliament)**, independent of the king, to supervise the application of laws by courts and officials. The first JO was **Lars August Mannerheim** (1809–10).

- **Appointment:** elected by the Riksdag. At present there are **four Parliamentary Ombudsmen**, one of whom is the **Chief Parliamentary Ombudsman**, for a term of four years (a legal provision in the Instrument of Government, Chapter 13, and the Act with Instruction for the Parliamentary Ombudsmen).

- **Jurisdiction:** the Ombudsmen supervise **public authorities, courts, civil servants and employees of the State and municipalities**, including the military and the police, to ensure that they observe laws and fulfil their obligations; the Ombudsmen do not usually supervise Members of Parliament and the Government ministers (who are subject to the Committee on the Constitution), or the Chancellor of Justice.

- **Powers:** to act on complaints or **on their own initiative (*suo motu*)**; to carry out **inspections** of authorities; to have access to files and officials; to issue **statements and opinions** (criticism, advice); to **initiate disciplinary action** and **prosecute officials** for criminal offences in office (rarely used). They cannot overrule decisions; the effect rests on prestige and publicity.

- **Public access to documents (*offentlighetsprincipen*)** and the **Freedom of the Press Act of 1766** (the world's oldest freedom of information law) give strength to the system.

- **Other Swedish Ombudsmen:** the **Equality Ombudsman**, Consumer Ombudsman, Children's Ombudsman, and the **Data Protection Authority** have separate mandates (executive ombudsmen).

- **Significance:** it is a model for the world; the Scandinavian model is **parliamentary**, **investigative** and **informal**.

### 9.3 United Kingdom

- **Background:** The **Whyatt Report (1961)**, by JUSTICE, recommended an Ombudsman-style institution for the UK. The **Parliamentary Commissioner Act, 1967** created the **Parliamentary Commissioner for Administration (PCA)**; the first PCA was **Sir Edmund Compton** (1967–71). It is now combined with the Health Service Commissioner as the **Parliamentary and Health Service Ombudsman (PHSO)** (the Health Service Commissioner was created in 1973).

- **Appointment:** by the Crown (on the recommendation of the Prime Minister, and the House of Commons has a say through select committee scrutiny); security of tenure like a High Court judge (removal by an address of both Houses).

- **Jurisdiction:** complaints about **maladministration** by **central government departments and bodies** listed in Schedule 2; **Schedule 3** excludes certain matters, such as civil and criminal proceedings, personnel and contract matters, foreign affairs, and extradition. The Act does not define **"maladministration"**; the famous **Crossman catalogue** (Richard Crossman, Leader of the House of Commons, 1966) lists "bias, neglect, inattention, delay, incompetence, ineptitude, perversity, turpitude, arbitrariness and so on".

- **MP filter:** the traditional rule that a complaint reaches the PCA only **through a Member of Parliament** (the "MP filter"), designed to preserve MPs' role; critics argue it limits accessibility, and reforms have been proposed.

- **Procedure and powers:** investigates in private; powers similar to the High Court in respect of summoning witnesses and the production of documents; **injustice caused by maladministration** is the test; reports to the MP and the department; **can make recommendations but cannot compel**; a special report may be laid before Parliament if the Government does not comply; the **Public Administration Select Committee (PASC)** scrutinises the work.

- **Landmark examples:** the **Barlow Clowes affair (1988–89)** (compensation to investors after the PCA's report found maladministration by the Department of Trade and Industry); the **Debt of Honour (2005)** (Second World War Far East prisoners); the **Equitable Life** report (2008, *Equitable Life: A Decade of Regulatory Failure*); and the **Channel Tunnel Rail Link** matter.

- **Judicial attitude:** **R v Parliamentary Commissioner for Administration, ex p Balchin (1997)** – the courts reviewed the Ombudsman's findings in respect of the Department of Transport; **R (Bradley) v Secretary of State for Work and Pensions (2008)** – the Government cannot reject the Ombudsman's findings without a rational and cogent reason; **R (Equitable Members Action Group) v HM Treasury (2009)** – the Government's decision to reject some recommendations was subject to review.

- **Other UK Ombudsmen:** **Local Government Ombudsman** (Local Government Act, 1974), now the **Local Government and Social Care Ombudsman**; **Scottish Public Services Ombudsman (2002)**; **Public Services Ombudsman for Wales (2005)**; **Northern Ireland Ombudsman**; **Financial Ombudsman Service** (2000); and other sectoral schemes.

### 9.4 India

#### Background

- The concept entered Indian discourse in the early 1960s. **Dr. L.M. Singhvi** coined the terms **"Lokpal"** and **"Lokayukta"** (1963). The **Administrative Reforms Commission (ARC, 1966–70)**, chaired first by **Morarji Desai** and then by **K. Hanumanthaiya**, recommended in its report on *Problems of Redress of Citizens' Grievances* (1966) the creation of **two special authorities**: **Lokpal** (at the Centre) and **Lokayuktas** (in the States), for complaints against ministers and administrators.

- The **Lokpal Bill was first introduced in 1968** (Lok Sabha) and lapsed; it was reintroduced in 1971, 1977, 1985, 1989, 1996, 1998, 2001, 2005, 2008 and 2011 without success. The 2011 **India Against Corruption** movement (Anna Hazare) accelerated its passing.

- **The Lokpal and Lokayuktas Act, 2013** received Presidential assent on **1 January 2014**, and came into force on **16 January 2014.**

#### Key Provisions of the Lokpal and Lokayuktas Act, 2013

- **Composition:** a **Chairperson** and up to **eight members**, of whom **50% must be judicial members**; **50% of members** must be from the SC, ST, OBC, minorities and women.

- **Chairperson:** a former Chief Justice of India, or a former Supreme Court judge, or an eminent person with impeccable integrity and outstanding ability, with at least 25 years of expertise in anti-corruption policy, public administration, vigilance, finance, law and management.

- **Selection Committee:** the **Prime Minister** (Chairperson); the **Speaker** of the Lok Sabha; the **Leader of Opposition** in the Lok Sabha; the **Chief Justice of India** (or a judge nominated by him); and an **eminent jurist** nominated by the President on the recommendation of the other four.

- **Term:** five years or till the age of 70, whichever is earlier.

- **Jurisdiction:** the **Prime Minister** (with exceptions for allegations relating to international relations, external and internal security, public order, atomic energy and space; and a special procedure requiring a full bench and a two-thirds majority for inquiry; proceedings *in camera*), **Ministers, Members of Parliament, Group A–D officials** and officers of the Central Government, and persons in entities funded or controlled by the Government or receiving foreign contributions above a threshold under the FCRA. The **judiciary** is not covered.

- **Wings:** an **Inquiry Wing** (preliminary inquiry) and a **Prosecution Wing**; the Lokpal can superintend the **CBI** in cases referred by it; the preliminary inquiry must usually be completed in 90 days; the investigation in six months; **special courts** are to try cases within a time limit; there is provision for **attachment of assets** from corruption; a complaint should be made within **seven years** of the alleged offence.

- **Lokayuktas:** the Act requires States to establish Lokayuktas within one year of commencement (Section 63), but allows States to determine the structure and the powers; so the structure varies.

- **First Lokpal:** **Justice Pinaki Chandra Ghose** (a retired Supreme Court judge) was appointed the first Chairperson in **March 2019**, five years after the Act. The 2016 amendment relaxed the requirement of public servants to declare assets. (Justice A.M. Khanwilkar was later appointed Chairperson in 2024.)

#### State Lokayuktas

- **Maharashtra** was the first State to establish the institution (**Lokayukta and Upa-Lokayuktas Act, 1971**, operational from 1972). Orissa passed an Act in 1970, but it lapsed and was not in force at the time. Others: Rajasthan (1973), Bihar (1974), Uttar Pradesh (1975), Madhya Pradesh (1981), Karnataka (1984 – considered strongest), Gujarat (1986), Delhi (1996), Kerala (1999), etc.

- **Variation:** in appointment, jurisdiction (whether the Chief Minister is covered), and powers (investigative or advisory). The **Karnataka Lokayukta** has police powers and is considered effective.

#### Related Institutions in India

- **Central Vigilance Commission (CVC):** set up in 1964 on the recommendation of the **Santhanam Committee**; statutory since the **CVC Act, 2003.**

- **CBI** (Delhi Special Police Establishment Act, 1946); **Central and State Information Commissions** (Right to Information Act, 2005); **Banking Ombudsman Scheme (1995)** (now integrated under the RBI's **Integrated Ombudsman Scheme, 2021**); **Insurance Ombudsman (1998)**; **Income Tax Ombudsman**; **National and State Human Rights Commissions** (Protection of Human Rights Act, 1993).

## Chapter 10: Open Government and Right to Information: USA, UK and India

### 10.1 Concept of Open Government

**Open government** is the doctrine that the business of government should be open to public scrutiny, based on **transparency, participation and accountability.** Secrecy should be the exception, not the rule. The idea rests on democratic theory: citizens cannot hold government accountable without information.

- **Justice Louis Brandeis (USA):** "Sunlight is said to be the best of disinfectants" (*Other People's Money*, 1914) – the most quoted line on transparency.

- **James Madison:** a popular government without popular information is a prologue to a farce or a tragedy.

- **Sweden:** the Freedom of the Press Act, 1766 is the world's first law giving public access to official documents (*offentlighetsprincipen*).

- **Pillars of open government:** (1) **proactive disclosure**; (2) **right to request information**; (3) **open meetings** and participation; (4) **open data**; (5) **protection of whistleblowers**; (6) **independent oversight** of disclosure disputes.

- **Open Government Partnership (OGP)**, launched in 2011 by eight founding governments (including the USA, UK, Brazil, Indonesia, Mexico, Norway, Philippines and South Africa). India has not joined the OGP.

- **Limits:** national security, privacy, commercial secrets, law enforcement, deliberative processes of government and international relations.

### 10.2 United States

#### Freedom of Information Act (FOIA), 1966

- Enacted in **1966** (signed by President Lyndon B. Johnson, effective from 1967), replacing the weak disclosure provision of the Administrative Procedure Act, 1946. Principle: **presumption of disclosure**; the **burden of justifying refusal rests on the agency.**

- **Amendments:** **1974** (after Watergate: judicial review of classification, time limits, fee waivers); **1996 (Electronic FOIA)**; **OPEN Government Act, 2007** (created the **Office of Government Information Services, OGIS**, as FOIA mediator/ombudsman); **FOIA Improvement Act, 2016** (a **"foreseeable harm" standard**: an agency may withhold exempt material only if disclosure would harm an interest protected by the exemption, or if disclosure is prohibited by law).

- **Coverage:** **federal executive agencies** (including independent agencies and government corporations). It **does not cover** Congress, the federal courts, and the President's immediate staff (the White House Office); State governments have their own laws.

- **Procedure:** a request to the agency; a decision within **20 working days**; administrative appeal; then **judicial review in a federal district court**, *de novo*, where the agency has the burden. **Vaughn v Rosen (1973)** led to the **"Vaughn index"** (the agency's itemised justification for withholding documents). Courts can order production and award attorney fees.

#### The Nine Exemptions (Memory List)

1.  **Classified** national defence or foreign policy information.

2.  Internal **personnel rules and practices** of an agency.

3.  Information exempted by **other statutes.**

4.  **Trade secrets** and confidential commercial or financial information.

5.  **Inter-agency or intra-agency memoranda** that would be privileged in litigation (**deliberative process privilege**, attorney–client, work product).

6.  **Personnel and medical files** where disclosure would be a "clearly unwarranted invasion of personal privacy".

7.  **Law enforcement records** (with specified harms).

8.  Records on the **regulation or supervision of financial institutions.**

9.  **Geological and geophysical information** concerning wells.

(Also, there are special exclusions for certain law enforcement and national security records.)

#### Related Statutes

- **Privacy Act, 1974** (protects personal records held by federal agencies; individuals can access and correct their records).

- **Government in the Sunshine Act, 1976** (open meetings of multi-member federal agencies).

- **Federal Advisory Committee Act, 1972** (open advisory committees).

- **Presidential Records Act, 1978** (presidential records are public property; released after a period).

- **Whistleblower Protection Act, 1989.**

- **Obama Memorandum on Transparency and Open Government (2009)** and the **Open Government Directive (2009)**; Data.gov.

#### Leading FOIA Cases

- **EPA v Mink (1973):** national security exemption and classification; led to the 1974 amendments.

- **NLRB v Robbins Tire (1978):** exemption 7 and the law enforcement process.

- **Department of Justice v Reporters Committee for Freedom of the Press (1989):** privacy exemption; compiled "rap sheets" protected as practical obscurity.

- **National Archives and Records Administration v Favish (2004):** privacy interests of family members.

- **Milner v Department of the Navy (2011):** narrow reading of exemption 2.

- **Constitutional basis:** the US Constitution contains no express right to information; the right is statutory (the First Amendment does not guarantee a general right of access to government information). Some cases (e.g., *Richmond Newspapers v Virginia*, 1980) recognise a right of access to criminal trials.

### 10.3 United Kingdom

#### Freedom of Information Act, 2000 (FOIA 2000)

- Passed in **2000**; the **right of access came fully into force on 1 January 2005.** Earlier, the UK relied on a culture of secrecy: the **Official Secrets Act 1911 (section 2)**, replaced by the **Official Secrets Act 1989**; the non-statutory **Code of Practice on Access to Government Information (1994)**. The Labour Government's White Paper *Your Right to Know* (1997) preceded the Act.

- **Scope:** covers public authorities listed in Schedule 1 (government departments, local authorities, NHS, police, schools, universities, etc.). The **Scottish FOI Act, 2002** applies to Scottish public bodies. **Environmental Information Regulations 2004** cover environmental information.

- **Two main duties (section 1):** (1) the duty to **confirm or deny** whether the authority holds the information; (2) the duty to **communicate** the information. **Section 19:** every authority must adopt a **publication scheme** (proactive disclosure).

- **Timing and cost:** 20 working days; no fee for requests but an authority can refuse requests that exceed an appropriate cost limit (**£600 for central government and £450 for other authorities**); vexatious or repeated requests can be refused (**section 14**).

- **Exemptions (Part II):** about two dozen, divided into:

  - **Absolute exemptions:** no public interest test applies – e.g., **section 21** (information accessible by other means), **section 23** (security bodies), **section 32** (court records), **section 34** (parliamentary privilege), **section 36** (prejudice to effective conduct of public affairs, as regards the House of Commons/Lords), **section 37** (communications with the Sovereign, heir and second in line – absolute since the 2010 amendment), **section 40** (personal information of the applicant; and personal data of third parties in some cases), **section 41** (information provided in confidence), **section 44** (statutory prohibitions).

  - **Qualified exemptions:** subject to the **public interest test** (public interest in maintaining the exemption must outweigh the public interest in disclosure) – e.g., **section 24** (national security), **section 26** (defence), **section 27** (international relations), **section 29** (economy), **section 30** (investigations), **section 31** (law enforcement), **section 35** (formulation of government policy), **section 36** (effective conduct of public affairs), **section 38** (health and safety), **section 42** (legal professional privilege), **section 43** (commercial interests).

- **Enforcement:** the **Information Commissioner (ICO)** (independent; also oversees data protection); appeals lie to the **First-tier Tribunal (Information Rights)**, then the **Upper Tribunal** and the courts on points of law.

- **Ministerial veto (section 53):** a cabinet minister can issue a certificate overriding the Commissioner's decision. **R (Evans) v Attorney General (2015, UK Supreme Court):** the "black spider memos" of Prince Charles; the Court held that the Attorney General could not override the court's decision merely because he disagreed, and the certificate was unlawful, upholding the rule of law and the finality of judicial decisions.

- **Historic significance:** the **MPs' expenses scandal (2009)** was uncovered through FOI requests; it advanced the transparency agenda.

- **Open government agenda:** the UK was a founding member of the OGP; **data.gov.uk** and the transparency agenda from 2010.

- **Related laws:** **Data Protection Act 2018** and UK GDPR; **Public Records Act 1958** (30-year rule, reduced to 20 years by the **Constitutional Reform and Governance Act 2010**); **Official Secrets Act 1989**.

### 10.4 India

#### Constitutional Foundations

- No express provision, but the Supreme Court has read the **right to know and to receive information** into **Article 19(1)(a)** (freedom of speech and expression) and **Article 21.**

- **State of Uttar Pradesh v Raj Narain (1975):** the Court rejected the government's claim of privilege under **Section 123 of the Evidence Act**, saying that the people have a right to know every public act, done in a public way; **Justice K.K. Mathew** said that in a government of responsibility, secrecy can only be for a few, and publicity is the rule.

- **S.P. Gupta v Union of India (1981):** the right to know is implicit in the right to free speech; open government is the new democratic culture; disclosure is the rule, secrecy the exception.

- **Secretary, Ministry of I&B v Cricket Association of Bengal (1995):** airwaves are public property; the right to receive information.

- **Dinesh Trivedi v Union of India (1997):** in a modern democracy, the citizen must have the right to know about the affairs of government.

- **Association for Democratic Reforms v Union of India (2002)** and **PUCL v Union of India (2003):** voters have the right to know about the antecedents and assets of candidates; **ADR v Union of India (2024)** struck down the **Electoral Bond Scheme**, holding that voters' right to information about political funding is protected under Article 19(1)(a).

- **Pre-RTI secrecy framework:** the **Official Secrets Act, 1923** (Section 5), **Indian Evidence Act (Sections 123, 124)**, and the Civil Service Conduct Rules.

#### Road to the RTI Act

- Grassroots movement: **Mazdoor Kisan Shakti Sangathan (MKSS)** in Rajasthan (**Aruna Roy**, Nikhil Dey, Shankar Singh) in the 1990s, which demanded public access to muster rolls and expenditure details (*jan sunwai* – public hearings). The **National Campaign for People's Right to Information (NCPRI)** (1996).

- **Press Council of India draft (1996)** and the **Shourie Committee (1997)** drafted bills.

- **State laws:** Tamil Nadu (1997), Goa (1997), Rajasthan (2000), Karnataka (2000), Delhi (2001), Maharashtra (2002), and others.

- **Freedom of Information Act, 2002** (passed by Parliament but never brought into force; criticised as weak).

- **Right to Information Act, 2005:** passed in June 2005 (assent 15 June 2005); came fully into force on **12 October 2005** (the National Advisory Council under the UPA government helped to strengthen it).

#### Key Provisions of the RTI Act, 2005

- **Section 2(h):** "public authority" (government bodies, and bodies owned, controlled or substantially financed by the government; NGOs substantially financed by the government). **Thalappalam Service Cooperative Bank v State of Kerala (2013)** discussed "substantially financed".

- **Section 2(j):** the "right to information" includes inspection of work, documents and records; taking notes, extracts or certified copies; taking samples; and obtaining information in electronic form.

- **Section 3:** all **citizens** have the right to information (not non-citizens or companies as such).

- **Section 4:** **proactive disclosure** – **Section 4(1)(b)** requires publication of 17 categories of information (organisation, functions, duties, powers, decision-making procedures, norms, budget, subsidies and so on).

- **Sections 5–7:** **Public Information Officers (PIOs)** and Assistant PIOs; application with a prescribed fee (typically Rs. 10) – **BPL applicants are exempt**; decision within **30 days**; **48 hours** where the information concerns the **life or liberty** of a person.

- **Section 8 – Exemptions:**

  - **8(1)(a):** information prejudicial to sovereignty and integrity of India, security, strategic, scientific or economic interests, relations with foreign States, or incitement to an offence.

  - **8(1)(b):** information expressly forbidden to be published by a court or tribunal, or the disclosure of which may constitute contempt of court.

  - **8(1)(c):** breach of privilege of Parliament or State Legislature.

  - **8(1)(d):** commercial confidence, trade secrets, intellectual property (unless larger public interest).

  - **8(1)(e):** information available to a person in a fiduciary relationship (unless larger public interest).

  - **8(1)(f):** information received in confidence from a foreign government.

  - **8(1)(g):** information that would endanger the life or physical safety of any person or identify a confidential source.

  - **8(1)(h):** information that would impede investigation or prosecution of offenders.

  - **8(1)(i):** **Cabinet papers**, including records of deliberations of the Council of Ministers, Secretaries and other officers (after the decision is taken, and the matter is complete or over, the material is open).

  - **8(1)(j):** personal information that has no relationship with any public activity or interest, or that would cause an unwarranted invasion of privacy, unless a larger public interest justifies disclosure. The proviso says that information that cannot be denied to Parliament or a State Legislature cannot be denied to a person. (**The Digital Personal Data Protection Act, 2023, section 44(3), amends Section 8(1)(j)** to exempt personal information more broadly and to omit the existing qualification, a change criticised by transparency advocates; verify its current commencement status.)

  - **8(2):** a **public interest override** – information may be disclosed if the public interest in disclosure outweighs the harm to protected interests.

  - **8(3):** the exemptions do not apply to information relating to events that occurred **20 years** before the request (except for 8(1)(a), (c) and (i)).

- **Section 9:** rejection for infringement of copyright; **Section 10:** severability; **Section 11:** third-party information.

- **Section 22:** the RTI Act **overrides** the **Official Secrets Act, 1923** and other inconsistent laws.

- **Section 24 and Second Schedule:** **intelligence and security organisations** (e.g., IB, RAW, CBI is not exempt but CBI is not listed... check); the exemption does not apply to allegations of **corruption and human rights violations** (for human rights information, the Information Commission's approval is needed, and the response must be within 45 days).

- **Section 23:** the jurisdiction of courts is barred (but the constitutional writ jurisdiction under Articles 32, 226 and 136 remains).

#### Appeals and Information Commissions

- **First appeal:** to an officer senior to the PIO within the same authority (within **30 days**).

- **Second appeal (Section 19(3)):** to the **Central Information Commission (CIC)** or **State Information Commission (SIC)** within **90 days**.

- **Complaints (Section 18):** directly to the Commission for refusal to accept an application and similar matters.

- **Penalty (Section 20):** Rs. 250 per day of delay, up to a maximum of **Rs. 25,000**, on the PIO; disciplinary action recommended; **compensation (Section 19(8)(b))** to the applicant.

- **Composition:** **CIC** – Chief Information Commissioner and up to **10 Information Commissioners**; **appointed by the President** on the recommendation of a committee: **Prime Minister** (Chair), **Leader of Opposition** in the Lok Sabha, and a **Union Cabinet Minister** nominated by the PM. For SICs: the Chief Minister, the Leader of Opposition and a Cabinet Minister nominated by the CM; appointed by the Governor.

- **RTI (Amendment) Act, 2019:** removed the statutory tenure (five years or until 65) and the status of **Election Commissioners/Chief Election Commissioner**, with the Central Government now empowered to prescribe the terms, salaries and tenure by rules. It was criticised as weakening the independence of the commissions.

- **Leading RTI cases:**

  - **CBSE v Aditya Bandopadhyay (2011):** examinees' right to inspect evaluated answer-books; also warned against using the RTI Act to disrupt administration or for impractical demands.

  - **Girish Ramchandra Deshpande v Central Information Commissioner (2012):** personal information such as income tax returns and service records is exempt under section 8(1)(j) unless there is a larger public interest.

  - **Reserve Bank of India v Jayantilal N. Mistry (2015):** RBI cannot withhold inspection reports and other documents on banks under the "fiduciary" exemption; transparency in financial regulation.

  - **Namit Sharma v Union of India (2012/2013):** the earlier decision that Information Commissions should have judicial members was clarified in the review (2013).

  - **Central Public Information Officer, Supreme Court of India v Subhash Chandra Agarwal (2019):** the office of the CJI is a public authority under the RTI Act; the collegium's information is subject to exemptions and a balancing test; RTI is not a tool for surveillance of the judiciary.

  - **Anjali Bhardwaj v Union of India (2019):** the Court directed timely appointments to Information Commissions.

- **Related law:** the **Whistle Blowers Protection Act, 2014** (not fully operationalised).

### Memory Capsule: Chapter 10

- **FOIA (US) 1966**; **FOIA (UK) 2000**, effective 2005; **RTI Act (India) 2005**, effective 12 October 2005; **Sweden 1766** is the oldest.

- **Brandeis:** "sunlight is the best disinfectant".

- **Raj Narain (1975)** and **S.P. Gupta (1981)** are the foundation cases for the right to know in India.

- US FOIA has **9** exemptions; the UK has **absolute** and **qualified** ones; India has **Section 8** with a **public interest override (8(2))** and **20-year rule (8(3))**.

## Chapter 11: Master Revision: Question Patterns, Cheat Sheet and Common Mistakes

### 11.1 Question Patterns Seen in UGC NET Law (Unit-wise Tendencies)

1.  **Match the following:** scholar ↔ idea (Bagehot, Dicey, Wheare, Fuller, Raz, Dworkin, Montesquieu, Madison, Kelsen, Zweigert and Kötz); case ↔ principle (Marbury, Kesavananda, Bommai, McCulloch, Ram Jawaya Kapur, Youngstown, Maneka Gandhi).

2.  **Country ↔ feature:** residuary power with the States/Centre; judicial review absent for federal statutes (Switzerland); a priori review (France); Ombudsman in Sweden; collegial executive (Switzerland).

3.  **Assertion–reason:** federalism and basic structure; Article 368 and ratification by States; rule of law and ADM Jabalpur.

4.  **Chronology:** FOIA 1966 → Privacy Act 1974 → FOIA UK 2000 → RTI 2005; Marbury 1803 → Kelsen 1920 → French 1958; Kesavananda 1973 → Minerva Mills 1980 → Coelho 2007.

5.  **Statements true/false:** UK has no strict separation of powers; India has single citizenship; the President in the US has no role in amendments; Switzerland's Federal Supreme Court cannot strike down federal acts.

6.  **Odd-one-out:** e.g., in federal states – USA, Canada, India, UK (UK is unitary).

### 11.3 Common Mistakes to Avoid

- Confusing **formal** (Fuller, Raz, Hayek) with **substantive** (Dworkin, Allan, Bingham) rule of law.

- Writing that the UK is a **federal** state or has a **written** constitution.

- Saying India has **dual citizenship** or **dual judiciary**.

- Assuming that US courts give **advisory opinions** (they do not; India's Article 143 does).

- Believing that the Swiss Court has full constitutional review of federal laws (it does not; Article 190).

- Forgetting that the French Council reviews laws **before** promulgation (and since 2010 also by QPC).

- Mixing the numbers: **Golaknath** (11 judges, 1967), **Kesavananda** (13 judges, 1973).
