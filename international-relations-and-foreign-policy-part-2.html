Passage: Strategic Autonomy and India’s Russia Policy

Foreign policy is often presented as a choice between friendship with one power and opposition to another. For India, however, the experience of the Russia–Ukraine conflict illustrates a more complicated approach. Since the beginning of the conflict in 2022, India has faced pressure from Western governments to take a stronger position against Russia, while simultaneously having longstanding strategic, defence, energy and economic relationships with Moscow. New Delhi's response can be understood through the idea of strategic autonomy: the capacity of a state to make decisions concerning its vital interests without allowing another power to determine those decisions.

Shyam Saran, in How India Sees the World, explains strategic autonomy not as the rejection of all partnerships, but as the ability to retain meaningful freedom of choice. A strategically autonomous state may cooperate closely with another country when its interests require it, while resisting pressure when its vital interests point in another direction. Autonomy, therefore, does not mean refusing every compromise; it means ensuring that compromises do not eliminate the ability to make independent decisions.

This approach can also be understood through the realist tradition in international relations. Hans J. Morgenthau, in Politics Among Nations, placed national interest at the centre of foreign policy. Kenneth Waltz, in Theory of International Politics, similarly argued that the absence of a central authority in international politics creates strong incentives for states to protect their own security and survival. E. H. Carr, in The Twenty Years' Crisis, criticised attempts to understand international politics entirely through abstract principles while ignoring power, interests and the actual circumstances confronting states.

India's response to the Russia–Ukraine conflict illustrates these ideas. India did not endorse Russia's invasion, and it repeatedly called for dialogue, diplomacy and an end to hostilities. At the same time, it did not join the Western sanctions regime in the manner demanded by some Western governments. India also continued its relationship with Russia, including substantial energy purchases. India's official position repeatedly emphasised the effects of the conflict on food, fuel and fertiliser security, particularly for developing countries.

The energy question became especially important. Following the disruption of global energy markets after 2022, Russian crude became an important source of supplies for Indian refiners. Bilateral trade expanded sharply, with Russian hydrocarbons contributing significantly to the increase. This illustrates an important distinction between moral preference and national policy. A government may disapprove of another state's actions while nevertheless maintaining economic relations with it if those relations are considered important to domestic security and economic stability.

The continuation of India–Russia relations also reflects a longer strategic relationship. Defence cooperation, nuclear energy, space cooperation and diplomatic coordination have developed over decades. The Indian government describes Russia as a longstanding strategic partner and continues to maintain cooperation across several sectors.

The Indian case therefore demonstrates that strategic autonomy does not necessarily require neutrality in every dispute. It involves preserving the ability to make issue-specific choices. India can cooperate with the United States and other Western powers in areas where their interests converge while maintaining relations with Russia where New Delhi considers those relations important. The underlying principle is that partnerships are instruments of national policy, rather than substitutes for it.

From this perspective, India's Russia policy under Prime Minister Narendra Modi can be understood less as choosing Russia over the West and more as an attempt to prevent any external power from determining India's choices. Strategic autonomy seeks room for manoeuvre: the capacity to cooperate, disagree, compromise or resist according to the particular interests at stake. The central question is therefore not “Which bloc does India belong to?”, but “Who makes the final decision when India's vital interests are involved?”

Question: What does the passage suggest about India's decision to maintain relations with Russia while cooperating with Western countries?

  1. India considers Russia and Western countries equally responsible for the conflict.
  2. India seeks to preserve flexibility by cooperating with different powers according to the particular interests involved.
  3. India has abandoned cooperation with Western countries.
  4. India has adopted a policy of permanent neutrality in international affairs.
Show answer

Answer: B. India seeks to preserve flexibility by cooperating with different powers according to the particular interests involved.

Passage: Indira Gandhi on 1971 and the Bangladesh Genocide

Indira Gandhi's account of the events of 1971, as she put it in speeches to Parliament and in appeals to foreign leaders, began from a plain fact: that a military government had launched a campaign of terror against its own citizens. After the election of December 1970 gave the Awami League a clear majority in Pakistan, the army, instead of transferring power, struck on the night of 25 March. Mass killing, rape and the burning of villages followed, directed especially against the Bengali intelligentsia and the Hindu minority. She called it by its name, genocide, and refused to accept that it was merely the internal affair of another state.

The consequence for India was immediate. Within months roughly ten million refugees had crossed the border, a number she argued no country, least of all a poor one, could absorb without grave harm to its economy and its social peace. She insisted that India was being asked to bear the cost of a crime committed elsewhere. The refugees, she said, were not a problem that could be solved by relief camps; they would return only when safety and a political settlement with the elected leaders made return possible. The cause had to be removed, not its symptoms treated.

She therefore toured the capitals of Europe and North America in the autumn, urging governments to pressure Islamabad towards a political solution and to release Sheikh Mujibur Rahman. She was deeply disappointed by the reluctance of the great powers to act, and she did not conceal her view that the world's silence encouraged the army. India, she maintained, had sought no territory and no war; it had waited and appealed for months. The treaty of friendship signed with the Soviet Union in August, she explained, was a prudent assurance in a hostile environment, not an abandonment of non-alignment.

When Pakistan attacked Indian airfields on 3 December, the question of war was settled by Pakistan itself. India's forces, working with the Mukti Bahini, brought the conflict to a close in a fortnight, and she announced in Parliament that Dhaka was the capital of a free country. In her view, the war ended a tragedy and fulfilled the aspirations of a people who had voted for their own future; and India, having secured the surrender, immediately declared a ceasefire in the west.

Question: Which of the following best states Indira Gandhi's position?

  1. The army's suppression was genocide, its consequences fell on India, and the world must press for a political settlement
  2. India should have annexed East Pakistan
  3. The refugees should be settled permanently in India
  4. The crisis was a purely internal matter of Pakistan
Show answer

Answer: A. The army's suppression was genocide, its consequences fell on India, and the world must press for a political settlement

Passage: The UAE Model of Countering Extremism

The United Arab Emirates (UAE) offers an important case study in the attempt to confront religious extremism through state-led social and intellectual transformation rather than through security measures alone. Its approach seeks to influence not only violent behaviour but also the ideas, institutions and social conditions that may contribute to extremism.

The Hedayah Centre, established in Abu Dhabi in 2012, represents this approach through research, training, prevention and rehabilitation aimed at countering violent extremism. Rather than treating extremism exclusively as a law-and-order problem, such an approach assumes that prevention requires understanding how extremist ideas develop and how individuals can be redirected away from violence.

The UAE has also attempted to challenge extremist narratives in the intellectual and digital sphere. The Sawab Centre, established jointly by the UAE and the United States in 2015, works to counter extremist narratives online and amplify alternative Muslim voices. This reflects an understanding that extremist organisations compete not only for territory but also for influence over ideas, identity and public discourse.

Another element is the Muslim Council of Elders, established in 2014 to promote religious scholarship, peace and interpretations of Islam opposed to extremism. The objective is not simply to suppress extremist interpretations but to strengthen alternative religious authorities and intellectual traditions.

The UAE's National Programme for Tolerance, launched in 2016, broadened the strategy further by combining education, family policy and cultural initiatives. The Abrahamic Family House, bringing a mosque, church and synagogue into a single complex, similarly presents interfaith coexistence as a social alternative to sectarian hostility.

However, this model is not without controversy. Scholars associated with the Carnegie Endowment have described these initiatives as part of the UAE's effort to promote a state-backed conception of “moderate Islam.” Supporters may view this as an attempt to prevent extremism by strengthening peaceful interpretations and social tolerance; critics may question how far the state should determine legitimate religious discourse.

The UAE case therefore illustrates a broader debate: Can extremism be sustainably reduced by transforming education, religious authority and social attitudes, or does state involvement in shaping belief create new problems? The model suggests that combating extremism may require not only security, but also competition over ideas, institutions and social norms.

Question: What is the central argument of the passage?

  1. Extremism can only be defeated through military force.
  2. The UAE combines security with social, educational and intellectual measures to counter extremism.
  3. The UAE has completely eliminated religious extremism.
  4. Religious institutions have no role in preventing extremism.
Show answer

Answer: B. The UAE combines security with social, educational and intellectual measures to counter extremism.
