13 practice questions with answers

1'Res Judicata' in administrative law means:

  1. Natural justice must be followed
  2. A superior court can always review lower court decisions
  3. No one can be a judge in his own case
  4. A decided question between the same parties cannot be re-agitated
Show answer and explanation

Answer: D. A decided question between the same parties cannot be re-agitated

Res judicata (a matter already judged) means that once a matter is decided between the same parties on the same facts and the same cause of action by a competent court, it cannot be re-litigated. It applies in administrative law too.

2Which of the following cases is associated with PIL being used to enforce Fundamental Rights of disadvantaged groups?

  1. Bandhua Mukti Morcha v. Union of India (1984)
  2. M.C. Mehta v. Union of India (environmental law)
  3. Vishaka v. State of Rajasthan (1997)
  4. All of the above — PIL has been used in all these
Show answer and explanation

Answer: D. All of the above — PIL has been used in all these

PIL has been used in all these landmark cases: Bandhua Mukti Morcha (1984) — bonded labour; M.C. Mehta — environmental protection; Vishaka (1997) — sexual harassment at workplace. PIL through Articles 32/226 has become a powerful tool for social justice.

3'Prohibition' writ differs from 'Certiorari' in that:

  1. Both are issued only after the decision
  2. There is no difference — both quash decisions
  3. Certiorari is preventive; Prohibition is curative
  4. Prohibition is issued before the inferior tribunal makes its decision; Certiorari is issued after to quash it
Show answer and explanation

Answer: D. Prohibition is issued before the inferior tribunal makes its decision; Certiorari is issued after to quash it

Prohibition is a preventive writ — issued to an inferior tribunal to stop it from exceeding jurisdiction before it makes a decision. Certiorari is a curative writ — issued after a decision to bring the record to a superior court and quash it. Both deal with jurisdictional excess.

4'Quo Warranto' writ can be issued against:

  1. Any person performing any duty
  2. A person holding a public office without legal authority or against a person claiming a public office
  3. Any government official
  4. Courts only
Show answer and explanation

Answer: B. A person holding a public office without legal authority or against a person claiming a public office

Quo Warranto (Latin: 'by what authority') questions the authority by which a person holds a public office. It can be issued against a person who claims or usurps a public office. Leading case: University of Mysore v. C.D. Govinda Rao (1964). It is a discretionary writ, not a writ of right.

5Which of the following CANNOT be enforced through a writ of Mandamus?

  1. A statutory duty of a public authority
  2. A duty to follow natural justice
  3. A private contractual obligation
  4. A discretionary duty where authority refuses to exercise discretion at all
Show answer and explanation

Answer: C. A private contractual obligation

Mandamus is issued only to enforce PUBLIC duties — statutory or constitutional. It cannot enforce purely private contractual rights. However, if a public authority refuses to exercise its discretion altogether (as opposed to exercising it wrongly), mandamus can compel it to act.

6'Jurisdictional error' in administrative law traditionally referred to:

  1. Any error of law in a decision
  2. An error going to the root of jurisdiction — acting without jurisdiction, exceeding jurisdiction, or refusing to exercise jurisdiction
  3. An error in applying the law correctly within jurisdiction
  4. A procedural error
Show answer and explanation

Answer: B. An error going to the root of jurisdiction — acting without jurisdiction, exceeding jurisdiction, or refusing to exercise jurisdiction

Traditional jurisdictional errors were of three types: (1) No jurisdiction — acting without any authority; (2) Excess of jurisdiction — exceeding the granted authority; (3) Failure to exercise jurisdiction — refusing to exercise power lawfully vested. Anisminic (1969) effectively widened this to include all errors of law.

7The 'Doctrine of Proportionality' is STRICTER than Wednesbury unreasonableness in the sense that:

  1. Proportionality gives more deference to administration
  2. Proportionality applies only to criminal matters
  3. Under proportionality, courts examine whether less restrictive means could be used — performing a balancing exercise; Wednesbury only asks if the decision was outrageous
  4. Courts can substitute their own decision for the administrative decision
Show answer and explanation

Answer: C. Under proportionality, courts examine whether less restrictive means could be used — performing a balancing exercise; Wednesbury only asks if the decision was outrageous

Wednesbury asks: was the decision so unreasonable that no reasonable authority could have made it? Proportionality asks: (1) Was the objective legitimate? (2) Were the means suitable? (3) Were the means necessary — could less restrictive means achieve the same goal? (4) Was there a fair balance between rights and public interest? Proportionality is a much more intensive review.

8'Colourable exercise of power' in judicial review of administrative action means:

  1. The authority made an error of fact
  2. The authority acted with improper motive — exercises a power for an unauthorized purpose
  3. The authority exceeded its jurisdiction
  4. The authority acted irrationally
Show answer and explanation

Answer: B. The authority acted with improper motive — exercises a power for an unauthorized purpose

Colourable exercise of power (also called 'abuse of power' or 'fraud on power') means the authority exercises its power for a purpose other than the one for which it was granted — i.e., the purpose was dressed up as something else. The substance, not the form, is examined.

9Which of the following is the CORRECT order of remedies from most available to most restricted?

  1. Quo Warranto → Habeas Corpus → Certiorari
  2. Habeas Corpus → Mandamus → Certiorari → Prohibition → Quo Warranto
  3. Certiorari → Mandamus → Prohibition → Habeas Corpus → Quo Warranto
  4. No particular order — all equally available
Show answer and explanation

Answer: B. Habeas Corpus → Mandamus → Certiorari → Prohibition → Quo Warranto

Habeas Corpus is the most readily available writ for protection of personal liberty — any person can apply regardless of locus standi in cases of illegal detention. Quo Warranto is the most restricted — it can only be sought against a person holding a public office without authority. The others fall in between.

10The 'Doctrine of Exhaustion of Remedies' in judicial review means:

  1. A party must exhaust all available statutory/alternative remedies before seeking judicial review, unless those remedies are inadequate or the case involves FRs or jurisdictional issues
  2. Courts should always be approached first
  3. Only applies in criminal matters
  4. Writs cannot be filed at all if any other remedy exists
Show answer and explanation

Answer: A. A party must exhaust all available statutory/alternative remedies before seeking judicial review, unless those remedies are inadequate or the case involves FRs or jurisdictional issues

The Doctrine of Exhaustion of Remedies is a procedural doctrine — courts generally require parties to exhaust available alternative remedies before granting judicial review. Exceptions: (1) alternative remedy is inadequate/illusory; (2) Fundamental Rights are directly infringed; (3) pure jurisdictional question; (4) natural justice violation. It is a rule of prudence, not an absolute bar.

11In Tata Cellular v. Union of India (1994), the Supreme Court established that judicial review of government contracts:

  1. Is not available for commercial decisions
  2. Applies the same strict standard as constitutional review
  3. Is available but courts should not substitute their commercial judgment — review limited to illegality, irrationality, procedural impropriety
  4. Government contracts are immune from judicial review
Show answer and explanation

Answer: C. Is available but courts should not substitute their commercial judgment — review limited to illegality, irrationality, procedural impropriety

In Tata Cellular v. Union of India (1994), the SC adopted Lord Diplock's three grounds from the GCHQ case for reviewing government contracts. Courts do not sit as a tender evaluation body — they only examine whether the process was legal, rational, and procedurally proper. Commercial wisdom of the decision is not reviewed.

12'Judicial activism' in India is associated with the expansion of jurisdiction through:

  1. Limiting the scope of writ jurisdiction
  2. Strict application of procedural rules
  3. PIL, epistolary jurisdiction, and expanded interpretation of Article 21
  4. Refusing to interfere in executive matters
Show answer and explanation

Answer: C. PIL, epistolary jurisdiction, and expanded interpretation of Article 21

Judicial activism in India is associated with: (1) Public Interest Litigation (PIL) — any public spirited person can approach courts; (2) Epistolary jurisdiction — letters to the court can be treated as writ petitions; (3) Expansive interpretation of Article 21 to include numerous uncontained rights. Pioneered by Justices P.N. Bhagwati and V.R. Krishna Iyer.

13Which Article provides for an independent body to audit accounts of the Union and States — the CAG?

  1. Article 148 — Comptroller and Auditor General of India
  2. Article 150
  3. Article 149
  4. Article 151
Show answer and explanation

Answer: A. Article 148 — Comptroller and Auditor General of India

Article 148 establishes the Comptroller and Auditor General of India. The CAG is appointed by the President and can be removed only in the same manner as a Supreme Court judge. Article 149 defines the duties and powers of CAG. Article 151 provides for presentation of CAG reports to Parliament/State Legislature.
