16 practice questions with answers

1Under the Patents Act 1970, the term 'voluntary licence' refers to:

  1. A licence granted by the patentee to another person by agreement
  2. A licence granted by the government without the patent owner's consent
  3. A licence automatically granted upon expiry of patent term
  4. A licence granted by the Controller under Section 84
Show answer and explanation

Answer: A. A licence granted by the patentee to another person by agreement

A voluntary licence under the Patents Act is a contractual arrangement where the patentee (licensor) grants permission to another person (licensee) to make, use, or sell the patented invention on agreed terms and conditions.

2Which of the following correctly describes a 'licence of right' under the Patents Act, 1970?

  1. A licence granted by the patent owner without any conditions
  2. A licence granted as of right to any person who applies, after endorsement by the Controller
  3. A compulsory licence under Section 84
  4. A government licence under Section 100
Show answer and explanation

Answer: B. A licence granted as of right to any person who applies, after endorsement by the Controller

Under Section 87, where a patent has been endorsed 'licences of right', any person who desires to work the patented invention may apply to the patentee for a licence — the patentee cannot refuse without cause, and disputes go to the Controller.

3Which of the following is TRUE about the assignment of a patent in India?

  1. Assignment of a patent must be in writing
  2. A patent can be assigned orally
  3. Assignment requires approval of the Controller
  4. A patent cannot be assigned under Indian law
Show answer and explanation

Answer: A. Assignment of a patent must be in writing

Section 68 of the Patents Act provides that an assignment of a patent or of a share in a patent, a mortgage, licence, or the creation of any other interest in a patent shall not be valid unless the same were in writing and the agreement between the parties concerned is reduced to the form of a document embodying all the terms and conditions.

4The 'doctrine of colourable imitation' in trademark law means:

  1. Using identical colours in a competing trademark
  2. Deliberately copying a competitor's colour scheme
  3. Making a mark so similar to a registered mark that it is likely to deceive consumers
  4. Using a similar sounding name for a competing product
Show answer and explanation

Answer: C. Making a mark so similar to a registered mark that it is likely to deceive consumers

Colourable imitation means a mark that so nearly resembles an existing registered mark as to be likely to deceive or cause confusion — it is a slightly altered version of the original mark designed to escape literal copying while still misleading consumers.

5The principle of 'functionality' in design and trademark law provides that:

  1. Functional features of a product are always protectable as trademarks
  2. Only functional designs can be registered
  3. Functional marks get stronger protection than non-functional marks
  4. Functional features of a product cannot receive trademark or design protection
Show answer and explanation

Answer: D. Functional features of a product cannot receive trademark or design protection

The functionality doctrine prevents the use of trademark or design law to protect utilitarian/functional features of a product — such features must be protected through patent law (if at all), not through trademark or design registration.

6Under TRIPS Article 27(2), member states may exclude from patentability inventions whose commercial exploitation would be contrary to:

  1. National economic interests
  2. Domestic employment policies
  3. Public order or morality (ordre public)
  4. Environmental regulations
Show answer and explanation

Answer: C. Public order or morality (ordre public)

TRIPS Article 27(2) allows member states to exclude from patentability inventions whose prevention within their territory of the commercial exploitation of which is necessary to protect ordre public or morality, including to protect human, animal or plant life or health or to avoid serious prejudice to the environment.

7The concept of 'orphan works' in copyright law refers to:

  1. Works whose authors are deceased
  2. Works in the public domain
  3. Works that are still under copyright but whose owners cannot be identified or located
  4. Works that have never been published
Show answer and explanation

Answer: C. Works that are still under copyright but whose owners cannot be identified or located

Orphan works are copyrighted works whose rights holders cannot be identified or found, making it difficult for potential users to obtain permissions. They represent a significant barrier to digitisation and access to cultural heritage.

8The 'three-step test' under TRIPS (Article 13) for copyright exceptions allows exceptions only if they:

  1. Are limited to certain special cases, do not conflict with normal exploitation of the work, and do not unreasonably prejudice the legitimate interests of the rights holder
  2. Are approved by WIPO
  3. Are explicitly listed in national legislation
  4. Apply only to educational uses
Show answer and explanation

Answer: A. Are limited to certain special cases, do not conflict with normal exploitation of the work, and do not unreasonably prejudice the legitimate interests of the rights holder

The three-step test (originally in Berne Convention Article 9(2), incorporated in TRIPS Article 13) permits copyright exceptions/limitations only if: (1) they are confined to certain special cases; (2) do not conflict with normal exploitation of the work; and (3) do not unreasonably prejudice the legitimate interests of the author.

9Under the Copyright Act 1957, a 'licence' of copyright is different from 'assignment' because:

  1. A licence transfers ownership; assignment does not
  2. An assignment transfers ownership of copyright; a licence only grants permission to use without transfer of ownership
  3. A licence must be registered; an assignment need not be
  4. Both transfer ownership equally
Show answer and explanation

Answer: B. An assignment transfers ownership of copyright; a licence only grants permission to use without transfer of ownership

An assignment (Section 18-19) transfers the ownership of copyright itself to the assignee. A licence (Section 30) merely grants permission to the licensee to exercise certain rights — the copyright remains with the licensor.

10Which of the following is an example of 'parallel importation' in the context of IP exhaustion?

  1. Importing counterfeit goods
  2. Importing genuine goods legally sold in another country without the Indian rights holder's consent
  3. Importing goods under a compulsory licence
  4. Government importation of patented medicines
Show answer and explanation

Answer: B. Importing genuine goods legally sold in another country without the Indian rights holder's consent

Parallel importation involves importing genuine (not counterfeit) goods that have been placed on the market in another country by or with the consent of the rights holder. Whether this is permitted depends on which exhaustion regime (national/regional/international) a country adopts.

11The 'Doctrine of Pith and Marrow' in patent law is used to determine:

  1. Whether an invention is novel
  2. Whether a mark is descriptive
  3. Whether a design is registrable
  4. The essential elements of a patent claim for infringement purposes
Show answer and explanation

Answer: D. The essential elements of a patent claim for infringement purposes

The Doctrine of Pith and Marrow (used in UK/Indian patent law, analogous to the US doctrine of equivalents) identifies the essential integers of a patent claim — if an alleged infringer takes all the essential elements (the pith and marrow) of the patent, infringement is established even without literal copying of every claim element.

12A 'patent troll' or 'Non-Practicing Entity (NPE)' in IP law refers to:

  1. A government body that administers patents
  2. A person who files fraudulent patent applications
  3. A company that files for multiple patents on the same invention
  4. An entity that acquires patents primarily to assert them against alleged infringers rather than to manufacture or commercialise products
Show answer and explanation

Answer: D. An entity that acquires patents primarily to assert them against alleged infringers rather than to manufacture or commercialise products

Patent trolls (NPEs) are entities that hold patents not to manufacture products but primarily to collect licensing fees or sue for infringement. They are a subject of significant controversy in patent policy.

13Under the Trade Marks Act 1999, which Section provides for removal of a trademark from the register for non-use?

  1. Section 29
  2. Section 46
  3. Section 47
  4. Section 57
Show answer and explanation

Answer: C. Section 47

Section 47 of the Trade Marks Act provides that a registered trademark may be removed from the register on the ground that it was registered without any bona fide intention to use it, or that it has not been used for a continuous period of 5 years and 3 months before the date of application for removal.

Report this question

14The 'Doctrine of Colourable Variation' in patent law provides that:

  1. Patents must describe their colour requirements
  2. Colour cannot be patented as a design element
  3. A trivial or immaterial variation from a patented invention does not avoid infringement
  4. A patent may be varied by the Controller on application
Show answer and explanation

Answer: C. A trivial or immaterial variation from a patented invention does not avoid infringement

The doctrine of colourable variation holds that making a trivial or inconsequential change to a patented invention to escape literal infringement is not sufficient — such a colourable variation still constitutes infringement if the essential features of the invention are taken.

Report this question

15The concept of 'anti-competitive practices' in the context of IP is dealt with under TRIPS in:

  1. Article 27
  2. Article 31
  3. Article 40
  4. Article 6
Show answer and explanation

Answer: C. Article 40

TRIPS Article 40 addresses control of anti-competitive practices in contractual licences, allowing member states to specify in their legislation conditions that may constitute an abuse of IP rights having an adverse effect on competition in the relevant market.

Report this question

16Under the Copyright Act 1957, 'rental rights' were introduced by which amendment?

  1. 1984 Amendment
  2. 1992 Amendment
  3. 1994 Amendment
  4. 2012 Amendment
Show answer and explanation

Answer: C. 1994 Amendment

The Copyright (Amendment) Act, 1994 introduced rental rights for sound recordings and computer programmes as part of TRIPS compliance.
