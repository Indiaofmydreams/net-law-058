11.2Case Law Master List (Jurisprudence Cases Most Likely to Appear)#

In brief

Covers Sources and precedent, Legal personality, Law and morality, Liability, Possession and property and more.

  • Sources and precedent
  • Legal personality
  • Law and morality
  • Liability
  • Possession and property
  • Poverty and development

  • Sources and precedent: Bengal Immunity (1955), Keshav Mills (1965), Golak Nath (1967, prospective overruling), Dawoodi Bohra (2005), Collector of Madura (1868, custom).

  • Legal personality: Salomon (1897), Pramatha Nath Mullick (1925, idol), Som Nath Dass (2000, Guru Granth Sahib), Mohd. Salim (2017, rivers), A. Nagaraja (2014, animals), Narayan Dutt Bhatt (2018).

  • Law and morality: Naz Foundation (2009), Koushal (2013), Navtej Johar (2018), Joseph Shine (2018), Puttaswamy (2017), Sabarimala (2018), Aveek Sarkar (2014), Common Cause (2018).

  • Liability: Rylands v Fletcher (1868), M.C. Mehta (1987, absolute liability), Vellore Citizens (1996), Kasturi Lal (1965), Nilabati Behera (1993), Bachan Singh (1980).

  • Possession and property: Armory v Delamirie (1722), Bridges v Hawkesworth (1851), South Staffordshire v Sharman (1896), I.R. Coelho (2007).

  • Poverty and development: Hussainara Khatoon (1979), Asiad (1982), Olga Tellis (1985), PUCL (2001), Janhit Abhiyan (2022).

11.3Ten Exam-Day Strategies for Jurisprudence MCQs#

In brief

Covers Read 'NOT' and 'INCORRECT' twice, For statement questions, Remember who attacks whom, Years matter, Distinguish similar concepts and more.

  • For statement questions
  • Remember who attacks whom
  • Years matter
  • Distinguish similar concepts
  • Where news is involved
  • court, year and outcome
  • Never leave a question blank

  1. Do the match-the-list questions by elimination: pin down the two items you are sure of; the rest usually follow.

  2. Read 'NOT' and 'INCORRECT' twice; the examiner often uses a minor swap (for example Bentham for Austin, Stammler for Kohler).

  3. For statement questions, look for absolute words ('always', 'only', 'never'); they usually signal a false statement.

  4. Remember who attacks whom: Hart attacks Austin; Dworkin attacks Hart; Fuller attacks Hart; Devlin versus Hart; Rawls versus utilitarians; Pogge and Beitz versus Rawls (on the global level); Habermas versus the postmodernists; Carrington and Fiss versus CLS.

  5. Years matter: Ancient Law 1861; Province 1832; Concept of Law 1961; Theory of Justice 1971; Law's Empire 1986; Development as Freedom 1999; Idea of Justice 2009; Declaration on the Right to Development 1986; SDGs 2015.

  6. Indian cases are favoured in applied questions: know the one-line holding of each landmark case, including whether it was later overruled or stayed.

  7. Distinguish similar concepts: ownership versus possession; right versus privilege (Hohfeld); fiction versus concession versus realist theory; strict versus absolute liability; legal versus moral duty.

  8. Use the schools' 'five questions' (nature, source of authority, law and morality, law and society, who finds the law) to place any unfamiliar proposition.

  9. Where news is involved (rivers, animals, EWS, privacy, climate), recall the court, year and outcome and any subsequent stay or reversal.

  10. Never leave a question blank, since there is no negative marking.
