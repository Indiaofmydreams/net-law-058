§Overview#

Bottom line: Unit VII is one of ten units in Paper 2 (Law), so expect roughly 8–12 of the 100 questions from Family Law. Every question is an MCQ worth 2 marks, there is no negative marking, and Papers 1 and 2 are attempted together in one 3-hour sitting (150 questions, 300 marks). Family Law rewards three things: exact section numbers and time limits, landmark case–principle pairs, and 2023–2026 developments. Recent NET papers lean on ‘current’ law, and your revised syllabus now names live-in relationships and the Uniform Civil Code outright, so both are fair game.

These notes follow the ten syllabus topics in order. Each topic has (1) a concept core in plain paragraphs, (2) bullet lists of provisions and conditions, (3) tables for case laws and comparisons, (4) a ‘Recent trend’ box, and (5) ‘MCQ traps’. The last section is a revision toolkit: master case table, numbers and dates, and one-liners. Items marked (verify) are recent developments taken from news and legal-portal reports (up to October 2026); confirm the exact title or holding on the Supreme Court website or LiveLaw before relying on them for a numerical or ‘who decided’ question.

0.2Where the marks usually sit (approximate, not an official breakup)#

In brief

Covers Highest density, Steady, Rising, Rarely but not never.

  • Highest density
  • Steady
  • Rising
  • Rarely but not never

  • Highest density: Muslim law (nikah, talaq forms, mahr, hiba, wasiyat, inheritance, wakf) and Hindu marriage and divorce (Sections 5, 7, 9–13B, 23–25 HMA).

  • Steady: maintenance (Section 125 CrPC / Section 144 BNSS, Shah Bano line, HAMA), adoption and guardianship (HAMA, HMGA, JJ Act, Guardians and Wards Act), Hindu succession (Sections 6, 8, 14, 15 HSA).

  • Rising: live-in relationships, UCC, foreign decrees, Article 142 and irretrievable breakdown, Waqf law, same-sex marriage (Supriyo).

  • Rarely but not never: Parsi and Christian statutes (grounds, mutual-consent waiting periods), Jewish law, Special Marriage Act details.

0.3Recent trend analysis (what the last 3–4 years suggest)#

In brief

Covers Constitutional lens on personal law, Judge-made divorce law, New criminal codes, Women’s property and maintenance, State-level codification and more.

  • Judge-made divorce law
  • New criminal codes
  • State-level codification
  • Waqf

  1. Constitutional lens on personal law. Questions ask which Article a judgment used (Shayara Bano: Article 14 ‘manifest arbitrariness’; Hadiya and Nandakumar: Article 21 choice of partner; Supriyo: no fundamental right to marry).

  2. Judge-made divorce law. Article 142 divorces for irretrievable breakdown (Shilpa Sailesh, 2023) and the waiver of the 6-month cooling-off period (Amardeep Singh, 2017) are now standard framework questions.

  3. New criminal codes. Old IPC/CrPC/Evidence Act numbers are being replaced by BNS/BNSS/BSA numbers: bigamy BNS 82, cruelty BNS 85, dowry death BNS 80, maintenance BNSS 144, presumption of legitimacy BSA 116. Know both numbers.

  4. Women’s property and maintenance. Vineeta Sharma (2020), Rajnesh v Neha (2020), Mohd. Abdul Samad (2024) and stridhan rulings recur.

  5. State-level codification. Uttarakhand (2024) and Gujarat (2026) UCCs, Goa’s Civil Code, and the ‘can a State enact a UCC?’ debate.

  6. Waqf. Waqf (Amendment) Act 2025 and the Supreme Court’s interim order of 15 September 2025 are almost certain current-affairs material.

0.4Top 25 high-yield one-liners (memorise first)#

In brief

Hindu marriage is a sacrament in origin; Section 7 requires customary rites, and saptapadi completes it where applicable. Section 5 HMA conditions: monogamy, sound mind, age…

  1. Hindu marriage is a sacrament in origin; Section 7 requires customary rites, and saptapadi completes it where applicable.

  2. Section 5 HMA conditions: monogamy, sound mind, age 21/18, not prohibited degrees, not sapindas (unless custom). Clause (vi) was omitted in 1978.

  3. Void: Section 11 (breach of 5(i), (iv), (v)). Voidable: Section 12 (impotence, 5(ii), force/fraud, pre-marital pregnancy by another).

  4. Section 16 HMA: children of void and voidable marriages are legitimate; Revanasiddappa (2023) extends property rights to parents’ share.

  5. Divorce grounds Section 13(1); wife-only grounds Section 13(2); mutual consent Section 13B (1 year separation + 6–18 month motion window).

  6. Amardeep Singh (2017): 6-month period is directory. Shilpa Sailesh (2023): Article 142 can dissolve a marriage for irretrievable breakdown.

  7. Saroj Rani (1984) upheld restitution of conjugal rights; T. Sareetha (1983) struck it down.

  8. Dastane (1975): cruelty proved on preponderance of probability. Samar Ghosh (2007): mental cruelty illustrations.

  9. Bipinchandra (1957): four elements of desertion.

  10. Muslim marriage is a civil contract; Shia recognise muta; Sunnis do not.

  11. Talaq-e-biddat void: Shayara Bano (2017, 3:2); criminalised by the 2019 Act.

  12. Dissolution of Muslim Marriages Act 1939: Section 2 lists nine grounds; wife’s conversion does not dissolve the marriage (Section 4).

  13. Mahr: debt, not price; wife has lien but no title (Maina Bibi; Kapore Chand).

  14. Shah Bano (1985) → Muslim Women (Protection of Rights on Divorce) Act 1986 → Danial Latifi (2001) → Mohd. Abdul Samad (2024).

  15. Section 125 CrPC / Section 144 BNSS: no monetary ceiling since 2001; secular remedy.

  16. HAMA: Section 6 requisites, Section 11 conditions, Section 12 effect, datta homam not essential.

  17. Shabnam Hashmi (2014): JJ Act adoption is open to all irrespective of religion; it is an enabling law.

  18. HMGA Section 6: father natural guardian ‘after’ whom the mother; Gita Hariharan (1999) reads ‘after’ as ‘in absence of’. Mother ordinarily has custody under 5.

  19. HSA Section 6 (2005): daughters coparceners by birth; Vineeta Sharma (2020) says father need not be alive on 9 September 2005.

  20. HSA Section 14: female Hindu is full owner; Tulasamma (1977) on widow’s maintenance-in-lieu.

  21. Muslim will: up to one-third of net estate without heirs’ consent; no registration, no writing needed.

  22. Hiba needs declaration, acceptance and delivery of possession; Section 129 TPA keeps Muslim gifts outside Section 123.

  23. Y. Narasimha Rao (1991): test for recognising foreign matrimonial decrees; Section 13 CPC exceptions (a)–(f).

  24. Live-in: Velusamy (2010) four conditions; Indra Sarma (2013) guidelines; DV Act Section 2(f) ‘relationship in the nature of marriage’.

  25. Article 44: UCC is a Directive Principle; Shah Bano, Sarla Mudgal, John Vallamattom urge it; Goa (Portuguese Civil Code), Uttarakhand (2024) and Gujarat (2026) are the practical examples.
