§Overview#

Core idea: There is no special statute on foreign matrimonial decrees. Their recognition is governed by Section 13 of the Code of Civil Procedure 1908 (conclusiveness of foreign judgments), read with Sections 14 and 44A CPC, and interpreted for matrimonial causes by Y. Narasimha Rao v Y. Venkata Lakshmi (1991). The aim is to prevent forum shopping and the ‘quickie’ divorces obtained abroad against an Indian spouse (often a wife left behind in India).

5.1Statutory framework#

In brief

Covers Section 2(5) and 2(6) CPC, Section 13 CPC, Section 14 CPC, Section 44A CPC, Foreign Marriage Act 1969 and more.

  • Section 2(5) and 2(6) CPC
  • Section 13 CPC
  • except
  • C-M-I-N-F-B
  • Section 14 CPC
  • Section 44A CPC
  • not
  • Foreign Marriage Act 1969

  • Section 2(5) and 2(6) CPC: ‘foreign court’ and ‘foreign judgment’.

  • Section 13 CPC: a foreign judgment is conclusive as to any matter directly adjudicated between the same parties, except where: (a) it has not been pronounced by a court of competent jurisdiction; (b) it has not been given on the merits of the case; (c) it appears on the face of the proceedings to be founded on an incorrect view of international law or a refusal to recognise the law of India where applicable; (d) the proceedings in which it was obtained are opposed to natural justice; (e) it has been obtained by fraud; (f) it sustains a claim founded on a breach of any law in force in India. Memory aid: C-M-I-N-F-B (Competence, Merits, International law, Natural justice, Fraud, Breach of Indian law).

  • Section 14 CPC: presumption that a foreign judgment was pronounced by a court of competent jurisdiction (rebuttable).

  • Section 44A CPC: a decree of a superior court of a ‘reciprocating territory’ (notified by the Central Government; e.g., the United Kingdom, Singapore, Bangladesh, UAE, Hong Kong, New Zealand, Trinidad and Tobago, Papua New Guinea, Fiji) is executable in India as if passed by an Indian District Court. The United States is not a reciprocating territory, so a US decree has to be sued on as a foreign judgment.

  • Foreign Marriage Act 1969: applies to marriages of Indian citizens abroad solemnised before a Marriage Officer, and to registration of foreign marriages; its provisions also cover validity and jurisdiction.

  • Section 19 HMA and Section 31 SMA: Indian courts’ jurisdiction in matrimonial causes (place of marriage; where the respondent resides; where parties last resided together; where the wife resides).

5.2Y. Narasimha Rao v Y. Venkata Lakshmi (1991)#

In brief

The Supreme Court held that a foreign matrimonial judgment will be recognised in India only if:

  • Jurisdiction
  • Grounds
  • Submission
  • Consent

The Supreme Court held that a foreign matrimonial judgment will be recognised in India only if:

  1. Jurisdiction: the foreign court had jurisdiction according to the law under which the parties were married (the matrimonial law of the parties); the jurisdiction assumed by the foreign court and the ground on which relief was granted must accord with the Indian matrimonial law (for Hindus, the HMA).

  2. Grounds: the ground for relief must be available under that law.

  3. Submission: the respondent must have been duly served, voluntarily and effectively submitted to the jurisdiction and contested on merits.

  4. Consent: if the decree rests on the respondent’s consent, the consent must be freely given, unequivocal and without fraud or mistake.

The Court rejected the rule that residence of the petitioner alone confers jurisdiction. Domicile and residence of the respondent matter; mere short stay of the petitioner in the foreign country is not enough. Ex parte decrees, or decrees on grounds (such as irretrievable breakdown) unknown to Indian personal law, are not recognised unless the respondent consented and submitted.

Case Principle
Satya v Teja Singh (1975) Divorce obtained from a Nevada court by a husband who falsely claimed domicile was a fraud on jurisdiction and not recognised
Smt. Neerja Saraf v Jayant Saraf (1994) Indian court can decide whether to continue its proceeding when a foreign proceeding is pending; principle of forum non conveniens and comity
Harmeeta Singh v Rajat Taneja (Delhi High Court, 2003) Anti-suit injunction against husband who sued in a foreign court to harass wife
Veena Kalia v Jatinder Nath Kalia (Delhi High Court, 1996) Ex parte foreign decree not recognised where respondent was not heard
Sondur Gopal v Sondur Rajini (2013) Indian court has jurisdiction in divorce where wife resides (in application of Section 19 HMA); foreign decrees do not shut out Indian proceedings if Narasimha Rao tests fail
Ruchi Majoo v Sanjeev Majoo (2011) Welfare of the child is paramount; a foreign custody order is only a factor, not a bar
Nithya Anand Raghavan v State (NCT of Delhi) (2017) Habeas corpus for custody of a child removed from foreign jurisdiction; Indian court may return the child under comity if it is in the child’s interest
Prateek Gupta v Shilpi Gupta (2018) Court’s duty is to protect the child’s welfare; it is not bound to return a child to a foreign court
Atul Jain (reported as 2026 LiveLaw (SC) 111, March 2026) Supreme Court (Justice Vikram Nath bench) set aside a High Court order dismissing a divorce petition in India because of a prior US divorce decree: the US decree on irretrievable breakdown is not recognised since that ground does not exist in the HMA and the husband had not effectively submitted to the foreign court; the Court itself granted divorce under Article 142 to end an eighteen-year separation (verify exact case title)

International conventions (background): India is not a party to the 1980 Hague Convention on Civil Aspects of International Child Abduction (the 2016 Law Commission 263rd Report recommended accession); India is party to the 1993 Hague Convention on Inter-country Adoption (ratified 2003). A Bill for compulsory registration of NRI marriages has been proposed but not enacted (verify).

5.4Applying the rule: examples#

In brief

Covers Muslim talaq pronounced abroad.

  • Muslim talaq pronounced abroad

  • US ‘no-fault’ divorce decree against an Indian wife who did not appear: not recognised (ex parte, ground not recognised, no submission).

  • Decree obtained by consent of both, after both appeared and contested: recognised if consent is real.

  • Decree from a court in the country where both parties are domiciled and married, on grounds also valid under the personal law: likely recognised.

  • Muslim talaq pronounced abroad: analysed according to the personal law and the 2019 Act when talaq-e-biddat is involved.

★Memory Capsule: Recognition of Foreign Decrees on Marriage and Divorce

Memorise

  • Section 13 CPC lists six exceptions: (a)–(f). ‘Conclusive’ is the default.
  • Section 44A applies only to decrees from reciprocating territories; the US and Canada are not (UK and Singapore are).
  • Narasimha Rao looks to the respondent’s residence and submission; the petitioner’s short stay is not enough.
  • Foreign decree on a ground unknown to HMA (irretrievable breakdown) is not binding on a Hindu spouse unless the spouse submitted.

★Analysis Angle: Recognition of Foreign Decrees on Marriage and Divorce

Analyse / traps

Recent trend: In 2026 the Supreme Court has combined non-recognition of foreign no-fault decrees with the exercise of Article 142 to give Indian divorces, so that the wife is not left without relief and parallel litigation ends. Expect questions on Section 13(a)–(f) and the four Narasimha Rao tests.
