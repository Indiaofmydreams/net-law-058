§Overview#

Core idea: Maintenance is available through several overlapping routes: the personal law (Hindu Adoptions and Maintenance Act 1956; Muslim law and the 1986 Act), the matrimonial statutes (Sections 24–25 HMA and equivalents), the secular criminal-procedure provision (Section 125 CrPC, now Section 144 BNSS), and the DV Act 2005. Dower and stridhan are the wife’s own property rights.

6.1Maintenance under Hindu law (HAMA 1956)#

In brief

Covers Rajnesh v Neha (2020).

  • Rajnesh v Neha (2020)
Section Subject Key points
18 Wife’s maintenance Entitled during her lifetime; entitled to live separately and still claim maintenance if husband is guilty of desertion, cruelty, has another living wife, keeps a concubine in the same house or habitually lives with one elsewhere, has converted, or on any other justifying cause (leprosy was also listed earlier; deleted 2019: verify). No maintenance if she is unchaste or ceases to be a Hindu
19 Widowed daughter-in-law Maintenance from father-in-law if she cannot maintain herself from her own earnings, property, her husband’s estate or from her children; obligation exists only if father-in-law has means out of coparcenary property; ceases on remarriage
20 Children and aged parents A Hindu is bound to maintain legitimate or illegitimate children (until minors) and aged or infirm parents and unmarried daughters who cannot maintain themselves
21 Dependants Father, mother, widow, son’s widow, minor sons and daughters of a deceased person etc.
22 Maintenance of dependants from estate Heirs who take the estate are bound to maintain the deceased’s dependants
23 Amount Discretion of court, considering position and status, number of claimants, reasonable wants, claimant’s own property, etc.
24–27 Disqualification, change of circumstances, debts, charge on property

  • Interim and permanent alimony in matrimonial cases: Section 24 (pendente lite; either spouse; decided within 60 days of service of notice); Section 25 (permanent alimony; at the time of or after the decree; may be varied). No fixed formula; the Supreme Court says the amount depends on status, needs, income and conduct (Rajnesh v Neha, 2020; Vinny Parmvir Parmar v Parmvir Parmar, 2011; Kiran Jyot Maini v Anish Pramod Patel, 2024).

  • Rajnesh v Neha (2020): all courts must require an affidavit of assets and liabilities; interim maintenance runs from the date of application; overlapping claims under different laws must be disclosed and adjusted; execution can be done through civil and criminal means. The Supreme Court in 2026 decisions on alimony (Deepa Joshi, Ruchika Rathi, Anju Jain; reported) repeated that there is no fixed formula and that the one-time settlement (‘clean break’) approach can be used with Article 142 (verify).

6.2Section 125 CrPC / Section 144 BNSS#

In brief

Covers Who can claim, Ceiling, Disqualification of wife (125(4)), Procedure, Secular and religion-neutral.

  • Who can claim
  • Ceiling
  • Procedure
  • BNSS
  • Secular and religion-neutral

  • Who can claim: wife (including a divorced wife who has not remarried), legitimate or illegitimate minor children, major children with physical or mental abnormality or injury, and father or mother, if unable to maintain themselves, from a person with sufficient means who neglects or refuses to maintain them.

  • Ceiling: the earlier Rs 500 limit was removed in 2001; there is no ceiling now.

  • Disqualification of wife (125(4)): living in adultery; refusing without sufficient reason to live with husband; living separately by mutual consent.

  • Procedure: Magistrate First Class; interim maintenance allowed; Sections 126–128 CrPC: procedure, alteration and enforcement; BNSS: Section 144 (maintenance), Section 145 (procedure), Section 146 (alteration), Section 147 (enforcement). The Supreme Court (Gaurav Joshi, 16 April 2026, reported) held that EMIs and loan commitments cannot defeat a maintenance obligation (verify).

  • Secular and religion-neutral: Bai Tahira v Ali Hussain Fissalli (1979), Fuzlunbi v K. Khader Vali (1980), Shah Bano (1985), Mohd. Abdul Samad v State of Telangana (2024).

  • Vijaya Manohar Arbat v Kashirao Rajaram Sawai (1987): a married daughter with means has a duty to maintain her parents under Section 125.

6.3Maintenance under Muslim law and the 1986 and 2019 Acts#

In brief

Covers During marriage, After divorce (classical law), Danial Latifi v Union of India (2001), 2019 Act, Section 5.

  • During marriage
  • After divorce (classical law)
  • 2019 Act, Section 5

  • During marriage: husband must maintain (nafaqa: food, clothing, lodging) even if the wife has means; the wife loses it if she is disobedient (nashiza) without lawful reason.

  • After divorce (classical law): only during iddat (three months or three menstrual courses; until delivery if pregnant; for a widow four months and ten days).

  • Shah Bano (Mohd. Ahmed Khan v Shah Bano Begum, 1985, Constitution Bench, Chandrachud CJ): Section 125 CrPC applies to Muslim women; mahr is not a sum payable ‘on divorce’ in lieu of maintenance; maintenance of Rs 179.20 per month. The case also urged a UCC.

  • Muslim Women (Protection of Rights on Divorce) Act 1986: Section 3: reasonable and fair provision and maintenance within the iddat period, mahr and property; Section 4: if no relatives, the State Waqf Board pays; Section 5: option of Section 125 by joint affidavit.

  • Danial Latifi v Union of India (2001): upheld the 1986 Act but read it down: ‘reasonable and fair provision’ means a lump sum covering her future needs and may extend beyond iddat; Section 125 remains available.

  • Iqbal Bano v State of UP (2007), Shabana Bano v Imran Khan (2010): divorced Muslim woman can claim under Section 125 even after iddat if she has not remarried and is unable to maintain herself.

  • Mohd. Abdul Samad v State of Telangana (10 July 2024): Justices Nagarathna and Masih: a Muslim woman, including one illegally divorced by triple talaq, can seek maintenance under Section 125 CrPC; the remedy is in addition to the 1986 Act and the 2019 Act’s subsistence allowance; Section 125 is secular and religion-neutral. The All India Muslim Personal Law Board criticised it.

  • 2019 Act, Section 5: subsistence allowance for the woman subjected to talaq-e-biddat, fixed by a Magistrate.

6.4Other laws and cross-cutting rules#

In brief

Covers Christians, Parsis, SMA, DV Act 2005, Dowry Prohibition Act 1961.

  • Christians
  • Parsis
  • SMA
  • DV Act 2005
  • Dowry Prohibition Act 1961

  • Christians: Divorce Act 1869 Sections 36 (alimony pendente lite) and 37 (permanent alimony); Section 125 CrPC.

  • Parsis: Parsi Act Sections 39 (pendente lite) and 40 (permanent alimony).

  • SMA: Sections 36–37.

  • DV Act 2005: monetary relief (Section 20), residence orders (Section 19). Satish Chander Ahuja v Sneha Ahuja (2020) overruled S.R. Batra v Taruna Batra (2006): a woman can claim residence in a house owned by in-laws if she has lived there in a domestic relationship; Hiral P. Harsora v Kusum Narottamdas Harsora (2016): ‘adult male’ struck from definition of respondent (women can be respondents); Prabha Tyagi v Kamlesh Devi (2022): actual residence at the time of filing not required.

  • Maintenance and Welfare of Parents and Senior Citizens Act 2007: tribunal-based maintenance for parents and senior citizens.

  • Dowry Prohibition Act 1961: Section 3 (giving or taking dowry: at least five years’ imprisonment and fine at least Rs 15,000 or the value of the dowry, whichever is more), Section 4 (demanding), Section 6 (dowry to be held in trust for the woman); BNS Section 80 (dowry death: minimum seven years); BSA Section 118 (presumption of dowry death).

6.5Dower (mahr)#

In brief

Definition: a sum of money or other property which the wife is entitled to receive from the husband in consideration of the marriage (Hedaya; Mulla).

  • Definition
  • Amount
  • Dower is a debt
  • Widow’s right of retention
  • Remission
  • Dower and the 1986 Act
  • Dower vs dowry

Definition: a sum of money or other property which the wife is entitled to receive from the husband in consideration of the marriage (Hedaya; Mulla). Mahmood J. in Abdul Kadir v Salima (1886): dower is not the price of consideration but an obligation imposed by law on the husband as a mark of respect for the wife.

Classification Meaning
Specified (mahr-i-musamma) Fixed by agreement before, at or after marriage
Proper (mahr-i-misl) Not specified: assessed by reference to the dower of paternal female relatives of the wife (her father’s family: sisters, aunts), her own personal qualities and her husband’s status
Prompt (muajjal) Payable on demand; wife can refuse cohabitation until it is paid; if marriage is not consummated she can refuse sexual relations
Deferred (muwajjal) Payable on dissolution of marriage by death or divorce; it is not payable earlier unless agreed

Amount: minimum under Hanafi law is 10 dirhams; Maliki 3 dirhams; Shia and Shafi’i: no fixed minimum. No maximum. If marriage is dissolved before consummation: half of the specified dower is due; if none was specified, a consolatory gift (mutah). Dower is a debt: the wife is an unsecured creditor of the husband’s estate ranking equally with other creditors; heirs are liable only to the extent of the estate in their hands, in proportion to their shares. Widow’s right of retention: if she is lawfully in possession of the husband’s property, she can retain it until her dower is paid, but she does not acquire title (Maina Bibi v Chaudhri Vakil Ahmad, PC 1924; Kapore Chand v Kadar Unnisa, SC 1953). Remission: wife can remit (hiba-i-mahr) with free consent. Dower and the 1986 Act: mahr is to be paid at divorce in addition to maintenance (Section 3).

Dower vs dowry: dower is the wife’s legal right in Muslim law and is not prohibited; dowry is illegal under the Dowry Prohibition Act 1961.

6.6Stridhan#

In brief

Meaning: a woman’s absolute property over which she has full right of ownership and disposal.

  • Meaning
  • Section 14 HSA
  • Husband’s position
  • Section 27 HMA
  • Succession to stridhan
  • Christians and Parsis

Meaning: a woman’s absolute property over which she has full right of ownership and disposal. In classical Hindu law (Manu, Yajnavalkya, Narada, Katyayana) stridhan covers: gifts before the nuptial fire (adhyagni), at the bridal procession (adhyavahanika), gifts of affection (pritidatta, from in-laws), gifts at the time of leave-taking or obeisance (padavandanika), gifts from relatives (saudayika), subsequent gifts (anvadheyaka), and (in some texts) bridal price (sulka). The Mitakshara gives the widest meaning (including property by inheritance, partition, purchase, and her own earnings), the Dayabhaga narrower; the Bombay school (Vyavahara Mayukha) is in between.

  • Section 14 HSA: any property possessed by a female Hindu, whether acquired before or after the Act, is held as full owner and not as limited owner; ‘property’ includes inheritance, devise, partition, maintenance or arrears, gift, own skill or exertion, purchase, prescription, and her stridhan. Section 14(2): property acquired by gift, will, instrument, decree or award which prescribes a restricted estate is excepted. V. Tulasamma v Sesha Reddy (1977): a widow’s right of maintenance converts a limited estate into an absolute estate under Section 14(1); Section 14(2) is a narrow exception. Eramma v Verrupanna (1966), Bai Vajia v Thakorbhai Chelabhai (1979): possession is a requirement.

  • Husband’s position: he has no right over stridhan during marriage except in an emergency, and must restore it (Pratibha Rani v Suraj Kumar, 1985: stridhan is the wife’s sole property; refusal to return it is criminal breach of trust under Section 406 IPC, now BNS Section 316). Rashmi Kumar v Mahesh Kumar Bhada (1997) reaffirms. The Supreme Court in 2024–25 judgments (reported; for instance Maya Gopinathan v Anoop S.B., verify) again held that stridhan is exclusively the woman’s and that the husband and in-laws hold it only in trust.

  • Section 27 HMA: court may pass orders about property presented at or about the time of marriage which belongs jointly to both parties.

  • Succession to stridhan: Section 15 HSA (see topic 8): the pattern depends on the source (parents’ or husband’s family).

  • Christians and Parsis: Married Women’s Property Act 1874 protects a married woman’s separate property (not applicable to Hindus, Muslims, Buddhists, Sikhs and Jains).

★Memory Capsule: Maintenance, Dower (Mahr) and Stridhan

Memorise

  • Section 125 CrPC ceiling of Rs 500 was removed in 2001.
  • Section 144 BNSS corresponds to Section 125 CrPC; Section 144 CrPC is the old prohibitory order provision (do not confuse).
  • Dower is a debt, not ‘consideration’ in the contractual sense; the widow’s lien is not ownership.
  • Section 14(2) HSA is an exception; Section 14(1) is the general rule (Tulasamma).
  • Shah Bano is a Section 125 case, and the 1986 Act partly overrode it; Danial Latifi upheld the 1986 Act as interpreted.

★Analysis Angle: Maintenance, Dower (Mahr) and Stridhan

Analyse / traps

Recent trend: Court decisions in 2024–2026 emphasise (a) full disclosure of income and assets, (b) maintenance as a means of dignity (not a bargaining tool), (c) no maintenance cap under Section 144 BNSS, (d) a wife’s qualification does not by itself bar alimony, and (e) stridhan as an enforceable right.
