§Overview#

Core idea: Intestate succession is governed by the personal law of the deceased: the Hindu Succession Act 1956 (HSA) for Hindus, Buddhists, Jains and Sikhs; uncodified Muslim law (Hanafi, Shia); the Indian Succession Act 1925 (ISA) for Christians, Jews and, with special rules, Parsis. Testamentary succession is covered in topic 9.

8.1Hindu Succession Act 1956#

In brief

Application (Section 2): Hindus in the extended sense (as in HMA); not Scheduled Tribes unless notified; not Muslims, Christians, Parsis or Jews.

  • Application (Section 2)
  • Overriding effect (Section 4)
  • 9 September 2005
  • by birth
  • notional partition
  • 20 December 2004
  • Cases
  • Repealed by 2005

Application (Section 2): Hindus in the extended sense (as in HMA); not Scheduled Tribes unless notified; not Muslims, Christians, Parsis or Jews. Overriding effect (Section 4): HSA overrides any text or custom on matters it deals with.

Mitakshara coparcenary and Section 6 (as amended in 2005)

  • Before 2005, only male members were coparceners. The Hindu Succession (Amendment) Act 2005 (in force from 9 September 2005) made the daughter a coparcener by birth ‘in the same manner as the son’, with the same rights and liabilities (Section 6(1)).

  • Section 6(3): on the death of a Hindu after the amendment, his interest in coparcenary property devolves by testamentary or intestate succession (not by survivorship), on the basis of notional partition; daughter gets the same share as a son; the share of a pre-deceased child goes to his or her surviving child or grandchild.

  • Section 6(1) proviso and 6(5): not applicable to a partition effected before 20 December 2004 (a registered deed or a court decree).

  • Cases: Prakash v Phulavati (2015): amendment prospective: only living daughters of living coparceners on 9 September 2005. Danamma @ Suman Surpur v Amar (2018): daughters entitled even if father died before 2005 (partly contrary). Vineeta Sharma v Rakesh Sharma (11 August 2020, three-judge bench of Justices Arun Mishra, S. Abdul Nazeer and M.R. Shah): the right of a daughter is by birth; it is not necessary that the father was alive on 9 September 2005; the provision is ‘retroactive’ in the sense that it applies to rights arising from birth; oral partition and unregistered family settlements cannot defeat the right unless proved by public documents or a court decree; overruled Prakash and part of Danamma. Ganduri Koteshwaramma v Chakiri Yanadi (2011): benefit of the amendment extended to pending partition suits before final decree. Arunachala Gounder v Ponnusamy (2022): a Hindu male’s self-acquired property devolves by succession; if he dies intestate without a male heir the daughter inherits, even for deaths before 1956, and the daughter’s right is superior to collaterals.

  • Dayabhaga: sons do not acquire an interest by birth; coparcenary in the Mitakshara sense does not exist, so Section 6 mostly concerns Mitakshara.

  • Notional partition and Gurupad Khandappa Magdum v Hirabai (1978): the interest of a deceased coparcener is determined by assuming a partition immediately before death; widow’s share added.

  • Repealed by 2005: Section 4(2) (agricultural holdings), Section 23 (daughter’s limited right in dwelling house), Section 24 (certain widows’ disqualification on remarriage).

Heirs of a Hindu male (Sections 8–13)

Class Members Rule
Class I (16 heirs after 2005) Son; daughter; widow; mother; son of predeceased son; daughter of predeceased son; son of predeceased daughter; daughter of predeceased daughter; widow of predeceased son; son of predeceased son of predeceased son; daughter of predeceased son of predeceased son; widow of predeceased son of predeceased son; and (added 2005) son of predeceased daughter of predeceased daughter; daughter of predeceased daughter of predeceased daughter; daughter of predeceased son of predeceased daughter; daughter of predeceased daughter of predeceased son Take simultaneously and to the exclusion of all others
Class II (9 entries) I: father; II: son’s daughter’s son, son’s daughter’s daughter, brother, sister; III: daughter’s son’s son, daughter’s son’s daughter, daughter’s daughter’s son, daughter’s daughter’s daughter; IV: brother’s son, sister’s son, brother’s daughter, sister’s daughter; V: father’s father, father’s mother; VI: father’s widow, brother’s widow; VII: father’s brother, father’s sister; VIII: mother’s father, mother’s mother; IX: mother’s brother, mother’s sister An entry excludes the later entries; those in one entry take together
Agnates Related wholly through males Preference by degrees (Section 12)
Cognates Related not wholly through males After agnates
Government If no heirs Escheat (Section 29), subject to liabilities

  • Distribution among Class I (Section 10): Rule 1: widow (or widows together) takes one share; Rule 2: surviving sons, daughters and the mother one share each; Rule 3: the heirs of a predeceased son or daughter take together one share; Rule 4: within each branch, the division is per stirpes.

  • Other sections: Section 14 (female’s absolute ownership), 15–16 (female’s property), 18 (full blood preferred over half blood), 19 (per capita; tenants in common), 20 (child in womb is treated as born), 21 (simultaneous deaths: the younger is presumed to survive), 22 (preferential right to acquire the property of the intestate), 25 (murderer disqualified), 26 (descendants of a convert disqualified unless Hindus at the time of opening succession), 27 (disqualified person treated as predeceased), 28 (disease or defect is not a disqualification), 29 (escheat), 30 (testamentary succession: a Hindu may dispose of his coparcenary interest by will).

Property of a Hindu female (Sections 15–16)

  • 15(1): order: (a) sons, daughters (including children of predeceased children) and husband together; (b) heirs of the husband; (c) mother and father; (d) heirs of the father; (e) heirs of the mother.

  • 15(2): exceptions (the ‘source’ rule): (a) property inherited from her father or mother: if no son, daughter or their children, goes to the father’s heirs; (b) property inherited from her husband or father-in-law: if no son, daughter or their children, goes to the husband’s heirs.

  • Section 16: rules for distribution among heirs.

  • Pending challenge: the Supreme Court is hearing petitions challenging Sections 15 and 16 as discriminatory against women; the Court indicated it would proceed cautiously (reported; verify status).

8.2Muslim law of inheritance#

In brief

Principles: inheritance arises only on death; no birth-right; no primogeniture; no distinction between self-acquired and ancestral property; no right of representation in…

  • Principles
  • Sunni (Hanafi) heirs
  • Residuaries (asaba)
  • Distant kindred (dhawul arham)
  • Doctrine of Aul (increase)
  • Doctrine of Radd (return)
  • Disqualifications
  • Posthumous child

Principles: inheritance arises only on death; no birth-right; no primogeniture; no distinction between self-acquired and ancestral property; no right of representation in Sunni law (nearer in degree excludes the remoter; a grandchild whose father predeceased is excluded by the deceased’s son; the Shia school allows a form of representation); the estate first pays funeral expenses, then debts, then bequests up to one-third, and the balance is divided among heirs; the female heir gets half of the male heir’s share in the same degree.

Sunni (Hanafi) heirs: (1) Sharers (Quranic heirs, ashab al-furud): husband, wife, daughter, son’s daughter, father, true grandfather, mother, true grandmother, full sister, consanguine sister, uterine sister, uterine brother; (2) Residuaries (asaba): those who take the residue (son, father, brother etc., and females made residuaries by males: daughter with son); (3) Distant kindred (dhawul arham): everyone else related by blood, in four classes.

Heir Share
Husband 1/2 if no child or child of son; 1/4 if there is a child
Wife (or wives together) 1/4 if no child; 1/8 if there is a child
Daughter 1/2 if the only child; 2/3 for two or more; with son she becomes a residuary and takes half of the son’s share
Mother 1/6 if there is a child or two or more brothers or sisters; otherwise 1/3
Father 1/6 if there is a child; otherwise a residuary
Uterine brother or sister 1/6 for one; 1/3 for two or more, shared equally

  • Doctrine of Aul (increase): if the total of the sharers’ fractions exceeds unity, each share is proportionately reduced. Doctrine of Radd (return): if there is a surplus and no residuary, the surplus returns to the sharers (except husband and wife in Hanafi law). Shia law rejects Aul and the concept of taasib; it classifies heirs in three classes (parents and children; grandparents, brothers and sisters; paternal and maternal uncles and aunts), allows representation and gives a childless widow no share in land (restricted share in immovable property).

  • Disqualifications: homicide, difference of religion (but Caste Disabilities Removal Act 1850 and Section 28 HSA principles limit this in India: convert not excluded), illegitimacy limitations, slavery (obsolete).

  • Posthumous child: counted as an heir if born within the maximum period of gestation (Hanafi 2 years; Shia 10 months).

  • Muslim Personal Law (Shariat) Application Act 1937: applies Muslim law to intestate succession notwithstanding custom (agricultural land included since 1943).

8.3Christians and Parsis: Indian Succession Act 1925#

In brief

Covers one-third, John Vallamattom v Union of India (2003), Special Marriage Act.

  • one-third
  • two-thirds
  • one-half
  • Special Marriage Act

  • Christians (Part V, Chapter II, Sections 31–49): where the intestate leaves a widow and lineal descendants, the widow takes one-third and the descendants two-thirds (Section 33); where there are no descendants but kindred, the widow takes one-half and the kindred the rest; if there are no kindred, the widow takes the whole (Section 33 and 34 pattern). A widower takes his wife’s intestate estate on a footing similar to a widow’s (Section 35: verify the exact share). Order of kindred: father, then mother and siblings, etc. (Sections 36–48). Sons and daughters take equally.

  • Parsis (Part V, Chapter III, Sections 50–56): a widow and each child take equal shares and each parent of the intestate takes half the share of a child (Section 51), with parallel rules for a female Parsi intestate (Section 52).

  • John Vallamattom v Union of India (2003): Section 118 ISA (restriction on a Christian’s bequest for religious or charitable purposes) struck down as violating Article 14 (the Court also urged a UCC).

  • Special Marriage Act: Section 21 (ISA applies) and Section 21A (HSA applies when the spouses are Hindu, Buddhist, Sikh or Jain).

8.4Historical and other points that MCQs love#

In brief

Hindu Law of Inheritance (Amendment) Act 1929: son’s daughter, daughter’s daughter, sister and sister’s son added as heirs. Hindu Women’s Rights to Property Act 1937: widow…

  • Hindu Law of Inheritance (Amendment) Act 1929: son’s daughter, daughter’s daughter, sister and sister’s son added as heirs.

  • Hindu Women’s Rights to Property Act 1937: widow got a limited estate; replaced by HSA 1956.

  • Hindu Gains of Learning Act 1930: gains of learning are separate property.

  • Marumakkattayam, Aliyasantana and Nambudri law: matrilineal systems in Kerala and Karnataka; Section 17 HSA contains special rules.

  • State amendments to Section 6: Andhra Pradesh (1986), Tamil Nadu (1989), Karnataka (1994), Maharashtra (1994).

★Memory Capsule: Succession and Inheritance

Memorise

  • Daughter is a coparcener by birth (2005), not merely a Class I heir. Father need not be alive on 9 September 2005 (Vineeta Sharma).
  • Class I heirs = 16 (12 originally, 4 added in 2005). Father is a Class II heir.
  • A Hindu female’s property: Section 15(1) order begins with sons, daughters and husband.
  • Muslim will limit: one-third; heirs’ consent for more.
  • Aul = increase (shares reduced); Radd = return (surplus returned).
  • Section 6 HSA proviso date: 20 December 2004 (partition) and 9 September 2005 (commencement).

★Analysis Angle: Succession and Inheritance

Analyse / traps

Recent trend: Vineeta Sharma (2020) and Arunachala Gounder (2022) shape all questions on women’s inheritance. The Supreme Court’s observation on the constitutionality of Sections 15–16 HSA (pending), the effect of State UCCs on inheritance (equal shares to sons and daughters; Uttarakhand’s scheme of Class I and II heirs) and the debate on equal Muslim inheritance (Shariat Act challenges) are likely to appear as current-affairs items.
