§Overview#

The “International Bill of Rights” (more formally the International Bill of Human Rights) is the core of the UN human rights system. It consists of the Universal Declaration of Human Rights (UDHR) 1948, the International Covenant on Civil and Political Rights (ICCPR) 1966 with its two Optional Protocols, and the International Covenant on Economic, Social and Cultural Rights (ICESCR) 1966 (with its Optional Protocol of 2008). The UN Commission on Human Rights under Eleanor Roosevelt originally planned a single three-part “Bill” (declaration, covenant, measures of implementation). Cold War divisions over civil-political versus socio-economic rights led to two separate covenants. This is one of the highest-yield topics in the unit because every fact in it is a number, a date or an Article.

8.1Universal Declaration of Human Rights, 1948#

In brief

Adoption and drafting

  • Adoption and drafting
  • 10 December 1948
  • Human Rights Day
  • Eleanor Roosevelt
  • René Cassin
  • Charles Malik
  • P.C. Chang
  • John Humphrey

Adoption and drafting

  • Adopted by the UN General Assembly on 10 December 1948 (Resolution 217 A (III)) at the Palais de Chaillot, Paris, by 48 votes in favour, none against and 8 abstentions (the Soviet Union, Byelorussian SSR, Ukrainian SSR, Czechoslovakia, Poland, Yugoslavia, Saudi Arabia and South Africa). Two members were absent (Honduras and Yemen). 10 December is celebrated as Human Rights Day.

  • Drafted by the Commission on Human Rights (chair: Eleanor Roosevelt). The eight-member drafting committee included Roosevelt, René Cassin (France), Charles Malik (Lebanon), P.C. Chang (China) and John Humphrey (Canada, Director of the UN Division of Human Rights, whose first draft was the working text), Hernán Santa Cruz (Chile), Alexandre Bogomolov (USSR) and Charles Dukes (UK). India’s Hansa Mehta is credited with changing “all men are born free and equal” to “all human beings are born free and equal”.

  • Structure: a Preamble and 30 Articles.

  • Legal status: a declaration, not a treaty, so not binding in itself. It has acquired strong legal significance: the 1968 Proclamation of Teheran stated that it states a common understanding of the peoples of the world and constitutes an obligation for the members of the international community; many of its provisions are regarded as customary international law; it has been incorporated into national constitutions and cited by courts (including the Indian Supreme Court).

Content by group of Articles

Articles Group Key rights
1–2 Foundations Born free and equal in dignity and rights (Art. 1); non-discrimination (Art. 2)
3–11 Individual civil rights Life, liberty and security (3); no slavery (4); no torture (5); recognition before the law (6); equality before the law (7); effective remedy (8); no arbitrary arrest (9); fair and public hearing (10); presumption of innocence and no retroactive penalty (11)
12–17 Relations to civil society Privacy (12); movement (13); asylum (14); nationality (15); marriage and family (16); property (17)
18–21 Spiritual, public and political rights Thought, conscience and religion (18); opinion and expression (19); peaceful assembly and association (20); participation in government and genuine periodic elections (21)
22–27 Economic, social and cultural rights Social security (22); work, equal pay, trade unions (23); rest and leisure (24); adequate standard of living, including food, clothing, housing and medical care (25); education (26); cultural life and protection of authors’ interests (27)
28–30 Duties and limits Entitlement to a social and international order in which rights can be realised (28); duties to the community and permissible limits only as determined by law to secure due recognition of others’ rights and the requirements of morality, public order and the general welfare in a democratic society (29); no interpretation to destroy rights (30)

Points often asked: the right to property is in the UDHR (Art. 17) but not in either Covenant; the right to asylum is in Art. 14; the right to nationality is in Art. 15; Art. 25 is the basis of the right to an adequate standard of living; Art. 29(1) is the only reference to duties.

8.2International Covenant on Civil and Political Rights, 1966#

In brief

Covers 16 December 1966, Article 1, Part II (Arts. 2–5), reports, India is not a party and more.

  • 16 December 1966
  • 23 March 1976
  • 53 Articles in six Parts
  • Article 1
  • Part II (Arts. 2–5)
  • respect and ensure
  • derogation in public emergency
  • reports

  • Adopted on 16 December 1966 (GA Resolution 2200A (XXI)); in force 23 March 1976 (three months after the 35th ratification). It has a preamble and 53 Articles in six Parts.

  • Article 1: right of all peoples to self-determination (common to ICCPR and ICESCR); permanent sovereignty over natural wealth and resources.

  • Part II (Arts. 2–5): States undertake to respect and ensure the rights to all individuals within their territory and subject to their jurisdiction (Art. 2(1)); effective remedy (Art. 2(3)); equal rights of men and women (Art. 3); derogation in public emergency (Art. 4), with no derogation from Arts. 6, 7, 8 (paragraphs 1 and 2), 11, 15, 16 and 18.

  • Part III (Arts. 6–27), the substantive rights:

Article Right
6 Inherent right to life; death penalty only for the most serious crimes, not for those below 18 or pregnant women
7 Freedom from torture and cruel, inhuman or degrading treatment; no medical or scientific experimentation without free consent
8 Freedom from slavery, servitude and forced labour
9 Liberty and security of person; arrest only on grounds established by law; right to compensation for unlawful arrest (9(5))
10 Humane treatment of persons deprived of liberty
11 No imprisonment merely for inability to fulfil a contractual obligation
12–13 Liberty of movement; procedural safeguards on expulsion of aliens
14 Fair trial; presumption of innocence; compensation for miscarriage of justice (14(6)); protection from double jeopardy (14(7))
15 No retrospective criminal penalties
16 Recognition as a person before the law
17 Privacy, family, home and correspondence
18 Freedom of thought, conscience and religion
19 Freedom of opinion and expression
20 Prohibition of propaganda for war and of advocacy of national, racial or religious hatred
21–22 Peaceful assembly; association including trade unions
23 Marriage and family
24 Rights of the child (protection, registration, name and nationality)
25 Participation in public affairs, voting, access to public service
26 Equality before the law and equal protection
27 Rights of ethnic, religious and linguistic minorities

  • Human Rights Committee (Part IV, Arts. 28–45): 18 independent experts, elected for four-year terms, who serve in their personal capacity. Functions: examining periodic reports by States (Art. 40); issuing General Comments; considering inter-State complaints (Art. 41, optional); and, under the First Optional Protocol, individual communications.

  • First Optional Protocol (1966, in force 1976): individual communications against States Parties for alleged violations of ICCPR rights, after exhaustion of domestic remedies. India is not a party.

  • Second Optional Protocol (1989, in force 1991): aims at the abolition of the death penalty; the only permitted reservation relates to wartime crimes of a military nature.

  • India: acceded to both the ICCPR and the ICESCR on 10 April 1979, with declarations (for example that the right to self-determination in Art. 1 applies to peoples under foreign domination and not to sovereign States, and that Arts. 9, 13, 19, 21 and 22 are to be applied consistently with the Constitution; and that the right to compensation for unlawful arrest in Art. 9(5) is not enforceable in India as a legal right in the manner of the Covenant).

8.3International Covenant on Economic, Social and Cultural Rights, 1966#

In brief

Covers 16 December 1966, Article 1, Monitoring, Optional Protocol to ICESCR, Limburg Principles 1986.

  • 16 December 1966
  • 3 January 1976
  • 31 Articles in five Parts
  • Article 1
  • Article 2(1)
  • progressively
  • Art. 2(2)
  • Art. 3

  • Adopted on 16 December 1966; in force 3 January 1976. It has a preamble and 31 Articles in five Parts.

  • Article 1: self-determination. Article 2(1): each State undertakes to take steps, individually and through international assistance and cooperation, to the maximum of its available resources, with a view to achieving progressively the full realisation of the rights; Art. 2(2): non-discrimination; Art. 3: equal right of men and women; Art. 4: limitations only as determined by law and solely for promoting the general welfare in a democratic society.

Article Right
6 Right to work
7 Just and favourable conditions of work, fair wages and equal pay
8 Trade unions, the right to strike
9 Social security
10 Protection of the family, mothers and children
11 Adequate standard of living including food, clothing and housing; freedom from hunger
12 Highest attainable standard of physical and mental health
13–14 Education (primary compulsory and free)
15 Cultural life; benefits of scientific progress; protection of authors’ moral and material interests

  • Monitoring: the Committee on Economic, Social and Cultural Rights (18 experts) was established by ECOSOC in 1985 (it is not created by the Covenant itself). It reviews State reports and issues General Comments (for example General Comment 3 on the nature of State obligations, with the concept of a minimum core obligation).

  • Optional Protocol to ICESCR adopted 10 December 2008, in force 5 May 2013: individual communications, inter-State communications, and an inquiry procedure.

  • Interpretation guides: Limburg Principles 1986, Maastricht Guidelines 1997, Maastricht Principles 2011 (extraterritorial obligations).

8.6The Bill of Rights in Indian courts#

In brief

Gramophone Co. of India v. Birendra Bahadur Pandey (1984): national courts should interpret municipal law consistently with international law where possible. Jolly George…

  • Gramophone Co. of India v. Birendra Bahadur Pandey (1984): national courts should interpret municipal law consistently with international law where possible.

  • Jolly George Varghese v. Bank of Cochin (1980): ICCPR Art. 11 used to read down imprisonment for debt.

  • Chairman, Railway Board v. Chandrima Das (2000): the UDHR and its principles were applied to uphold the rights of a foreign national who was a rape victim at a railway guest house.

  • People’s Union for Civil Liberties v. Union of India (1997): telephone tapping and privacy, with reference to Art. 17 ICCPR and Art. 12 UDHR.

  • Vishaka v. State of Rajasthan (1997): CEDAW used to frame sexual harassment guidelines in the absence of legislation.

  • Nilabati Behera v. State of Orissa (1993): compensation for custodial death; reference to ICCPR Art. 9(5).

  • Socio-economic rights under Art. 21: education (Unni Krishnan, 1993), health (Paschim Banga Khet Mazdoor Samity, 1996), shelter (Olga Tellis, 1985), food (PUCL v. Union of India, 2001).

★Memory Capsule: International Bill of Rights

Memorise

  • UDHR: 1948, 48-0-8, 30 Articles; Covenants: 1966, in force 1976; ICCPR 53 Articles, ICESCR 31.
  • Optional Protocols: ICCPR OP1 = complaints (1966), OP2 = death penalty (1989), ICESCR OP = 2008.
  • Human Rights Committee: 18 members; CESCR: 18 members, set up by ECOSOC 1985.
