1.1Meaning#

In brief

Intellectual property (IP) refers to creations of the human intellect to which the law grants exclusive, time-bound (or, in some cases, renewable) rights that allow the…

Intellectual property (IP) refers to creations of the human intellect to which the law grants exclusive, time-bound (or, in some cases, renewable) rights that allow the creator or owner to exclude others from exploiting the creation without permission. The property is intangible (incorporeal): the right is not in the physical object but in the idea-expression, invention, mark or know-how embodied in it. Buying a book gives you ownership of the copy, not the copyright; buying a patented machine does not give you the patent.

The classic definition is in Article 2(viii) of the WIPO Convention 1967, which lists IP as rights relating to: literary, artistic and scientific works; performances of performing artists, phonograms and broadcasts; inventions in all fields of human endeavour; scientific discoveries; industrial designs; trademarks, service marks and commercial names and designations; protection against unfair competition; and all other rights resulting from intellectual activity in the industrial, scientific, literary or artistic fields.

Industrial property vs copyright. Industrial property (patents, trademarks, designs, GIs, unfair competition) is governed internationally by the Paris Convention; copyright and related rights by the Berne Convention and the Rome Convention.

1.3Essential features of IP rights#

In brief

Covers Intangible and incorporeal, Territorial, Exclusive but limited, Negative right, Freely transferable and more.

  • Intangible and incorporeal
  • Territorial
  • Exclusive but limited
  • Negative right
  • Freely transferable
  • Registration
  • optional

  • Intangible and incorporeal: the same creation can be used simultaneously by many people, which is why law must create artificial scarcity.

  • Territorial: rights exist only in the country that grants them (an Indian patent has no effect in the US). The Paris and Berne principles of national treatment reduce the harshness of this.

  • Exclusive but limited: limited in time (except trademarks, GIs and trade secrets), in scope (fair dealing, compulsory licences, the Bolar exception) and by public interest.

  • Negative right: primarily a right to exclude, not a right to practise. You may hold a patent on an improvement whose use still infringes someone else's earlier patent.

  • Freely transferable (assignment, licence, mortgage), except GI rights (Section 24 GI Act) and moral rights.

  • Registration: mandatory for patents, designs, registered trademarks and GIs; optional for copyright (Section 45 registration is only prima facie evidence under Section 48).

1.4Constitutional and institutional framework in India#

In brief

Covers Entry 49, List I (Union List), Article 300A, Article 21, Article 51(c), Administration and more.

  • Entry 49, List I (Union List)
  • Article 300A
  • Article 21
  • Article 39(b)
  • Article 51(c)
  • Administration
  • Mumbai
  • Chennai

  • Entry 49, List I (Union List): "Patents, inventions and designs; copyright; trade-marks and merchandise marks." Parliament has exclusive power over these subjects.

  • Article 300A (right to property) has been held to include IP. In Entertainment Network (India) Ltd v. Super Cassette Industries Ltd (2008, SC), the Court recognised the balance between the copyright owner's property right and the public interest in access.

  • Article 21 (health) and Article 39(b) (distribution of material resources) are invoked to justify limits on pharmaceutical patents (the Section 3(d) and compulsory licensing debate).

  • Article 51(c) and Entry 14, List I: treaty-making and implementing international obligations (TRIPS compliance).

  • Administration: the Department for Promotion of Industry and Internal Trade (DPIIT, Ministry of Commerce) is the nodal department. The Office of the Controller General of Patents, Designs and Trade Marks (CGPDTM), headquartered in Mumbai, oversees the patent offices (Kolkata, Delhi, Mumbai, Chennai), the Trade Marks Registry (Mumbai, with branch offices including Delhi, Kolkata, Chennai and Ahmedabad) and the Geographical Indications Registry (Chennai). The Copyright Office and the Registrar of Copyrights are in New Delhi (Department-level administration under DPIIT).

  • National IPR Policy 2016 (slogan "Creative India; Innovative India"): seven objectives including IPR awareness, creation of IPRs, legal framework, administration, commercialisation, enforcement and human capital. CIPAM (Cell for IPR Promotion and Management) implements it.

  • Dispute forums: The Intellectual Property Appellate Board (IPAB), established under the Trade Marks Act 1999, was abolished by the Tribunals Reforms Act 2021. Appeals formerly heard by IPAB now go to the High Courts (Delhi High Court has a dedicated IP Division, 2021). Infringement suits go to the District Court/High Court and are commercial disputes under the Commercial Courts Act 2015.

1.5Exhaustion of rights (important concept question)#

In brief

The doctrine of exhaustion (first-sale doctrine) says that once the owner sells a product lawfully, the right to control its further resale ends.

  • doctrine of exhaustion
  • international exhaustion

The doctrine of exhaustion (first-sale doctrine) says that once the owner sells a product lawfully, the right to control its further resale ends. Three models: national (only sales within the country exhaust the right), regional (e.g. EU) and international (sale anywhere exhausts the right). India follows international exhaustion for patents (Section 107A(b), parallel imports) and, through Section 30(3)–(4) of the Trade Marks Act, for trademarks (Kapil Wadhwa v. Samsung Electronics, Delhi HC 2012). Parallel imports in copyright are restricted under Section 51(b)(iv) (import of infringing copies). TRIPS Article 6 leaves exhaustion to each member.
