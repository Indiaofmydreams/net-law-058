§Overview#

Theories justify why the law should grant exclusive rights over intangible creations. UGC NET asks mainly: which thinker, which theory, and what is its core idea (and sometimes which theory best explains a particular doctrine). Learn the table first, then read the explanations.

Theory Key thinkers Core idea Best explains
Natural rights / Labour theory John Locke A person owns the fruits of their labour; mixing labour with the common creates property Copyright and patents as reward for effort; "sweat of the brow"
Personality theory Kant, Hegel (modern: Margaret Radin, Justin Hughes) Property is an extension of personality; creative works embody the creator's self Moral rights (paternity, integrity); droit d'auteur tradition
Utilitarian / Incentive theory Jeremy Bentham, J.S. Mill IP rights give incentive to create and invest; maximises social welfare Patents, limited-term copyright, TRIPS rationale
Economic/Public goods theory Modern law and economics (Landes & Posner) Creations are non-rival and non-excludable, so free-riding destroys incentives unless law creates exclusivity Why IP exists at all; why terms are limited
Social planning theory William Fisher IP should be used to build a just and attractive culture Fair use, compulsory licences, public interest limits
Prospect theory Edmund Kitch Patent grant is like a mining claim that lets the patentee coordinate development of a technology Early patent filing, scope of claims
Contract / Social bargain theory Classical patent theory Inventor discloses the invention; society grants a temporary monopoly in exchange Disclosure requirement, sufficiency of specification, term limit
Reward theory Associated with natural-law tradition Society owes the creator a reward for benefit conferred Remuneration claims, royalties
Tragedy of the anticommons Michael Heller Too many overlapping exclusive rights block use of resources Patent thickets, licensing bottlenecks

2.1Labour (natural rights) theory: Locke#

In brief

In his Second Treatise of Government (1690), Locke argued that God gave the world to humankind in common, but a person owns his body and therefore his labour.

  • "enough and as good" proviso
  • spoilage limitation

In his Second Treatise of Government (1690), Locke argued that God gave the world to humankind in common, but a person owns his body and therefore his labour. When labour is mixed with a thing in the common, it becomes the labourer's property. Applied to IP, the author who labours to compose a work or the inventor who toils to create an invention deserves ownership. Two limits are famous: the "enough and as good" proviso (appropriation is justified only if enough and as good is left in common for others) and the spoilage limitation. Critics say ideas are not scarce, so the proviso is hard to apply, and that labour theory cannot justify a time-limited right. The theory underlies the old UK "sweat of the brow" standard (University of London Press v. University Tutorial Press, 1916), later displaced in India by the "modicum of creativity" standard (Eastern Book Company v. D.B. Modak, 2008).

2.2Personality theory: Kant and Hegel#

In brief

Kant treated a book as an act of speech by the author to the public, an expression of personality that cannot be fully alienated.

  • moral rights

Kant treated a book as an act of speech by the author to the public, an expression of personality that cannot be fully alienated. Hegel argued that property is the embodiment of the will and personality of the owner. Radin's "personhood" version distinguishes personal property (closely tied to self-development) from fungible property. This theory explains moral rights: the rights of paternity and integrity that survive assignment of copyright (Section 57, Copyright Act; Article 6bis Berne). It is dominant in civil-law countries (France, Germany), where author's rights (droit d'auteur) are central, while the common-law tradition leans utilitarian.

2.3Utilitarian and economic theories#

In brief

Bentham's maxim "the greatest happiness of the greatest number" supports IP only where benefits of creativity exceed the costs of monopoly.

  • incentive theory

Bentham's maxim "the greatest happiness of the greatest number" supports IP only where benefits of creativity exceed the costs of monopoly. The economic form is the incentive theory: information is a public good (non-rival: my use does not diminish yours; non-excludable: hard to stop copying), so without legal exclusivity the creator cannot recover costs and too little will be created. This theory explains why terms are limited, why fair dealing and compulsory licences exist, and why the US Constitution grants Congress power "to promote the progress of science and useful arts." TRIPS Article 7 (objectives: "mutual advantage of producers and users of technological knowledge") is explicitly utilitarian. Criticisms: it rests on assumptions that are hard to measure, and it cannot explain moral rights.

2.4Social planning theory and the commons#

In brief

Fisher's social planning theory asks that IP be used as a tool to achieve a just culture, giving weight to democratic participation, diversity and access.

  • public-interest limits
  • commons
  • anticommons

Fisher's social planning theory asks that IP be used as a tool to achieve a just culture, giving weight to democratic participation, diversity and access. It underpins public-interest limits: Sections 31–31D (compulsory/statutory licences) and Section 52 (fair dealing) of the Copyright Act, and Sections 84 and 92 (compulsory licences) of the Patents Act. The commons and anticommons arguments warn that over-protection harms follow-on innovation.

2.5Related concepts examiners like#

In brief

Covers Idea–expression dichotomy, Originality tests, Rights vs welfare, Public domain.

  • Idea–expression dichotomy
  • Originality tests
  • Rights vs welfare
  • Public domain

  • Idea–expression dichotomy: copyright protects expression, not ideas (R.G. Anand v. Delux Films, SC 1978).

  • Originality tests: sweat of the brow (UK, old) vs modicum of creativity (US Feist v. Rural, 1991; India Eastern Book Company, 2008).

  • Rights vs welfare: natural-law and personality theories are rights-based (deontological); utilitarian and economic theories are consequence-based.

  • Public domain: works whose protection has expired or never existed, free for all to use.
