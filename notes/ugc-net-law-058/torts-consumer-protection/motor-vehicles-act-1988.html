9.1Overview#

In brief

The Motor Vehicles Act 1988 (MVA) consolidates the law on motor vehicles: licensing, registration, traffic control, no-fault compensation (Chapter X), compulsory third-party…

  • Motor Vehicles Act 1988 (MVA)
  • no-fault compensation
  • 1 September 2019

The Motor Vehicles Act 1988 (MVA) consolidates the law on motor vehicles: licensing, registration, traffic control, no-fault compensation (Chapter X), compulsory third-party insurance (Chapter XI) and Motor Accident Claims Tribunals (MACT) (Chapter XII). It was substantially amended by the Motor Vehicles (Amendment) Act 2019 (many provisions in force from 1 September 2019). The policy is that road-accident victims must receive speedy and certain compensation without the ordeal of proving negligence.

Section numbers below follow the commonly cited text; because the 2019 amendment rearranged some provisions, verify section numbering against the current bare Act where an exam question turns on a number.

9.2No-fault liability#

In brief

Principle: compensation is payable on death or permanent disablement irrespective of any wrongful act, neglect or default of the owner or any other person.

  • Principle
  • strict liability
  • ₹ 50,000
  • ₹ 25,000
  • Section 141
  • adjusted
  • Section 142
  • permanent disablement

Principle: compensation is payable on death or permanent disablement irrespective of any wrongful act, neglect or default of the owner or any other person. It is an exception to the fault principle, similar in spirit to strict liability.

  • Section 140 (as it stood after the 1994 amendment): the owner is liable to pay ₹ 50,000 for death and ₹ 25,000 for permanent disablement; the claimant need not plead or prove wrongful act or negligence. (The original 1988 figures were ₹ 25,000 and ₹ 12,500.)

  • Section 141: the right to claim under other laws is unaffected, but no-fault compensation paid is adjusted against the final award.

  • Section 142: meaning of permanent disablement; Section 143: application of the Chapter to claims under the Workmen's Compensation Act (now Employees' Compensation Act); Section 144: overriding effect.

  • 2019 amendment (Section 164): no-fault compensation of ₹ 5 lakh for death and ₹ 2.5 lakh for grievous hurt (verify the current section arrangement).

  • Section 163A (structured formula): compensation in death or permanent disablement cases according to the Second Schedule (based on age and income), where the claimant need not prove negligence; applicable to persons with annual income up to ₹ 40,000. Deepal Girishbhai Soni v United India Insurance (2004): remedies under Section 163A and Section 166 are mutually exclusive; the claimant must choose. Ningamma v United India Insurance (2009): the owner/borrower who is driving is not a third party for Section 163A.

  • Section 161 (hit-and-run): compensation from the Solatium Fund. Earlier ₹ 25,000 (death) and ₹ 12,500 (grievous hurt); the 2019 amendment raised these to ₹ 2 lakh and ₹ 50,000.

  • Section 164B (2019): the Motor Vehicle Accident Fund for treatment and compensation of road-accident victims, with compulsory insurance for all road users.

9.3Compulsory third-party insurance (Chapter XI)#

In brief

Covers Section 146, Section 147, Section 149, Section 150, Key cases and more.

  • Section 146
  • policy of insurance
  • Section 147
  • death or bodily injury
  • damage to third-party property
  • unlimited
  • Section 149
  • Section 149(2)

  • Section 146: necessity for insurance against third-party risks: no person shall use a motor vehicle in a public place unless a policy of insurance complying with the Chapter is in force (with limited exemptions for government vehicles).

  • Section 147: requirements of policies and limits of liability. The policy must cover liability for death or bodily injury to any person (including owners of goods or their representatives carried in the vehicle) and for damage to third-party property. Liability for death and injury is unlimited; property damage is subject to a prescribed limit.

  • Section 149: duty of insurers to satisfy judgments and awards in respect of third-party risks, even if the insurer is entitled to avoid the policy, subject to the limited defences in Section 149(2) (breach of specified conditions such as use for hire or reward, or use by an unlicensed driver). The insurer then has the right to recover from the insured (the "pay and recover" principle).

  • Section 150: rights of third parties against insurers on the insolvency of the insured.

  • Key cases: Skandia Insurance Co v Kokilaben Chandravadan (1987): an insurer cannot escape its liability to the third party through policy exclusions; National Insurance Co v Swaran Singh (2004): the insurer must prove that the breach was fundamental and that the owner was aware of it (e.g., fake driving licence); otherwise it must pay the third party and may recover from the owner; Oriental Insurance v Meena Variyal (2007).

  • Penalty for driving without insurance (Section 196): imprisonment up to three months, or fine up to ₹ 2,000, or both (as amended in 2019).

9.4Motor Accident Claims Tribunals (Chapter XII)#

In brief

Covers Section 165, Section 166, Section 167, Section 168, Section 169 and more.

  • Section 165
  • Claims Tribunals
  • Section 166
  • application for compensation
  • injured person
  • owner of damaged property
  • agent
  • fault-based

  • Section 165: the State Government may constitute Claims Tribunals to adjudicate claims for compensation arising from accidents (death, bodily injury, damage to third-party property).

  • Section 166: application for compensation by (a) the injured person, (b) the owner of damaged property, (c) all or any legal representatives of the deceased, or (d) a duly authorised agent. It may be made to the Tribunal where the accident occurred, or where the claimant resides or carries on business, or where the defendant resides. A Section 166 claim is fault-based, so the claimant must prove negligence.

    • 2019 amendment: the six-month limitation in Section 166(3) was omitted, so there is no limitation period; the Detailed Accident Report filed by the police may be treated as the claim application.

  • Section 167: option to claim under the Workmen's/Employees' Compensation Act or the MVA, not both.

  • Section 168: the Tribunal's award must be just compensation, and it specifies whether the insurer, owner or driver pays.

  • Section 169: summary procedure, not bound by strict rules of evidence. Section 170: impleading the insurer. Section 171: interest. Section 172: compensatory costs for false or vexatious claims or defences.

  • Section 173: appeal to the High Court within 90 days; an insurer's appeal requires a deposit of ₹ 25,000 or 50 percent of the amount awarded, whichever is less.

  • Section 174: recovery as arrears of land revenue. Section 175: bar on civil court jurisdiction over matters that the Tribunal can decide.

  • Reporting duties: the police must prepare a Detailed Accident Report within a short time (48 hours for the interim report under the 2019 scheme) and forward it to the Tribunal.

9.5Determination of compensation: principles from cases#

In brief

Covers multipliers, future prospects, parental and filial, Contributory negligence, owner is vicariously liable.

  • multipliers
  • future prospects
  • permanent job
  • self-employed or fixed-salary
  • Conventional heads
  • ₹ 15,000
  • ₹ 40,000
  • 10% every three years

  • Sarla Verma v Delhi Transport Corporation (2009): standard multipliers by age and deduction for personal expenses (one-third for 2–3 dependants; one-fourth for 4–6; one-fifth for more than 6; one-half for a bachelor).

  • National Insurance Co v Pranay Sethi (2017) (Constitution Bench): upheld Sarla Verma; future prospects must be added: for a person with a permanent job 50% (below 40 years), 30% (40–50), 15% (50–60); for self-employed or fixed-salary persons 40%, 25%, 10%; none above 60. Conventional heads: loss of estate ₹ 15,000, funeral expenses ₹ 15,000, loss of consortium ₹ 40,000, increasing by 10% every three years.

  • Magma General Insurance v Nanu Ram (2018): loss of consortium includes parental and filial consortium, not only spousal.

  • R.D. Hattangadi v Pest Control (India) (1995): heads of compensation in personal injury (pecuniary and non-pecuniary: pain and suffering, loss of amenities, loss of expectation of life).

  • Contributory negligence reduces compensation proportionately; in composite negligence the claimant may recover from any of the joint tortfeasors.

  • The owner is vicariously liable for the driver's negligence in the course of employment (Pushpabai Purshottam Udeshi v Ranjit Ginning, 1977); res ipsa loquitur is also invoked.

9.6The Motor Vehicles (Amendment) Act 2019: key changes (trend)#

In brief

Covers Higher penalties, Good Samaritan protection (Section 134A), "Golden hour", Motor Vehicle Accident Fund, aggregators.

  • Higher penalties
  • juvenile
  • not liable
  • "Golden hour"
  • cashless treatment
  • Motor Vehicle Accident Fund
  • removal of limitation
  • aggregators

  • Higher penalties for traffic offences; where a juvenile commits an offence, the guardian or owner is deemed guilty and the vehicle registration may be cancelled.

  • Good Samaritan protection (Section 134A): a person who voluntarily helps an accident victim is not liable to civil or criminal action.

  • "Golden hour" (Section 2(12A)): the hour following a traumatic injury; cashless treatment during the golden hour (Section 162). A national cashless-treatment scheme has been framed in recent years (verify the latest notified scheme).

  • Motor Vehicle Accident Fund (Section 164B); enhanced hit-and-run compensation; removal of limitation for claims.

  • Provisions on aggregators, recall of defective vehicles, digital licensing and road safety.
