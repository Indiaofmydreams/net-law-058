1.1Meaning and origin#

In brief

The word tort comes from the Latin tortum , meaning "twisted" or "wrong", and came into English through Norman French.

  • civil wrong
  • unliquidated damages
  • uncodified and judge-made
  • Limitation Act 1963

The word tort comes from the Latin tortum, meaning "twisted" or "wrong", and came into English through Norman French. A tort is a civil wrong for which the law provides a remedy, typically in the form of unliquidated damages (damages not fixed in advance, left to the court to assess). It protects interests such as bodily integrity, reputation, property, liberty, and economic interests.

In India the law of torts is largely uncodified and judge-made, drawing on English common law, modified by Indian conditions (including constitutional values), with statutes covering specific areas (Consumer Protection Act, Motor Vehicles Act, Competition Act, etc.). Section 2(m) of the Limitation Act 1963 defines tort in a negative way: "a civil wrong which is not exclusively a breach of contract or the breach of a trust."

1.2Key definitions (frequently matched with authors)#

In brief

Covers Winfield, Salmond, Fraser, Underhill/others.

  • Winfield
  • Salmond
  • Fraser
  • Underhill/others
  • Memory hook

  • Winfield: Tortious liability arises from the breach of a duty primarily fixed by law; this duty is towards persons generally, and its breach is redressible by an action for unliquidated damages. Key points: duty fixed by law, owed to persons generally, unliquidated damages.

  • Salmond: A civil wrong for which the remedy is a common law action for unliquidated damages, and which is not exclusively the breach of a contract or the breach of a trust or other merely equitable obligation.

  • Fraser: An infringement of a right in rem of a private individual giving a right of compensation at the suit of the injured party.

  • Underhill/others: Emphasise that tort is breach of a duty fixed by law.

Memory hook: Winfield → duty primarily fixed by law, towards persons generally. Salmond → civil wrong, not exclusively contract or trust. Fraser → right in rem.

1.3Essential elements of a tort#

In brief

Covers A wrongful act or omission, Legal damage, A legal remedy.

  • A wrongful act or omission
  • Legal damage
  • actionable per se
  • A legal remedy

  1. A wrongful act or omission by the defendant (conduct which the law treats as wrongful, whether intentional, negligent, or sometimes without fault, as in strict liability).

  2. Legal damage to the plaintiff, meaning violation of a legal right. Some torts need proof of actual damage (negligence, nuisance); others are actionable per se (trespass, libel).

  3. A legal remedy in the form of an action for damages (or other relief such as injunction).

1.4Foundational maxims#

In brief

Covers Ubi jus ibi remedium, Injuria sine damno, Damnum sine injuria.

  • Ubi jus ibi remedium
  • Injuria sine damno
  • Damnum sine injuria
  • Concept trap

  • Ubi jus ibi remedium: where there is a right, there is a remedy. The foundation of tort law; Ashby v White (1703) illustrates it (voter wrongfully denied vote could sue though no loss was proved).

  • Injuria sine damno: violation of a legal right without actual loss; actionable. Cases: Ashby v White; in India, Bhim Singh v State of J&K; the classic Indian illustration often cited is Rogers v Rajendro Dutt (1860).

  • Damnum sine injuria: actual loss without violation of a legal right; not actionable. Cases: Gloucester Grammar School v Bradford (teacher opening rival school; students left), Mogul Steamship Co v McGregor Gow & Co (lawful competition), Acton v Blundell and Chasemore v Richards (abstracting underground water), Bradford Corporation v Pickles (motive irrelevant when exercising a lawful right), Town Area Committee v Prabhu Dayal (Indian, demolition of unauthorized construction).

Concept trap: Do not confuse damnum (loss) with injuria (legal wrong). Damnum sine injuria is the one that is NOT actionable.

1.5Mental element: intention, motive, malice#

In brief

Covers Intention, Motive, Malice, Negligence.

  • Intention
  • Motive
  • irrelevant
  • Malice
  • nuisance
  • malicious prosecution
  • conspiracy
  • defamation

  • Intention: the desire to bring about a consequence, or knowledge that it is substantially certain.

  • Motive: the underlying reason; generally irrelevant in tort (Bradford Corporation v Pickles; Allen v Flood), because a lawful act does not become unlawful merely because of a bad motive.

  • Malice (in law: wrongful act done intentionally without just cause) matters in a few torts: nuisance (Christie v Davey, deliberate banging to annoy neighbours; Hollywood Silver Fox Farm v Emmett), malicious prosecution, conspiracy (Quinn v Leathem), defamation (defeats qualified privilege and fair comment), and malicious falsehood.

  • Negligence: carelessness in breach of a duty; Recklessness: conscious disregard of risk.

1.6Theories of liability: pigeon-hole vs general principle#

In brief

Covers Salmond's "pigeon-hole" theory, Winfield's general theory, Position today.

  • Salmond's "pigeon-hole" theory
  • Winfield's general theory
  • Position today

  • Salmond's "pigeon-hole" theory: Law of Torts. There are only specific named torts (assault, battery, defamation, nuisance etc.). If a wrong does not fit a recognised category, no liability.

  • Winfield's general theory: Law of Tort. There is a general principle that every harm caused by wrongful conduct is actionable unless justified, so new torts can develop.

  • Position today: The courts are closer to Winfield; the categories remain open, as shown by the growth of negligence (Donoghue v Stevenson), privacy-related claims, and constitutional torts in India.

1.7Tort distinguished from other concepts#

In brief

Tort vs Crime

  • Tort vs Crime
  • Tort vs Breach of contract
  • Tort vs Breach of trust
  • Tort vs Quasi-contract

Tort vs Crime

  • Tort: private wrong; remedy is compensation; action by injured party; no necessity of mens rea in all torts; no codified list.

  • Crime: public wrong; remedy is punishment; prosecution by State; mens rea normally required; defined in statute (now the Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita 2023).

  • Same act may be both (assault, defamation, negligent driving causing death, nuisance). Rule in Smith v Selwyn, once barred civil suits for felony until prosecution; not followed in modern law.

Tort vs Breach of contract

  • Contract: duty arises from agreement of parties, owed to specific person (privity), damages may be liquidated, and there is a right to enforce promised performance.

  • Tort: duty fixed by law, owed to persons generally, damages unliquidated.

  • Overlap: a doctor, carrier, or bailee may be liable in both. Donoghue v Stevenson removed the privity limitation that Winterbottom v Wright imposed.

Tort vs Breach of trust: breach of trust is an equitable obligation remedied by equity; tort is a common law wrong.

Tort vs Quasi-contract: quasi-contract (Sections 68–72, Indian Contract Act 1872) is based on unjust enrichment, not wrongdoing.

1.8Remedies in tort#

In brief

Covers Judicial remedies, Injunction, Specific restitution of property, Extra-judicial remedies (self-help).

  • Judicial remedies
  • Damages
  • Injunction
  • recaption
  • abatement of nuisance
  • distress damage feasant
  • Indian development
  • public law compensation

  1. Judicial remedies

    • Damages: nominal (right violated, no loss, e.g., Constantine v Imperial Hotels), contemptuous (technical right but claim is worthless), substantial/compensatory, aggravated (injury to feelings), exemplary/punitive (only in limited categories laid down in Rookes v Devlin (1964): oppressive, arbitrary or unconstitutional action by government servants; conduct calculated to make profit exceeding compensation; where authorised by statute; confirmed in Cassell v Broome).

    • Injunction: temporary or perpetual, prohibitory or mandatory (nuisance, trespass, defamation).

    • Specific restitution of property (recovery of goods or land).

  2. Extra-judicial remedies (self-help)

    • Self-defence, prevention of trespass, re-entry on land, recaption of goods, abatement of nuisance, distress damage feasant (seizing trespassing cattle until compensation is paid), and expulsion of trespasser.

Indian development: constitutional courts award public law compensation under Articles 32 and 226 for violation of Article 21 (e.g., Rudul Sah, Nilabati Behera); this is distinct from private law tort damages (discussed in Topic 7).

1.9Discharge and survival of tort liability#

In brief

Covers Death.

  • Death
  • except

  • Death: historical maxim actio personalis moritur cum persona (personal action dies with the person). In India, Section 306, Indian Succession Act 1925 provides that causes of action survive to the executor, except defamation, assault, and personal injuries not causing death of the party (and also where the party dies in other personal injury cases).

  • Waiver / Accord and satisfaction / Release / Limitation (Limitation Act 1963 prescribes periods; for example, one year for defamation, malicious prosecution, false imprisonment; three years for general torts like conversion).
