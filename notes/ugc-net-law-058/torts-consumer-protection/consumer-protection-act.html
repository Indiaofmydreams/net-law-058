8.1Critical note on the syllabus#

In brief

The syllabus names the Consumer Protection Act 1986 (CPA 1986) .

  • 20 July 2020
  • 2019 Act

The syllabus names the Consumer Protection Act 1986 (CPA 1986). That Act was repealed and replaced by the Consumer Protection Act 2019 (CPA 2019), which came into force on 20 July 2020 (Consumer Protection Rules 2020 followed on 24 July 2020). Many MCQs now test the 2019 Act directly or contrast the two. Learn both: first the 1986 framework, then the 2019 upgrades.

8.2Background and objectives#

In brief

The CPA 1986 was enacted for the better protection of the interests of consumers and the establishment of consumer councils and authorities for the settlement of consumer…

  • in addition to
  • social-welfare legislation

The CPA 1986 was enacted for the better protection of the interests of consumers and the establishment of consumer councils and authorities for the settlement of consumer disputes. The remedy is in addition to, and not in derogation of, other remedies (Section 3 of the 1986 Act; Section 100 of the 2019 Act). It is social-welfare legislation and is interpreted liberally.

8.3Key definitions under the CPA 1986 (Section 2)#

In brief

Covers Consumer, Complainant, Complaint, Defect, Deficiency and more.

  • Consumer
  • buys goods
  • not
  • hires or avails of any service
  • Free services
  • contract of personal service
  • Complainant
  • Complaint

  • Consumer (2(1)(d)): (i) a person who buys goods for a consideration (paid, promised, partly paid or under a deferred payment system), including a user of the goods with the approval of the buyer, but not a person who obtains goods for resale or any commercial purpose; (ii) a person who hires or avails of any service for a consideration, including a beneficiary with the approval of the hirer, excluding services availed for a commercial purpose. Free services and services under a contract of personal service are excluded.

  • Complainant (2(1)(b)): a consumer; a registered voluntary consumer association; the Central/State Government; one or more consumers having the same interest; or the legal heir/representative of a deceased consumer.

  • Complaint (2(1)(c)): a written allegation of an unfair or restrictive trade practice, defective goods, deficiency in service, excess price charged, or hazardous goods offered for sale.

  • Defect (2(1)(f)): any fault, imperfection or shortcoming in the quality, quantity, potency, purity or standard required to be maintained by law or claimed by the trader.

  • Deficiency (2(1)(g)): any fault, imperfection, shortcoming or inadequacy in the quality, nature and manner of performance required by law or undertaken by contract in relation to a service.

  • Service (2(1)(o)): service of any description made available to potential users, including banking, financing, insurance, transport, processing, supply of electrical or other energy, telecom, boarding or lodging, entertainment, amusement, or purveying of news or other information; not free services or contracts of personal service.

  • Unfair trade practice (2(1)(r)): false representation, misleading advertisement, false claims about standard or quality, offering gifts or prizes not intended to be given, non-compliance with prescribed standards, hoarding, etc.

  • Restrictive trade practice (2(1)(nnn)): a practice that tends to manipulate price or conditions of delivery or to affect the flow of supplies in an unjust manner (e.g., tie-in sale).

8.4Consumer rights (Section 6, CPA 1986; Section 2(9), CPA 2019)#

In brief

Drawn from President Kennedy's 1962 Consumer Bill of Rights and the UN Guidelines for Consumer Protection (1985):

  • Right to safety
  • Right to be informed
  • Right to choose
  • Right to be heard
  • Right to seek redressal
  • Right to consumer education

Drawn from President Kennedy's 1962 Consumer Bill of Rights and the UN Guidelines for Consumer Protection (1985):

  1. Right to safety: protection against marketing of goods and services hazardous to life and property.

  2. Right to be informed: about quality, quantity, potency, purity, standard and price.

  3. Right to choose: access to a variety of goods and services at competitive prices.

  4. Right to be heard: consumer interests receive due consideration at appropriate fora.

  5. Right to seek redressal: against unfair or restrictive trade practices or unscrupulous exploitation.

  6. Right to consumer education.

The 2019 Act defines "consumer rights" in Section 2(9) in similar terms (protection from hazardous goods and services, information, assured access at competitive prices, to be heard, to seek redressal, and consumer awareness).

8.5Redressal machinery under the CPA 1986#

In brief

Three-tier quasi-judicial system:

  • District Forum
  • ₹ 20 lakh
  • State Commission
  • ₹ 1 crore
  • National Commission
  • Appeal to the Supreme Court
  • Limitation
  • Consumer Protection Councils

Three-tier quasi-judicial system:

  • District Forum: headed by a person who is, or has been, or is qualified to be a District Judge, with two other members (one a woman). Pecuniary jurisdiction up to ₹ 20 lakh (after the 2002 amendment).

  • State Commission: President is or has been a High Court Judge. Original jurisdiction above ₹ 20 lakh and up to ₹ 1 crore; appeals from District Forums.

  • National Commission: President is or has been a Supreme Court Judge. Original jurisdiction above ₹ 1 crore; appellate and revisional jurisdiction over State Commissions.

  • Appeal to the Supreme Court from the National Commission (Section 27A) within 30 days.

  • Limitation: 2 years from the cause of action (Section 24A).

  • Consumer Protection Councils (Central, State, District): advisory bodies promoting consumer rights.

  • Reliefs (Section 14): removal of defects, replacement, refund of price, compensation for loss or injury, removal of deficiency, discontinuance of unfair trade practice, withdrawal of hazardous goods, punitive damages, costs.

  • Procedure: summary; not bound by CPC technicalities, but natural justice applies.

8.6Consumer Protection Act 2019: key changes (highest-yield part)#

In brief

Covers Wider "consumer" (Section 2(7)), consumer rights as a class, product manufacturer, Unfair contract (Section 2(46)), E-commerce and direct selling and more.

  • any mode
  • resale or commercial purpose
  • self-employment
  • consumer rights as a class
  • recall of goods
  • refunds
  • class action
  • ₹ 10 lakh

  1. Wider "consumer" (Section 2(7)): covers buyers of goods or services through any mode, offline or online, including electronic means, teleshopping, direct selling and multi-level marketing. Free goods/services and purchases for resale or commercial purpose are excluded; "commercial purpose" does not include goods bought and used exclusively for earning a livelihood by self-employment.

  2. Central Consumer Protection Authority (CCPA) (Sections 10–22): a regulator to promote, protect and enforce consumer rights as a class. Powers: inquiries and investigations (through an Investigation Wing headed by a Director General), search and seizure, recall of goods, refunds, discontinuance of unfair practices, safety notices, class action, and penalties for false or misleading advertisements (up to ₹ 10 lakh on a manufacturer or endorser, with a ban on the endorser of up to one year; for subsequent contraventions up to ₹ 50 lakh and a ban of up to three years). Endorsers have a due diligence defence (Section 21). The CCPA does not adjudicate individual disputes.

  3. Product liability (Chapter VI, Sections 82–87): the product manufacturer, product service provider and product seller are liable for harm from defective products or deficient services. A manufacturer is liable for a manufacturing defect, design defect, deviation from manufacturing specifications, non-conformity with an express warranty, or failure to give adequate instructions or warnings. The seller's liability is in Section 86; Section 87 lists exceptions (e.g., misuse, alteration, claimant's knowledge of danger).

  4. Unfair contract (Section 2(46)): terms causing a significant change in the consumer's rights (excessive security deposit, disproportionate penalty, unilateral termination, etc.). The State and National Commissions can declare such terms null and void (Sections 49(2) and 59(2)).

  5. E-commerce and direct selling: e-commerce is defined (Section 2(16)); the Consumer Protection (E-Commerce) Rules 2020 and Direct Selling Rules 2021 impose disclosure and grievance-redressal duties.

  6. Mediation (Chapter V, Sections 74–81): consumer mediation cells attached to the Commissions; a mediated settlement is final and no appeal lies (Section 80).

  7. Easier filing: complaints can be filed electronically (e-Daakhil), hearings by video-conferencing; the complaint may be filed where the complainant resides or personally works for gain (Section 34(2)(d)).

  8. Pecuniary jurisdiction is based on the value of the consideration paid (not the value of goods/services plus compensation claimed). The original 2019 Act provided District up to ₹ 1 crore and State up to ₹ 10 crore. Under the 2021 Jurisdiction Rules (from 30 December 2021): District Commission up to ₹ 50 lakh; State Commission above ₹ 50 lakh up to ₹ 2 crore; National Commission above ₹ 2 crore (verify current limits).

  9. Admissibility: the District Commission must decide admissibility within 21 days; otherwise the complaint is deemed admitted.

  10. Appeals: District → State within 45 days (Section 41); State → National within 30 days (Section 51); National → Supreme Court within 30 days (Section 67). An appellant against a money award must deposit 50 percent of the amount.

  11. Limitation (Section 69): two years, with power to condone delay for sufficient cause.

  12. Composition: District Commission: President and at least two members; State Commission: President and at least four members; National Commission: President and at least four members.

  13. Penalties (Chapter VII): non-compliance with Commission orders (Section 72: imprisonment of one month up to three years, or fine of ₹ 25,000 to ₹ 1 lakh, or both); misleading advertisements (imprisonment up to 2 years and fine up to ₹ 10 lakh; repeat offence up to 5 years and ₹ 50 lakh); adulterated goods (Section 90: up to 1 year and ₹ 1 lakh if no injury; up to 7 years and ₹ 3 lakh for grievous hurt; for death, not less than 7 years up to life and fine not less than ₹ 10 lakh); spurious goods (Section 91: similar graded punishments).

8.7Recent regulatory developments (trend)#

In brief

Covers greenwashing, National Consumer Helpline.

  • greenwashing
  • National Consumer Helpline

  • Guidelines for Prevention and Regulation of Misleading Advertisements, 2022 (CCPA).

  • Guidelines for Prevention and Regulation of Dark Patterns, 2023 (30 November 2023): 13 specified dark patterns such as false urgency, basket sneaking, confirm shaming, forced action, subscription trap, interface interference, bait and switch, drip pricing, disguised advertisement, nagging, trick question, SaaS billing and rogue malware.

  • CCPA guidelines on misleading advertisements in the coaching sector and on greenwashing (2024) (verify exact titles and dates).

  • National Consumer Helpline and the e-Daakhil portal.

8.8Leading cases#

In brief

Covers commercial purpose.

  • commercial purpose
  • self-employment

  • Lucknow Development Authority v M.K. Gupta (1994): statutory development authorities are covered; housing construction is "service"; compensation can be awarded for harassment.

  • Indian Medical Association v V.P. Shantha (1995): doctors and hospitals charging fees are covered; free service to all patients in government hospitals and contracts of personal service are excluded; where some patients pay and others do not, all are covered.

  • Laxmi Engineering Works v P.S.G. Industrial Institute (1995): meaning of commercial purpose; dominant-intention test; machinery bought for self-employment is not commercial.

  • Spring Meadows Hospital v Harjol Ahluwalia (1998): parents can be consumers for a child's treatment.

  • Consumer Education and Research Centre v Union of India (1995): right to health of workers is part of Article 21.

  • State of Karnataka v Vishwabharathi House Building Co-op Society (2003): constitutional validity of the Act upheld.

  • Bangalore Development Authority v Syndicate Bank (2007): the Act and allotment of sites.

  • Ireo Grace Realtech v Abhishek Khanna (2021) and Pioneer Urban Land v Govindan Raghavan (2019): one-sided builder–buyer clauses are unfair; buyers may claim refund with interest for inordinate delay.

  • Bar of Indian Lawyers v D.K. Gandhi PS National Institute of Communicable Diseases (2024): the Supreme Court held that advocates' services are not "service" under the CPA; the profession is sui generis. The decision was later subject to further consideration, so verify the present status before the exam.
