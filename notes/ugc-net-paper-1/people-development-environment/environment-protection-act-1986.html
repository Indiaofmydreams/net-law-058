7.1Concept: why India needed an umbrella law#

In brief

Before 1986 India had separate laws on water (1974), air (1981) and forests (1980), but no single law that could deal with all environmental problems, fill gaps between…

Before 1986 India had separate laws on water (1974), air (1981) and forests (1980), but no single law that could deal with all environmental problems, fill gaps between those laws, and give the Central Government wide powers to act quickly. The Bhopal gas tragedy of December 1984, in which methyl isocyanate leaked from a pesticide plant, exposed these gaps. The Environment (Protection) Act (EPA) was enacted in 1986 to implement the decisions taken at the United Nations Conference on the Human Environment (Stockholm, 1972), under Article 253 of the Constitution, which lets Parliament legislate to implement international decisions. It extends to the whole of India and is often described as an umbrella or enabling legislation, because it authorizes the Central Government to make rules, issue directions and set standards on almost any aspect of environmental protection, rather than listing detailed technical limits itself. The Ministry of Environment, Forest and Climate Change is the administering ministry.

The Act is short (26 sections) and has four chapters: Preliminary (sections 1 and 2), General Powers of the Central Government (sections 3 to 6), Prevention, Control and Abatement of Environmental Pollution (sections 7 to 17), and Miscellaneous (sections 18 to 26). Its central idea is that the Central Government may take all measures it considers necessary or expedient to protect and improve the quality of the environment and to prevent, control and abate environmental pollution.

7.2Key definitions (Section 2)#

In brief

Environment includes water, air and land and the inter-relationship which exists among and between water, air and land, and human beings, other living creatures, plants…

  • Environment includes water, air and land and the inter-relationship which exists among and between water, air and land, and human beings, other living creatures, plants, micro-organisms and property

  • Environmental pollutant is any solid, liquid or gaseous substance present in such concentration as may be, or tend to be, injurious to the environment

  • Environmental pollution is the presence in the environment of any environmental pollutant

  • Hazardous substance is any substance or preparation which, by reason of its chemical or physico-chemical properties or handling, is liable to cause harm to human beings, other living creatures, plants, micro-organisms, property or the environment

  • Handling, in relation to any substance, includes manufacture, processing, treatment, package, storage, transportation, use, collection, destruction, conversion, offering for sale, transfer or the like

In brief

Section Subject
3 Powers of the Central Government to take measures to protect and improve the environment; includes setting standards, restricting areas, and constituting authorities under 3(3)
4 Appointment of officers and their powers
5 Power to give directions, including closure, prohibition or regulation of any industry, operation or process, and stoppage of electricity, water or other services
6 Rules to regulate environmental pollution

8 Persons handling hazardous substances must follow prescribed safeguards and procedures
9 Furnishing of information on accidental or apprehended discharge of pollutants
10 Powers of entry and inspection
11 Power to take samples and procedure for analysis
15 Penalty for contravention of the Act, rules, orders or directions
16 Offences by companies
17 Offences by Government departments
19 Cognizance of offences
22 Bar of jurisdiction of civil courts
24 Effect of other laws
25 Power to make rules

On penalties, the original Section 15 prescribed imprisonment up to five years or fine up to one lakh rupees or both, an additional fine up to five thousand rupees per day for continuing contravention, and imprisonment up to seven years if contravention continued beyond one year. The Jan Vishwas (Amendment of Provisions) Act, 2023 later replaced criminal penalties with monetary penalties imposed through an adjudicating officer; exam questions generally follow the original structure, but check the latest bare act if a question refers to current penalties. Under the original text of Section 19, a court takes cognizance of an offence on a complaint by the Central Government or an authorized authority or officer, or by any person who has given not less than sixty days' notice of the alleged offence, which is how citizens can approach the courts.

7.4Rules and notifications made under the Act#

In brief

Environment (Protection) Rules, 1986: standards for emission and discharge of pollutants and procedures; Environmental Statement (Form V) to be filed by industries by 30…

  • Environment (Protection) Rules, 1986: standards for emission and discharge of pollutants and procedures; Environmental Statement (Form V) to be filed by industries by 30 September each year

  • Environmental Impact Assessment notification: 1994, replaced by the EIA Notification, 2006 (projects in Category A and B, screening, scoping, public hearing, appraisal)

  • Coastal Regulation Zone notification: 1991, revised in 2011 and 2019

  • Eco-sensitive zones around protected areas are notified under the Act; Environment (Siting for Industrial Projects) Rules, 1999; Wetlands Rules, 2010 (revised 2017)

  • Hazardous waste: Hazardous Wastes (Management and Handling) Rules, 1989, now the Hazardous and Other Wastes (Management and Transboundary Movement) Rules, 2016; Manufacture, Storage and Import of Hazardous Chemical Rules, 1989; Chemical Accidents (Emergency Planning, Preparedness and Response) Rules, 1996

  • Noise Pollution (Regulation and Control) Rules, 2000; Ozone Depleting Substances (Regulation and Control) Rules, 2000

  • Waste rules (all 2016 versions in force, with later amendments): Solid Waste Management, Plastic Waste Management, E-Waste (Management), Bio-medical Waste Management, Construction and Demolition Waste; Battery Waste Management Rules, 2022

  • Authorities under Section 3(3): the Environment Pollution (Prevention and Control) Authority for the NCR (EPCA, 1998), and later the statutory Commission for Air Quality Management in NCR and Adjoining Areas (Act of 2021)

7.5Constitutional provisions and allied laws#

In brief

Article 48A (Directive Principle): the State shall endeavour to protect and improve the environment and safeguard forests and wildlife; Article 51A(g) (Fundamental Duty)…

  • Article 48A (Directive Principle): the State shall endeavour to protect and improve the environment and safeguard forests and wildlife; Article 51A(g) (Fundamental Duty): citizens must protect and improve the natural environment; both were inserted by the 42nd Amendment, 1976, which also moved forests and wild animals and birds to the Concurrent List

  • Article 21: the Supreme Court has held that the right to a wholesome environment is part of the right to life (Subhash Kumar v. State of Bihar, 1991)

  • 73rd and 74th Amendments: Eleventh and Twelfth Schedules give panchayats and municipalities roles in environment-related matters

The National Green Tribunal has its principal bench in New Delhi, deals with substantial questions relating to the environment, and endeavours to dispose of cases within six months. Schedule I of the NGT Act covers the Water Act, Water Cess Act, Forest (Conservation) Act, Air Act, EP Act, Public Liability Insurance Act and Biological Diversity Act, but not the Wildlife (Protection) Act, 1972 or the Indian Forest Act, 1927.

★Analysis Angle: The Environment (Protection) Act, 1986

Analyse / traps

  • The EP Act is a framework law. Section 3 gives general powers, Section 5 gives direction-making power, and Section 6 allows rule-making, so most of the actual standards and procedures are in rules and notifications, not in the Act. Questions that ask where emission standards or EIA procedure come from therefore point to the rules and notifications made under the Act.

  • The Act is Central-government centred, whereas the Water and Air Acts work through pollution control boards. This is why the EP Act is said to fill gaps and give a single point of coordination.

  • Absolute liability and the polluter pays principle make the polluter responsible for compensation and restoration; the precautionary principle shifts the burden of proof to the developer when serious environmental harm is possible.

  • Common weakness noted in analysis: enforcement depends heavily on executive action and on limited inspection capacity, and criticism of weaker penalties is a continuing theme in debates on the Act.

  • Trap to avoid: The Water Act 1974 created the pollution control boards and the Air Act 1981 extended their powers to air; the EP Act 1986 came after Bhopal and implements the Stockholm decisions.
