4.1Why Learner Characteristics Matter#

In brief

Teaching is only effective when it is matched to who is learning.

  • individual differences exist

Teaching is only effective when it is matched to who is learning. Learner characteristics are the attributes that learners bring into a classroom: age and developmental stage, prior knowledge, intelligence and aptitude, motivation, interests, learning style, self-concept, emotional state, social and cultural background, language and special needs. Two principles run through the whole chapter. First, individual differences exist in every class, so one method for all will fail some learners (this is why differentiated instruction is recommended). Second, what the learner already knows is the single strongest factor in what he or she will learn next (Ausubel: “the most important single factor influencing learning is what the learner already knows; ascertain this and teach accordingly”).

4.2Piaget’s Theory of Cognitive Development#

In brief

Jean Piaget saw the child as an active little scientist who builds knowledge by interacting with the world.

  • schema
  • assimilation
  • accommodation
  • adaptation
  • equilibration
  • Sensorimotor (0 to 2 years)
  • object permanence
  • Preoperational (2 to 7 years)

Jean Piaget saw the child as an active little scientist who builds knowledge by interacting with the world. His main terms are schema (a mental framework or structure for organising knowledge), assimilation (fitting new information into an existing schema), accommodation (changing the schema to fit new information), adaptation (the combined process of assimilation and accommodation) and equilibration (the balancing force that drives development when there is a conflict between what is known and what is experienced). Development proceeds through four invariant stages, and each stage is qualitatively different:

  • Sensorimotor (0 to 2 years): knowledge comes through senses and actions. Key achievement: object permanence (understanding that objects continue to exist when out of sight), at about 8 to 12 months. Early reflexes give way to goal-directed action.

  • Preoperational (2 to 7 years): use of symbols, language and pretend play grows, but thinking is intuitive and illogical. Characteristics: egocentrism (cannot see from another person’s viewpoint), animism (believing objects are alive), centration (focusing on one feature), irreversibility, and failure of conservation. The classic experiment is three mountains for egocentrism.

  • Concrete operational (7 to 11 years): logical thinking about concrete, real objects. The child masters conservation (quantity stays the same despite change in shape), reversibility, classification (grouping by categories) and seriation (arranging in an ordered series, such as by size). Abstract and hypothetical thinking is still difficult.

  • Formal operational (about 12 years and above, some books say 11+): abstract, hypothetical and hypothetico-deductive reasoning; systematic thinking about possibilities, ideals and “what if” problems, and use of scientific logic.

Teaching implications: give concrete materials and hands-on activities in the primary years; arrange tasks that cause cognitive conflict so that learning pushes development; do not teach abstract ideas before the child is ready (readiness); respect that children learn in different time frames. Critics note that Piaget underestimated children’s abilities and understated the role of social and cultural factors, which is Vygotsky’s point.

4.3Vygotsky’s Sociocultural Theory#

In brief

Lev Vygotsky argued that cognitive development is a social process: higher mental functions begin between people (interpsychological) and are then internalised…

  • More Knowledgeable Other (MKO)
  • Scaffolding
  • gradually withdrawn
  • Private speech
  • Piaget vs Vygotsky

Lev Vygotsky argued that cognitive development is a social process: higher mental functions begin between people (interpsychological) and are then internalised (intrapsychological). Language is the main cultural tool for thought. The key concepts are:

  • Zone of Proximal Development (ZPD): the gap between what a learner can do independently and what he or she can do with the guidance of a more capable person. Good teaching targets the ZPD, so tasks should be slightly beyond current ability, neither too easy nor impossibly hard.

  • More Knowledgeable Other (MKO): anyone with a better understanding or higher ability in a task: a teacher, peer, parent, coach or even software.

  • Scaffolding: temporary support (hints, modelling, questioning, breaking a task into steps) that is gradually withdrawn as the learner grows competent. The term was introduced by Wood, Bruner and Ross (1976), not by Vygotsky himself, though it fits his theory.

  • Private speech: children talking to themselves while working is not a mental disorder; it is a cognitive self-regulation tool that gradually becomes silent inner speech.

Piaget vs Vygotsky: Piaget emphasised individual discovery and the idea that development precedes learning; Vygotsky emphasised social interaction and the idea that learning leads development. Piaget focused on stages; Vygotsky did not propose fixed stages.

4.4Other Theorists Useful for Learner Characteristics#

In brief

Jerome Bruner described three modes of representation: enactive (action), iconic (images) and symbolic (language), and proposed the spiral curriculum (revisiting ideas at…

  • Jerome Bruner
  • enactive
  • iconic
  • symbolic
  • spiral curriculum
  • discovery learning
  • Erik Erikson
  • industry vs inferiority

Jerome Bruner described three modes of representation: enactive (action), iconic (images) and symbolic (language), and proposed the spiral curriculum (revisiting ideas at increasing depth) and discovery learning. Erik Erikson gave eight psychosocial stages, each with a crisis: trust vs mistrust, autonomy vs shame and doubt, initiative vs guilt, industry vs inferiority (6 to 12 years, the school years), identity vs role confusion (adolescence), intimacy vs isolation, generativity vs stagnation and integrity vs despair. Lawrence Kohlberg described moral development at three levels: preconventional (obedience and self-interest), conventional (approval of others and law-and-order) and postconventional (social contract and universal ethical principles).

Abraham Maslow’s hierarchy of needs is a pyramid in which lower needs must be reasonably met before higher ones can motivate: physiological, safety, love and belonging, esteem, self-actualisation. A hungry, frightened or neglected student cannot concentrate on learning; this is the main teaching implication. Albert Bandura proposed observational (social) learning (Bobo doll experiment) and self-efficacy, which is a person’s belief in his or her own capacity to carry out a task, not the actual ability and not an intelligence score. High self-efficacy predicts effort and persistence. Julian Rotter’s locus of control distinguishes learners who believe outcomes depend on their own effort (internal) from those who attribute outcomes to luck or others (external). Metacognition (John Flavell) is “thinking about one’s own thinking”: planning, monitoring and evaluating one’s own learning.

4.5Intelligence and Learning Styles#

In brief

Howard Gardner’s Multiple Intelligences include linguistic, logical-mathematical, spatial, bodily-kinaesthetic, musical, interpersonal, intrapersonal and naturalistic (added…

  • VARK

Howard Gardner’s Multiple Intelligences include linguistic, logical-mathematical, spatial, bodily-kinaesthetic, musical, interpersonal, intrapersonal and naturalistic (added later). The teaching implication is to present content in several ways. David Kolb’s experiential learning cycle has four stages: concrete experience, reflective observation, abstract conceptualisation and active experimentation, and four learning styles: diverger, assimilator, converger and accommodator. The VARK model (Visual, Aural, Read/write, Kinaesthetic) is a popular classification. Evidence for matching teaching strictly to a “style” is weak, so the wise teacher uses varied methods rather than labelling learners.

4.6Adult Learners (Andragogy)#

In brief

Malcolm Knowles contrasted pedagogy (teaching children) with andragogy (teaching adults).

  • internal motivation

Malcolm Knowles contrasted pedagogy (teaching children) with andragogy (teaching adults). Adult learners (a) have a self-concept of being independent and self-directed, (b) bring a rich reservoir of prior experience that is a resource, (c) have readiness to learn linked to life roles, (d) want problem-centred, immediately applicable learning, (e) are driven mainly by internal motivation and (f) need to know why they are learning something. Therefore adult education prefers discussion, case studies, projects and self-directed learning, and teachers act as facilitators. The common error is to think that adults rely only on concrete operations or have no capacity for abstract thinking, which is false.

4.7Special Groups: Gifted, Slow, Differently Abled; Inclusive Education#

In brief

Gifted learners (IQ commonly taken as above 130, or exceptional talent) need enrichment, acceleration and challenge .

  • Gifted learners
  • Slow learners
  • Differently abled learners
  • Inclusive education

Gifted learners (IQ commonly taken as above 130, or exceptional talent) need enrichment, acceleration and challenge. Slow learners (IQ roughly 70 to 90) need more repetition, simpler steps, concrete material and patience, but they are not mentally disabled and are not necessarily less motivated. Differently abled learners require accessible materials and assistive technology. Inclusive education means the education system includes all learners in the common classroom, regardless of disability, gender, caste or economic background; it is backed by the Salamanca Statement (1994), the Right of Children to Free and Compulsory Education Act (2009), the RPwD Act (2016) and NEP 2020. It is not education only for gifted learners, not education only for differently abled learners, and not education for economically weaker sections alone.

★Memory Capsule: Learner Characteristics

Memorise

  • Piaget: Sensorimotor (0 to 2, object permanence), Preoperational (2 to 7, egocentrism, animism), Concrete operational (7 to 11, conservation, seriation), Formal operational (12+, abstract, hypothetico-deductive).
  • Vygotsky: ZPD, MKO, scaffolding (Wood, Bruner and Ross), private speech, social constructivism.
  • Bruner: enactive, iconic, symbolic; spiral curriculum.
  • Erikson: school age = industry vs inferiority; adolescence = identity vs role confusion.
  • Maslow: physiological, safety, love and belonging, esteem, self-actualisation.
  • Bandura: observational learning, self-efficacy. Rotter: locus of control. Flavell: metacognition.
  • Kolb: concrete experience, reflective observation, abstract conceptualisation, active experimentation. Gardner: multiple intelligences.
  • Knowles: andragogy (self-directed, experience, internal motivation, problem-centred).

★Analysis Angle: Learner Characteristics

Analyse / traps

Most application questions here give a learner behaviour and ask for the concept. A child who says that a taller glass holds more water: preoperational, no conservation. A student working a maths problem aloud to himself: private speech as self-regulation. A teacher who gives a hint and then withdraws it as the student improves: scaffolding within the ZPD. A student who believes “I can do this if I work at it”: high self-efficacy (not locus of control, which is about causes of outcomes). A learner who cannot focus because of hunger: Maslow, a lower need unmet. When two thinkers are compared, anchor on the central contrast: individual vs social; stages vs no stages; development leads learning vs learning leads development. For adult learners, the correct option is nearly always the one that stresses prior experience and self-direction. For special groups, reject options that treat slow learners as unmotivated or inclusive education as limited to one group.
