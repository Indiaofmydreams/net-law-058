10.1How the Whole Subject Connects#

In brief

Teaching aptitude is not ten separate chapters but one connected story.

  • Objectives
  • levels of teaching
  • learner’s characteristics
  • factors
  • methods
  • Support systems
  • Institutional frameworks
  • Evaluation

Teaching aptitude is not ten separate chapters but one connected story. Objectives (Topic 2, Bloom) decide what to teach and at which cognitive level. The levels of teaching (Topic 3) tell you how deeply to teach it: memory for facts, understanding for principles, reflection for problem-solving. The learner’s characteristics (Topic 4) decide how much a learner can handle (Piaget’s stage, Vygotsky’s ZPD, prior knowledge, motivation). The factors (Topic 5) remind you that teacher, learner, content and environment interact. The methods (Topic 6) are the vehicles, each suited to one level and one objective. Support systems (Topic 7) enrich delivery from Dale’s concrete experiences to SWAYAM courses. Institutional frameworks (Topic 8) like CBCS and NEP shape the programme. Evaluation (Topic 9) closes the loop by checking whether the objectives were met and feeding back into planning, which is exactly Jackson’s pre-active, interactive and post-active cycle.

The classic integrative question asks which statement best integrates Piaget, Vygotsky, Dewey, Bloom and Dale. The right answer reads like this: match content to the learner’s developmental stage (Piaget), support learners within the ZPD with scaffolding (Vygotsky), move from concrete to abstract experiences (Dale), engage learners in reflective inquiry around real problems (Dewey) and set objectives and tests at appropriate levels of thinking (Bloom). Wrong options always take one thinker and make it absolute: “focus only on examination preparation” or “standardise all methods regardless of learner characteristics.”

10.3Commonly Confused Pairs#

In brief

Covers Teaching vs instruction, Aim vs objective, Assimilation vs accommodation, Piaget vs Vygotsky, Self-efficacy vs locus of control and more.

  • Teaching vs instruction
  • Teaching vs indoctrination
  • Aim vs objective
  • Assimilation vs accommodation
  • Piaget vs Vygotsky
  • Seminar vs symposium vs panel
  • Linear vs branching programme
  • Validity vs reliability

  • Teaching vs instruction: teaching is broader (includes attitudes and values); instruction focuses on knowledge and skills. Teaching vs indoctrination: teaching respects reason; indoctrination bypasses it.

  • Aim vs objective: aim is general and long-term; objective is specific and observable.

  • Assimilation vs accommodation: fitting new information into an existing schema vs changing the schema.

  • Piaget vs Vygotsky: individual vs social; development precedes learning vs learning leads development.

  • Self-efficacy vs locus of control: belief in one’s ability vs belief about what causes outcomes.

  • Seminar vs symposium vs panel: paper-and-discussion; several experts on aspects of one theme; experts discussing in front of an audience.

  • Linear vs branching programme: one fixed path with small steps (Skinner) vs response-dependent path (Crowder).

  • Formative vs summative; diagnostic vs formative: during vs end; diagnostic digs into causes.

  • Norm-referenced vs criterion-referenced: compared with peers vs compared with a standard.

  • Validity vs reliability: accuracy of what is measured vs consistency of scores.

  • CBT vs CAT: test delivered by computer vs test whose difficulty adapts to the candidate.

  • SWAYAM vs SWAYAMPRABHA: internet-based MOOC platform with certification vs DTH television channels without certification.

  • cMOOC vs xMOOC: connectivist, network-based vs institution-led, content-based.

  • Dale’s cone vs retention pyramid: concreteness-abstraction spectrum vs a myth about retention percentages.

10.4Reasoning Strategies for the Three Question Types#

In brief

Memory questions: convert each list into a mnemonic or a story and revise it within 24 hours, then again after a week.

  • Memory questions
  • Concept questions
  • Analysis questions

Memory questions: convert each list into a mnemonic or a story and revise it within 24 hours, then again after a week. Practise recall by writing, not re-reading: close the notes and list Bloom’s six levels, Piaget’s four stages, Dale’s eleven rungs and Flanders’ ten categories. Learn each name with its one distinctive idea (for example, “scaffolding term: Wood, Bruner and Ross”). Where two dates are possible, remember that SWAYAM and SWAYAMPRABHA are 2017, the CBCS guidelines 2015, NEP 2020, RTE 2009, microteaching 1963 and the MOOC term 2008.

Concept questions: state the idea in your own sentence before looking at the options. If the definition has a key word (intended, interpersonal, intimate, interactive), find the option that carries it. Eliminate options with absolute words such as only, always, never, entirely and none unless the statement is a known fact. Be wary of options that contradict a core principle: that teaching is evidenced by learning, that learners are active, that evaluation guides teaching, that technology supplements and does not replace teachers.

Analysis questions: read the scenario and identify the real variable being tested: the cognitive demand of a task (Bloom), the stage of the child (Piaget), the support provided (scaffolding), who is talking (Flanders), what happens after the test (formative or summative), or which learner is being served (SWAYAMPRABHA for the unconnected). For assertion-reason items, first test whether the assertion is true on its own, then whether the reason is true, then whether the reason explains the assertion. For matching items, solve the pairs you are sure of first and use elimination for the rest. For “EXCEPT” questions, mark each option as true or false and pick the false one. For “best integrates” or “most appropriate” questions, choose the option that is balanced and combines several principles rather than the one that overstates a single idea.

10.5One-Page Rapid Revision Sheet#

In brief

Covers Teaching, Bloom 2001, Piaget, Methods, Flanders and more.

  • Teaching
  • Bloom 2001
  • Affective
  • Piaget
  • Vygotsky
  • Maslow
  • Methods
  • Flanders

  • Teaching: system of actions to induce learning (Smith). Three phases (Jackson). Levels: memory (Herbart) \< understanding (Morrison) \< reflective (Hunt).

  • Bloom 2001: Remember, Understand, Apply, Analyse, Evaluate, Create. Affective: Receiving, Responding, Valuing, Organisation, Characterisation.

  • Piaget: Sensorimotor, Preoperational, Concrete operational, Formal operational. Vygotsky: ZPD, MKO, scaffolding. Maslow: physiological to self-actualisation.

  • Methods: lecture (large group, memory), demonstration (skills), discussion and seminar (thinking), project (Kilpatrick), heuristic (Armstrong), programmed (Skinner, Crowder), microteaching (Allen, 1963), cooperative (Jigsaw).

  • Flanders: 1 to 4 indirect, 5 to 7 direct, 8 to 9 student talk, 10 silence.

  • Dale: concrete (bottom) to abstract (top); verbal symbols at the apex.

  • SWAYAM: 2017; four quadrants; free; proctored exam; credit transfer up to 40 percent. SWAYAMPRABHA: 32 DTH channels, 24x7. MOOC: 2008; cMOOC (Siemens and Downes) vs xMOOC.

  • CBCS: core, DSE, GE, AECC, SEC; 1 credit = 1 hour lecture or 2 hours practical; O = 10 to P = 4; SGPA weighted by credits. NEP 2020: 5+3+3+4; GER 50 percent by 2035; multiple entry and exit 1-2-3-4; ABC.

  • Evaluation: formative (Scriven) vs summative; norm vs criterion (Glaser); validity first, then reliability; CAT adapts to the candidate; percentile = share of candidates scored below.

10.6Closing Advice#

In brief

Teaching Aptitude rewards two habits: precise recall of a limited set of names, dates and sequences and calm reasoning about what a scenario is really testing .

Teaching Aptitude rewards two habits: precise recall of a limited set of names, dates and sequences and calm reasoning about what a scenario is really testing. Revise the memory capsules daily in the last two weeks, attempt your 550 questions topic by topic, and for every wrong answer write one sentence that begins “I should have noticed that…”. The patterns you note (absolute words, mixed-up thinkers, level of cognition misjudged) will almost always repeat on exam day.
