4.1Oral Communication#

In brief

Covers Advantages, Limitations.

  • Advantages
  • Limitations

  • Uses spoken words: conversation, telephone, interviews, meetings, lectures, presentations.

  • Advantages: immediate feedback, personal touch, flexible, faster, can use tone and expression, builds rapport.

  • Limitations: no permanent record, chances of distortion, speaker may be impulsive, not suited to complex data, depends on memory.

4.2Written Communication#

In brief

Covers Advantages, Limitations.

  • Advantages
  • Limitations

  • Letters, emails, reports, memos, notices, manuals, circulars.

  • Advantages: permanent record and legal evidence, careful drafting, wide reach, reference value, suitable for complex details.

  • Limitations: delayed feedback, costly and time-consuming, needs literacy, lacks personal touch.

4.3Listening (Critical for Effectiveness)#

In brief

Covers Hearing, Types of listening, Active listening.

  • Hearing
  • listening
  • Types of listening
  • Active listening

  • Hearing is a physiological act; listening is a psychological, active process of attending, interpreting and responding.

  • Types of listening: appreciative (for enjoyment), empathic (for emotional support), comprehensive/informational (to learn), critical/evaluative (to judge arguments), discriminative (distinguishing sounds and cues).

  • Active listening involves attention, eye contact, paraphrasing, clarifying, withholding judgment and giving feedback.

  • Barriers to listening: distractions, prejudgment, daydreaming, emotional bias, speaker-listener speed gap.

4.5In simple words: Choosing oral vs written#

In brief

Covers Use oral, Use written.

  • Use oral
  • Use written
  • Why written feels "permanent"

  • Use oral when it is urgent, emotional, needs immediate clarification, or involves a small group (e.g., resolving a student's misunderstanding).

  • Use written when you need a record, legal proof, detailed data, or a message that must reach many people (e.g., an exam notification).

Why written feels "permanent": an email can be saved and shown later as proof. That is why contracts, notices and reports are in writing.

4.6In simple words: Listening: why it is hard#

In brief

People can think much faster than others can speak, so the mind gets spare time and wanders .

  • speed gap
  • Listening types with examples
  • Appreciative
  • Empathic
  • Comprehensive / informational
  • Critical / evaluative
  • Discriminative
  • Active listening steps

People can think much faster than others can speak, so the mind gets spare time and wanders. This is the speed gap, and it is a major cause of poor listening. Good listeners use that spare time to summarise what they hear and predict the next point.

Listening types with examples:

  • Appreciative: enjoying music or a poem.

  • Empathic: a friend is upset and you listen to understand their feelings, without rushing to give advice.

  • Comprehensive / informational: listening to a lecture to learn and remember facts.

  • Critical / evaluative: listening to a political speech and judging whether the claims are logical.

  • Discriminative: noticing a change in someone's tone or telling similar sounds apart (the base of other types).

Active listening steps: pay full attention, keep eye contact, do not interrupt, nod and use small encouragers, paraphrase ("So you mean..."), ask clarifying questions, and respond with feedback.

4.7In simple words: Written communication basics#

In brief

Common forms are letters, emails, memos, notices, circulars, reports and minutes.

Common forms are letters, emails, memos, notices, circulars, reports and minutes. A good written message has a clear purpose, logical order, simple sentences, correct grammar and a polite close. In emails, use a meaningful subject line, a greeting, a short body and a sign-off; avoid ALL CAPS (which reads as shouting) and think before pressing "send" (communication is irreversible).
