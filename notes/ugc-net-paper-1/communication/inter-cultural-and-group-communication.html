6.1Inter-cultural communication: meaning#

In brief

Meaning: communication between people from different cultural backgrounds (language, religion, values, customs, communication styles).

  • Meaning
  • different cultural backgrounds

Meaning: communication between people from different cultural backgrounds (language, religion, values, customs, communication styles). It requires awareness of how culture shapes perception, meaning and behaviour.

6.2Key Concepts#

In brief

Covers High-context, Low-context, Geert Hofstede's cultural dimensions, Ethnocentrism, Stereotyping and prejudice and more.

  • High-context
  • Low-context
  • Ethnocentrism
  • Stereotyping and prejudice
  • Cultural relativism
  • Culture shock (Kalervo Oberg)

  • Edward T. Hall – High-context vs low-context cultures:

    • High-context (e.g., Japan, China, Arab countries; India has many high-context features): much of the meaning lies in the context, relationships and non-verbal cues; indirect, implicit communication.

    • Low-context (e.g., USA, Germany, Scandinavia): meaning is carried in explicit words; direct, precise, detail-oriented.

  • Geert Hofstede's cultural dimensions: power distance, individualism vs collectivism, masculinity vs femininity, uncertainty avoidance, long-term vs short-term orientation, and indulgence vs restraint. India is generally characterised by high power distance and collectivism.

  • Ethnocentrism: judging other cultures by the standards of one's own, assuming one's own culture is superior.

  • Stereotyping and prejudice: generalised, fixed beliefs or attitudes about a group.

  • Cultural relativism: understanding behaviour within its own cultural context.

  • Culture shock (Kalervo Oberg): stages commonly described as honeymoon, crisis/frustration, adjustment and acceptance/adaptation.

6.6Group Basics#

In brief

Covers Primary groups, Group norms, roles, cohesion and status, Benne and Sheats, Leadership styles (Kurt Lewin).

  • Primary groups
  • secondary groups
  • Benne and Sheats
  • Leadership styles (Kurt Lewin)

  • Primary groups (family, close friends; Charles Cooley) vs secondary groups (formal, goal-oriented, e.g., committees).

  • Formal vs informal groups; in-group vs out-group.

  • Group norms, roles, cohesion and status shape communication.

  • Tuckman's stages of group development (1965; adjourning added 1977): Forming, Storming, Norming, Performing, Adjourning.

  • Benne and Sheats classified member roles as task roles, group-building/maintenance roles, and individual (self-centred) roles.

  • Leadership styles (Kurt Lewin): autocratic, democratic, laissez-faire.

6.7Group Phenomena#

In brief

Covers Groupthink (Irving Janis, 1972), Social loafing (Ringelmann effect), Group polarization, Synergy.

  • Group polarization
  • Synergy

  • Groupthink (Irving Janis, 1972): the desire for harmony overrides critical thinking.

  • Social loafing (Ringelmann effect): individuals put in less effort in a group.

  • Group polarization: group discussion pushes opinions to extremes.

  • Synergy: the group's combined output exceeds the sum of individual outputs.

6.8Group Communication Formats (Often Asked in Matching)#

In brief

Covers Group discussion (GD), Panel discussion, Symposium, Seminar, Workshop and more.

  • Group discussion (GD)
  • Panel discussion
  • Symposium
  • Seminar
  • Workshop
  • Conference
  • Brainstorming (Alex Osborn)
  • no criticism

  • Group discussion (GD): free exchange among 8 to 12 participants on a topic, assessed on content, communication, leadership and teamwork.

  • Panel discussion: a small panel of experts discusses a topic before an audience, led by a moderator.

  • Symposium: several experts present formal papers or talks on different aspects of a topic, usually followed by audience questions.

  • Seminar: participants present and discuss papers on a theme, usually in an academic setting.

  • Workshop: hands-on, skill-oriented sessions.

  • Conference: a large formal gathering for presenting and exchanging research or views.

  • Brainstorming (Alex Osborn): rapid idea generation with no criticism in the idea-generation phase; quantity first.

  • Buzz groups (Phillips 66): small groups of about six discussing for about six minutes.

  • Debate: structured argument for and against a motion.

  • Round table, forum, colloquium: variants of open discussion.

6.10In simple words: High-context vs low-context cultures#

In brief

Covers High-context, Low-context.

  • High-context
  • Low-context
  • Exam cue

  • High-context: the meaning is "between the lines". People rely on relationships, tone, and unspoken understanding. A polite "that might be difficult" often means "no". Examples often given: Japan, China, many Arab and South Asian cultures.

  • Low-context: the meaning is in the words. "No" means no; contracts are detailed. Examples often given: USA, Germany, Scandinavia.

Exam cue: "indirect, relationship-based, implicit" = high-context; "direct, explicit, detail in words" = low-context.

6.11In simple words: Hofstede's dimensions in plain words#

In brief

Covers Power distance, Individualism vs collectivism, Masculinity vs femininity, Uncertainty avoidance, Long-term vs short-term orientation and more.

  • Power distance
  • Individualism vs collectivism
  • Masculinity vs femininity
  • Uncertainty avoidance
  • Indulgence vs restraint

  • Power distance: how comfortable a society is with unequal power. High power distance: students never question the teacher; titles are important. Low: first names, open challenge to bosses.

  • Individualism vs collectivism: "I" versus "we". Individualist cultures stress personal goals; collectivist cultures stress family and group loyalty.

  • Masculinity vs femininity: achievement, competition and success versus caring, quality of life and cooperation.

  • Uncertainty avoidance: how uncomfortable people feel with ambiguity. High: many rules and detailed plans. Low: flexible and comfortable with risk.

  • Long-term vs short-term orientation: future-focused thrift and perseverance versus respect for tradition and quick results.

  • Indulgence vs restraint: freedom to enjoy life versus strict social norms controlling desires.

6.12In simple words: Three terms often mixed up#

In brief

Covers Stereotype, Prejudice, Discrimination.

  • Stereotype
  • Prejudice
  • Discrimination
  • Ethnocentrism
  • Cultural relativism

  • Stereotype is a belief ("all X are lazy").

  • Prejudice is a feeling or attitude (disliking X without evidence).

  • Discrimination is an action (treating X unfairly).

Ethnocentrism: treating your own culture as the standard and judging others by it. Cultural relativism is its opposite: judging a behaviour in its own cultural context.

6.13In simple words: Culture shock in four stages (simple)#

In brief

Covers Honeymoon, Crisis / frustration, Adjustment, Acceptance / adaptation.

  • Honeymoon
  • Crisis / frustration
  • Adjustment
  • Acceptance / adaptation

  1. Honeymoon: everything is new and exciting.

  2. Crisis / frustration: differences become irritating and homesickness appears.

  3. Adjustment: you learn local ways and feel more comfortable.

  4. Acceptance / adaptation: you function comfortably in the new culture.

6.14In simple words: Group communication simplified#

In brief

Group vs crowd: a group has a shared goal, interaction and roles (a project team).

  • Group vs crowd
  • Forming
  • Storming
  • Norming
  • Performing
  • Adjourning
  • Group problems explained
  • Groupthink

Group vs crowd: a group has a shared goal, interaction and roles (a project team). A crowd is a physical gathering without structure (people at a bus stop).

Tuckman's stages as a project-team story:

  • Forming: members meet, are polite and unsure of roles.

  • Storming: disagreements and power struggles appear.

  • Norming: rules and roles are accepted; cooperation grows.

  • Performing: the team works smoothly and delivers results.

  • Adjourning: the project ends and the team disbands.

Group problems explained:

  • Groupthink: everyone agrees quickly to avoid conflict, even when the plan is bad (nobody dares say "this will fail").

  • Social loafing: in a group assignment, one member puts in less effort because they think others will cover.

  • Group polarization: after discussing among like-minded people, the group's view becomes more extreme than it was at the start.

  • Synergy: the group achieves more together than the sum of individual efforts.

Group formats: the distinguishing cue for each

  • Group discussion: free exchange among participants, no formal speeches; often used in selection.

  • Panel discussion: a few experts discuss in front of an audience with a moderator; informal conversation among panellists.

  • Symposium: each expert gives a prepared formal presentation on one aspect of a theme.

  • Seminar: participants present papers and discuss a theme, usually academic and led by a chairperson.

  • Workshop: hands-on practical skill building.

  • Conference: a large gathering with many sessions for research and policy exchange.

  • Brainstorming: generate many ideas without criticism first, evaluate later.

  • Debate: two sides argue for and against a motion.

Panel vs symposium (common confusion): panel = conversation between experts; symposium = a series of formal talks by experts.
