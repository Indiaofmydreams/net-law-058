§Overview#

A number series is a list of numbers that follows a hidden rule. You are asked to find the missing term, the wrong term, or the odd one out. Most questions use one of about a dozen patterns, so recognising the pattern quickly is the whole skill.

2.1Four-step method (use it every time)#

In brief

Covers Look at the growth, Write the differences, If the differences are not constant, If differences fail.

  • Look at the growth
  • Write the differences
  • If differences fail

  1. Look at the growth. If the numbers rise slowly, think addition or small differences. If they rise very fast, think multiplication, squares, cubes or powers.

  2. Write the differences between neighbouring terms. If the differences are constant, it is an arithmetic series.

  3. If the differences are not constant, write the differences of the differences, or check whether the differences are squares, primes, or double each time.

  4. If differences fail, test ratios, then squares and cubes (with a small plus or minus), then alternating or two-series patterns.

Item Formula
nth term of an AP (first term a, common difference d) a + (n - 1)d

nth term of a GP (common ratio r) a x r^(n-1)

Sum of cubes of first n natural numbers [n(n + 1)/2] squared
Sum of first n odd numbers n squared
Sum of first n even numbers n(n + 1)

2.4Shortcuts and tricks#

In brief

Covers Near a square or cube?, Fast growth test, Slow, irregular growth, Zig-zag numbers, Check the last digit and more.

  • Near a square or cube?
  • Fast growth test
  • Slow, irregular growth
  • Zig-zag numbers
  • Check the last digit
  • Decreasing series

  • Memorise squares up to 30 and cubes up to 15. Almost every "hidden" square or cube pattern is solved instantly when you recognise 121, 169, 196, 289, 343, 512, 729 and similar numbers.

  • Near a square or cube? If the terms sit just above or below perfect squares or cubes, test n squared +/- k and n cubed +/- k.

  • Fast growth test: if each term is roughly double or triple the previous one, test x2 +/- c or x3 +/- c before anything else.

  • Slow, irregular growth: write the differences; if differences themselves grow steadily, go to second-order differences.

  • Zig-zag numbers (up, down, up, down) usually mean two alternating operations or two interleaved series.

  • Check the last digit. If the last digits repeat in a cycle such as 2, 4, 8, 6, suspect powers or repeated doubling.

  • Test the rule on two terms before you trust it. A rule that fits only the first two terms is usually wrong.

  • Decreasing series: try reverse of the increasing patterns (minus, divide, descending squares or cubes).

Example 1. 4, 7, 12, 19, 28, ?

  • Differences: 3, 5, 7, 9. These rise by 2, so the next difference is 11.

  • Answer: 28 + 11 = 39. Check: the terms are n squared + 3 and 6 squared + 3 = 39.

Example 2. 6, 11, 21, 36, 56, ?

  • Differences: 5, 10, 15, 20, so the next is 25.

  • Answer: 56 + 25 = 81.

Example 3. 3, 7, 15, 31, 64, 127. Find the wrong term.

  • Rule from the first terms: x2 + 1 gives 7, 15, 31, 63, 127.

  • The term 64 breaks the rule. The correct term is 63, so the wrong term is 64.

Example 4. 3, 5, 7, 12, 13, 17, 19. Find the odd one out.

  • All terms except 12 are prime numbers. The odd one out is 12.

Example 5. 2, 5, 4, 8, 6, 11, 8, ?

  • Odd places: 2, 4, 6, 8 (adding 2). Even places: 5, 8, 11, so the next is 14.

  • Answer: 14.

2.6Traps#

In brief

Using only the first two terms to guess a rule, which often fits several different patterns. Forgetting that the series may be in descending order. Missing that a series is…

  • Using only the first two terms to guess a rule, which often fits several different patterns.

  • Forgetting that the series may be in descending order.

  • Missing that a series is two interleaved series because the numbers look random.

  • Reading "wrong number" questions as "missing number" questions. For a wrong-number question, find the rule from the early, reliable terms and locate where it breaks.
