1A.1Meaning, Definitions and Purpose of Research#

Concept

Research is a systematic, objective and organised search for new knowledge or for a better understanding of existing knowledge. The word comes from the old French recercher, “to search again”, which captures the idea that research does not end at one answer; it re-examines, verifies and extends. A study counts as research only when it begins with a clearly defined problem, uses a planned procedure, collects evidence that others can inspect, and draws conclusions that are supported by that evidence. Simply collecting or compiling information, writing a summary of what is already known, or giving a personal opinion is not research, because none of these involves a controlled search for something not yet established. The purposes of research are usually listed as exploration (finding out about a little-known area), description (portraying accurately), explanation (finding why), prediction (forecasting what will happen) and control (using knowledge to influence events).

Memorise

  • Research = systematic + objective + empirical + critical investigation; the goal is discovery, verification, relationship-finding, theory-building or problem-solving.
  • Redman and Mory: research is a systematised effort to gain new knowledge. Clifford Woody: research involves defining problems, formulating hypotheses, collecting and evaluating data, and testing conclusions. Kerlinger: systematic, controlled, empirical and critical investigation of hypothetical propositions about presumed relations among natural phenomena.
  • Kothari’s four objectives: familiarity with a phenomenon (exploratory), accurate portrayal (descriptive), frequency or association (diagnostic), and testing a causal hypothesis (hypothesis-testing).
  • Research methods are the techniques (sampling, interview, t-test). Research methodology is the logic and philosophy behind choosing and justifying those techniques.

Analyse / traps

Options that say research “proves” something are almost always wrong; research supports, confirms or rejects. A statement that equates research with mere data collection, or that says it needs no hypothesis in any form, is a trap. Remember that methodology is broader than methods: every question that asks “the study of methods and the reasoning behind them” points to methodology.

1A.2Characteristics of Research#

Concept

Good research is recognisable by a cluster of features that make it trustworthy. It is systematic because it follows ordered steps, logical because conclusions follow from evidence by valid reasoning, empirical because it rests on observation and data rather than speculation, and objective because the personal views of the researcher are kept out of the procedure as far as possible. It is replicable, meaning another researcher using the same method on similar subjects should get comparable results, and cumulative, since each study builds on earlier ones and adds to a body of knowledge. It is problem-oriented, controlled (variables are identified and extraneous influences are minimised), critical (assumptions are questioned) and ethical. Research also aims at generalisation, although action research and many qualitative studies deliberately give that up in return for depth and local relevance.

Memorise

  • Core features: systematic, logical, empirical, objective, replicable, verifiable, cumulative, precise, problem-focused, ethical.
  • Replicability and objectivity are what separate scientific research from opinion.
  • Research is cyclical: findings raise new questions, which start the next cycle.
  • Reliable tools and valid measures are part of the character of good research.

Analyse / traps

“Research is always completely value-free” is too strong; the realistic position is that objectivity is a goal, and qualitative research openly admits researcher subjectivity. Be careful with an option that says research always aims at universal laws; applied and action research aim at solving specific problems.

1A.3Scientific Method, Attitude and Reasoning#

Concept

The scientific method is the disciplined way of acquiring knowledge: observe a phenomenon, define a problem, form a hypothesis, test it by collecting evidence under controlled conditions, analyse the results, and then accept, modify or reject the hypothesis, which may feed into a theory. Behind the method lies a scientific attitude, which means curiosity, open-mindedness, healthy scepticism, honesty in reporting, objectivity, and readiness to revise beliefs when evidence demands it. Reasoning takes two classical forms. Deductive reasoning goes from general premises to a particular conclusion (all humans are mortal; Socrates is human; therefore Socrates is mortal), and the conclusion is certain if the premises are true. Inductive reasoning goes from particular observations to a general statement, and the conclusion is only probable. Modern research usually combines both in the hypothetico-deductive cycle: induction suggests a hypothesis and deduction derives testable consequences.

Memorise

  • Deduction: general to particular, associated with Aristotle and the syllogism. Induction: particular to general, associated with Francis Bacon.
  • Parsimony (Occam’s razor): among competing explanations, prefer the simplest that fits the facts.
  • Serendipity: a valuable discovery made by accident but recognised through a prepared mind (the term is from Horace Walpole; penicillin is the usual example).
  • Abduction (C. S. Peirce): inference to the best explanation.

Analyse / traps

Do not mix up the directions: if the statement moves from many observed cases to a rule, it is induction. A deductive argument can be valid yet false if a premise is false. Scientific attitude includes scepticism, not dogmatic belief.

1A.4Variables#

Concept

A variable is any characteristic that can take different values across persons, objects or occasions, such as age, income or teaching method. The independent variable (IV) is the presumed cause, manipulated or selected by the researcher; the dependent variable (DV) is the presumed effect that is measured. An extraneous variable is any other factor that might affect the DV and must be controlled; when it actually varies together with the IV and muddies the conclusion it is called a confounding variable. An intervening (mediating) variable lies between IV and DV and explains how the effect happens, but it is a hypothetical construct that is not directly observed, such as motivation between teaching method and achievement. A moderator variable changes the strength or direction of the IV–DV relationship, for example gender changing how strongly study hours affect marks. An attribute variable (age, sex, caste) is a characteristic that the researcher can only select, never assign.

Memorise

  • IV = cause/predictor/treatment; DV = effect/outcome/criterion.
  • Mediator explains how or why; moderator explains for whom or when.
  • Continuous variables take any value in a range (height); discrete take whole counts (number of children).
  • Control variables are held constant deliberately.

Analyse / traps

The classic confusion is moderator versus mediator versus confounder. Ask: is it in the causal chain (mediator), does it alter the strength of the relationship (moderator), or is it an uncontrolled outside factor spoiling the comparison (confounder)? Also, an attribute variable cannot be experimentally manipulated, which is why such studies are ex post facto.

1A.5Hypothesis, Theory and Operational Definitions#

Concept

A hypothesis is a tentative, testable statement about the relationship between variables, formed before data collection and expected to be confirmed or rejected by evidence. A good hypothesis is clear, specific, testable, consistent with existing knowledge, and expressed in simple terms. The null hypothesis (H0) states that there is no difference or no relationship; the alternative hypothesis (H1 or Ha) states that there is. A theory is a more developed body of interrelated concepts, definitions and propositions that explains and predicts a wide range of phenomena; it sits above a single hypothesis, and a well-supported theory is not a guess. A concept is an abstract idea, a construct is a concept deliberately built for research, and an operational definition states exactly how that construct will be measured, such as defining “intelligence” as the score on a named test. Operational definitions are the reason studies can be replicated.

Memorise

  • Hypothesis → tested; theory → explains; law → describes a regularity that holds without exception in its domain.
  • Operational definition: concept turned into measurable procedure (operationalism is linked to P. W. Bridgman).
  • Parsimony is a desirable quality of a theory; replicability needs clear operational definitions.
  • Hypotheses are never “proved”; we reject or fail to reject H0.

Analyse / traps

Watch the wording “accept the null hypothesis”; the correct statistical language is “fail to reject”. A hypothesis that cannot be tested is not a scientific hypothesis. The conceptual definition is dictionary-like; only the operational one tells you how to measure.

1A.6Reliability and Validity of Research Tools#

Concept

Reliability means consistency: a tool is reliable if it gives similar results under similar conditions. Validity means that the tool measures what it claims to measure. Reliability is checked by test–retest (stability over time), parallel or alternate forms (equivalence), split-half (internal consistency, usually corrected with the Spearman–Brown formula), Cronbach’s alpha or Kuder–Richardson (KR-20) for internal consistency, and inter-rater reliability when judges score responses. Validity has several types: content validity (the items fairly cover the subject; face validity is a surface judgement of this), criterion-related validity (agreement with an external standard, either concurrent or predictive), and construct validity (the tool measures the theoretical trait, shown by convergent and discriminant evidence).

Memorise

  • Reliability ≠ validity; reliability is necessary but not sufficient for validity.
  • A valid instrument is necessarily reliable; a reliable instrument may be invalid (a scale that is consistently 2 kg off).
  • Alpha of about 0.70 or above is usually treated as acceptable.
  • Predictive validity: tool today predicts future performance; concurrent: agrees with a present criterion.

Analyse / traps

If an option says a reliable test must be valid, it is wrong. The target analogy helps: tight shots away from the bullseye = reliable but not valid; shots on the bullseye = both.

1A.7Research Process, Design and Data#

Concept

The research process is a sequence: choosing a problem, reviewing literature, formulating objectives and hypotheses, preparing the research design, deciding the sample, collecting data, analysing and interpreting it, and finally writing the report. A pilot study is a small preliminary trial that tests the tools, procedure and feasibility before the main study. The research design is the overall plan or blueprint that specifies what data are needed, from whom, by what method and how they will be analysed. Kerlinger’s MAX-MIN-CON principle says a good design maximises systematic (experimental) variance, minimises error variance and controls extraneous variance. Data are primary when collected first-hand for the present purpose and secondary when collected earlier by someone else for another purpose.

Memorise

  • Order: problem → literature review → hypothesis → design → sampling → data collection → analysis → report.
  • Pilot study: tests tools and feasibility; not meant to prove hypotheses.
  • Primary data: questionnaire, interview, observation. Secondary data: census reports, journals, records.
  • Design = blueprint; methodology = reasoning behind the methods.

Analyse / traps

Questions on “first step” almost always point to identifying and defining the problem. A pilot study is not the same as a preliminary hypothesis test.
