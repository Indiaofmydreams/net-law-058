3D.1Purpose and Principles of Research Design#

Concept

The research design is the plan that connects the research questions to the data and the analysis; it specifies the type of study, the sample, the tools, the timing and the analytical procedures. Its purpose is to obtain valid answers economically and to control variance. Kothari groups designs as exploratory (flexible, to formulate a problem), descriptive and diagnostic (accurate description, little flexibility) and experimental (hypothesis testing). Fisher’s three principles of experimental design are replication, randomisation and local control, and Kerlinger’s MAX-MIN-CON principle guides variance control. A research proposal or synopsis sets out the design in advance: title, background, review, objectives, hypotheses, methodology, sampling, tools, analysis plan, schedule and references.

Memorise

  • Design = blueprint. Fisher: replication, randomisation, local control.
  • Exploratory = flexible; descriptive = structured; experimental = tests causal hypotheses.

Analyse / traps

Design is decided before data collection; it is not the same as the report structure.

3D.2Exploratory and Mixed-Methods Designs#

Concept

Exploratory designs are used to formulate a problem or hypotheses when knowledge is limited. Selltiz and colleagues named three common methods: a literature survey, an experience survey of knowledgeable people, and the analysis of insight-stimulating examples (case studies). Mixed-methods designs, as classified by Creswell, include the convergent parallel design (both strands collected together and merged), the explanatory sequential design (quantitative first, qualitative to explain), and the exploratory sequential design (qualitative first, then a quantitative test). Notation such as QUAN → qual shows the order and the priority of the strands.

Memorise

  • Exploratory methods: literature survey, experience survey, insight-stimulating cases.
  • Explanatory sequential = QUAN then qual; exploratory sequential = qual then QUAN.

Analyse / traps

In “explanatory” sequential, the qualitative strand explains numbers, which is the reverse of the exploratory sequence.

3D.3Quasi-experimental and Experimental Designs#

Concept

In quasi-experimental designs the researcher manipulates a treatment but cannot randomly assign participants, as in the non-equivalent control group design, the interrupted time-series design and the regression-discontinuity design. True experimental designs use random assignment. Fisher’s formal designs include the completely randomised design (units assigned wholly at random), the randomised block design (units grouped into homogeneous blocks and randomised within blocks), the Latin square (two blockings) and factorial designs (several factors studied together with their interactions). Notation: R for random assignment, X for the treatment and O for an observation.

Memorise

  • Quasi-experiment: treatment but no randomisation.
  • CRD, RBD, Latin square, factorial: formal experimental designs.

Analyse / traps

If groups were formed by existing classrooms rather than random assignment, the design is quasi-experimental.

3D.4Construct Validity and Types of Variables#

Concept

Construct validity asks whether the study’s measures and treatments really represent the theoretical constructs intended; it is threatened by vague definitions of the construct, relying on a single measure (mono-operation bias) or a single method (mono-method bias). Variables can be further classified: latent variables are not directly observable (intelligence, attitude) and are inferred from manifest or observed indicators such as test scores; dichotomous variables have two categories (pass/fail); dummy variables code categories as 0 and 1 for regression; composite variables combine several items into one score.

Memorise

  • Latent = unobserved construct; manifest = observed indicator.
  • Dummy variable: 0/1 coding of categories.

Analyse / traps

Construct validity concerns meaning of measures, not the size of the sample.
