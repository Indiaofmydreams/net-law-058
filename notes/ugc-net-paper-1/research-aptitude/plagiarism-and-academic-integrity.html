6B.1Meaning and Types of Plagiarism#

Concept

The word comes from the Latin plagiarius, a kidnapper. Plagiarism is presenting another person’s words, ideas, data or creative work as one’s own, without proper acknowledgement. The UGC regulations of 2018 describe it as taking someone else’s work or ideas and passing them off as one’s own. Forms include verbatim copying, mosaic plagiarism or patchwriting (stitching together phrases from sources with small changes), paraphrasing without credit, self-plagiarism (reusing one’s own published text without disclosure), translation plagiarism, idea plagiarism, plagiarism of data or images, secondary-source plagiarism (citing a source one has not read), and contract cheating or ghostwriting. It can be intentional or accidental, but carelessness is not a defence. Presenting a junior colleague’s results as one’s own is also plagiarism. Common knowledge need not be cited.

Memorise

  • Plagiarism = using others’ work without credit; applies to words, ideas, data, images.
  • Mosaic/patchwriting: stitched phrases with minor changes.
  • Self-plagiarism: reusing your own published text without disclosure.

Analyse / traps

Changing a few words in a copied sentence is still plagiarism; accidental plagiarism is still plagiarism.

6B.2Paraphrasing, Quotation and Citation Practice#

Concept

There are three legitimate ways to use a source: quote (exact words in quotation marks, with citation and page), paraphrase (restate the idea in your own words and sentence structure, still with citation) and summarise (condense the main points, with citation). Direct quotations should be rare and short; long ones are set as block quotes. Statistics, theories, findings, figures and unusual ideas must be cited, while widely known facts do not need citation. Keep notes that clearly separate your words from source words, cite as you write, and match every in-text citation to a reference-list entry. A paraphrase that keeps the original structure and merely swaps synonyms is inadequate.

Memorise

  • Quote: exact words + quotation marks + citation. Paraphrase: new wording + citation.
  • Even a perfect paraphrase needs a citation.

Analyse / traps

Removing quotation marks while keeping the wording is plagiarism even if a citation is given.

6B.3Copyright and Licences#

Concept

In India copyright is governed by the Copyright Act, 1957 (amended in 2012). It arises automatically when an original literary, dramatic, musical or artistic work, film or sound recording is created; registration is optional. It protects the expression of ideas, not the ideas themselves. For literary works the term is the author’s life plus sixty years. Under fair dealing (Section 52) limited use for private study, research, criticism, review, reporting and teaching is allowed, a narrower idea than the US doctrine of fair use. Authors also hold moral rights of paternity and integrity. Creative Commons licences (CC BY, BY-SA, BY-NC, BY-ND, CC0) state what reuse is allowed. Plagiarism is an ethical offence against academic norms, whereas infringement is a legal offence; one can plagiarise public-domain work, which is free from copyright, without breaking copyright law.

Memorise

  • Copyright Act, 1957; term: life + 60 years for literary works; arises without registration.
  • Fair dealing (Section 52): research, criticism, review, teaching.
  • CC BY requires attribution; CC0 waives rights.

Analyse / traps

Copyright protects expression, not ideas; plagiarism and copyright infringement are different wrongs.

6B.4UGC 2018 Plagiarism Regulations#

Concept

The UGC (Promotion of Academic Integrity and Prevention of Plagiarism in Higher Educational Institutions) Regulations, 2018 apply to students, researchers, faculty and staff. They require an undertaking of originality, use of plagiarism-detection software, and institutional bodies for academic integrity (a Departmental Academic Integrity Panel and an Institutional Academic Integrity Panel). They exclude from similarity counting quotations with attribution, references and bibliography, table of contents, preface and acknowledgements, and generic terms, laws, standard symbols and standards. Penalties depend on the similarity level. For students: Level 0 up to 10% (no penalty); Level 1 above 10% to 40% (a revised script must be submitted within six months); Level 2 above 40% to 60% (debarred from submitting a revised script for one year); Level 3 above 60% (registration for that programme cancelled). For faculty and staff the levels lead to withdrawal of the manuscript and, at higher levels, denial of increments and exclusion from supervision, and repeat offences attract severe action.

Level Similarity Student consequence
0 Up to 10% None
1 10–40% Revise within six months
2 40–60% Barred from submitting revised version for one year
3 Above 60% Registration cancelled

Memorise

  • Levels 0–3 at 10%, 40%, 60% thresholds.
  • Exclusions: quotes with attribution, references, preface, acknowledgements, common knowledge.
  • Bodies: DAIP and IAIP.

Analyse / traps

The thresholds define where each level begins; check whether the question says “above” or “up to”.

6B.5Academic Integrity Values and Misconduct#

Concept

The International Center for Academic Integrity names the fundamental values of academic integrity as honesty, trust, fairness, respect, responsibility and, added later, courage. Breaches include cheating (unauthorised aid), collusion (unauthorised collaboration), contract cheating (paying someone to produce work, as through essay mills), ghostwriting, impersonation, fabrication of data or citations, facilitating others’ dishonesty and multiple submission of the same work for credit more than once. Institutions respond with honour codes, education, clear policies and fair procedures.

Memorise

  • ICAI values: honesty, trust, fairness, respect, responsibility, courage.
  • Contract cheating = paying for work; collusion = improper collaboration.

Analyse / traps

Collaboration that the instructor allows is not collusion; unauthorised collaboration is.

6B.6Questionable Research and Publishing Practices#

Concept

Beyond plain plagiarism, several practices damage integrity. Image manipulation (splicing, duplicating or selectively adjusting blots and micrographs) distorts evidence. Padding the reference list adds irrelevant sources to look scholarly. Coercive citation is when editors or reviewers pressure authors to cite their journal or their own work to raise metrics, and citation cartels are groups citing each other to inflate counts; excessive self-citation has a similar effect. Paper mills are commercial services that sell fabricated papers or authorship. Peer-review rings use fake reviewers, and predatory journals accept papers for a fee without genuine review. Such journals typically promise very quick acceptance and make false claims about indexing.

Memorise

  • Coercive citation: demanded by editors or reviewers. Paper mill: sells papers or authorship.
  • Predatory journal: fee, no real review, quick acceptance, false claims.
  • Image manipulation is misconduct, not mere “enhancement”.

Analyse / traps

Quick acceptance guaranteed within days is a red flag, not a feature.

6B.7Authorship and Acknowledgement Issues#

Concept

Authorship disputes are best prevented by agreeing contributions and author order at the start of the project against the ICMJE criteria and institutional rules. When a dispute arises, the first step is a discussion among the authors using those criteria, then referral to the institution or the journal, whose editor may consult COPE guidance. People who contributed but do not meet authorship criteria (technical help, language editing, general supervision, funding access) are named in acknowledgements with their permission, and funding sources are stated. The CRediT roles are conceptualisation, data curation, formal analysis, funding acquisition, investigation, methodology, project administration, resources, software, supervision, validation, visualisation, writing the original draft, and writing (review and editing).

Memorise

  • First step in an authorship dispute: discuss within the team against agreed criteria.
  • Acknowledge non-author contributors and funders.
  • CRediT: 14 roles.

Analyse / traps

Honorary authorship damages credibility; acknowledgement is the correct recognition for minor help.

6B.8Consequences and Notable Cases#

Concept

Consequences of misconduct include retraction of papers, rejection or withdrawal of a thesis, cancellation of registration or degree, loss of funding and employment, debarment, reputational damage and sometimes legal action. Well-known cases include Hwang Woo-suk (fabricated stem-cell papers in Science, 2005), Diederik Stapel (fabricated data in many social-psychology papers, 2011), Jan Hendrik Schön (fabricated physics results at Bell Labs, 2002), Andrew Wakefield (the retracted 1998 Lancet paper linking MMR vaccine to autism), Haruko Obokata (retracted STAP cell papers, 2014) and the Piltdown Man hoax (exposed in 1953). Retraction Watch, founded in 2010, tracks retractions, and COPE gives retraction guidelines.

Memorise

  • Hwang: stem cells. Stapel: social psychology. Schön: physics. Wakefield: MMR and autism. Obokata: STAP.
  • Retraction Watch: database of retractions.

Analyse / traps

A retraction does not always mean fraud; it can follow honest error, but the reason must be stated.

6B.9Plagiarism Detection and AI-related Issues#

Concept

Detection combines software (see 5F) with human judgement: sudden shifts in style, anachronisms, inconsistent terminology and unusual fluency can signal copying. Software cannot catch everything, such as translated text, paraphrase and unavailable sources. Generative AI adds new problems: tools can invent references, reproduce source text and produce paraphrase that evades checkers, and “tortured phrases” from paraphrasing tools can reveal machine-spun text. Journals and COPE advise that AI cannot be an author, that its use must be disclosed, and that the human authors remain responsible for accuracy. AI-text detectors are unreliable and should not be used alone to accuse anyone.

Memorise

  • AI cannot be an author; disclose use; humans are accountable.
  • Check every AI-generated citation against a real source.

Analyse / traps

An AI-detection score is not proof of misconduct.
